Buying Japanese Ceramics: Regions, Shapes and Getting Them Home

Every region makes pottery and every region's pottery looks different. Knowing four or five of the styles turns a shelf of similar bowls into a choice worth making.

Why there are so many kinds

Japanese ceramics are organised by place. A style is named for the town or province that produces it, the local clay and the local kiln tradition determine how it looks, and the differences are large enough that an experienced eye identifies the origin instantly.

This is not a marketing construct. It comes from centuries of separate development in domains that controlled their own kilns, in a country where the material available in one valley is not the material available in the next.

For a buyer it means the useful question is not 'is this good pottery' but 'which tradition is this, and do I like that tradition'. Once a few of them are recognisable, shopping stops being guesswork.

The ones worth recognising

Hagi, from Yamaguchi, is pale, soft-looking and covered in a crackled glaze that absorbs tea over years and changes colour as it does. Collectors talk about a Hagi cup being finished by use rather than by the potter.

Bizen, from Okayama, is unglazed. The surface comes entirely from wood ash and flame in the kiln, producing russet and dark brown markings that cannot be planned precisely. It is heavy, earthy and unmistakable.

Arita and Imari, from Kyushu, are the opposite: fine white porcelain with detailed painting in cobalt blue, or in enamel colours. This is the ware that was exported to Europe in enormous quantities from the seventeenth century and shaped what Europeans thought Asian porcelain looked like.

Mashiko, north of Tokyo, is thick, sturdy folk pottery with generous glazes, closely associated with the mingei folk-craft movement. Karatsu is restrained and understated and much loved for tea. Kutani is boldly coloured with green, yellow and red overglaze.

Those six cover most of what a visitor will meet.

Shapes that are worth owning

The rice bowl and the small side dish are the everyday pieces and the easiest to use outside Japan. Buy more of the small dishes than you think; Japanese meals use several per person and they are the most versatile things you will bring home.

The yunomi, a tall handleless tea cup, and the guinomi, a small sake cup, are the pieces where individual potters do their most interesting work and where the price range is widest.

A donburi bowl, larger and deeper, is genuinely useful. A tokkuri flask for sake pairs with the cups. A kyusu teapot with a side handle is the traditional shape for green tea, and the ones with a built-in mesh strainer are the practical choice.

Chopstick rests are the cheapest thing in any pottery shop and make excellent small gifts.

Where to buy

Department store craft floors carry a curated selection from across the country with knowledgeable staff, and they will wrap for travel properly. Prices are higher and the quality is dependable.

Pottery towns are the enjoyable option: buying from the person who made the object, in the town where it was made, usually below city prices. Mashiko is the accessible one from Tokyo; Arita, Bizen, Hagi and Karatsu all reward a detour if the trip is heading that way.

Pottery fairs are the best of all. Most kiln towns hold one or two a year, typically in spring and autumn, when hundreds of potters set up along the streets. Prices drop and the selection is enormous.

In cities, look for craft shops in the older districts rather than souvenir shops near stations. Kyoto and Kanazawa both have several streets of them.

Judging a piece without expertise

Pick it up. Weight and balance in the hand are most of what separates a good piece from a mediocre one, and they cannot be judged from a photograph.

Look at the foot ring, the unglazed circle on the base. That is where the potter's skill and the character of the clay show most plainly, and it is what a Japanese buyer turns the bowl over to inspect.

Irregularity is not a fault. Asymmetry, a run in the glaze, a mark where the piece rested in the kiln — these are usually deliberate or accepted, and in several traditions they are the point.

Ask whether a piece is handmade or slip-cast, and whether it is dishwasher and microwave safe. Unglazed Bizen and porous Hagi need hand washing and, in some cases, soaking before first use.

Getting it home intact

Have the shop pack it. Japanese shops wrap ceramics extremely well as a matter of course, often in a wooden box for anything expensive, and the box is part of the object's value.

Carry the pieces you care about in hand luggage. Checked baggage handling is not gentle and a wrapped bowl in a suitcase is a risk taken for no reason.

For a larger purchase, shipping is straightforward and shops arrange it routinely. Ask about insurance.

And buy the tax-free exemption in mind if the total is large: ceramics are general goods rather than consumables, so the rules are simpler for them than for food or cosmetics.

A note on price

There is no reliable relationship between price and how much you will enjoy using something. A thousand-yen rice bowl from a pottery town used every day will give more pleasure than an expensive display piece.

The genuinely expensive end — work by named artists, tea ceremony pieces with provenance — is a collectors' market with its own logic, and it is not where a visitor should start.

Buy what you will put food in.
