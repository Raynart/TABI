How to Spend 10 Days in Japan Without a Single Tourist Trap

Most first-time visitors follow the same Golden Route — Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka — and come home feeling like they saw a theme park. This itinerary takes a different approach: slower, deeper, and built around the neighborhoods that locals actually use.

The problem with the Golden Route

Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka in ten days is the default because it works: the Shinkansen connects all three, every hotel speaks some English, and you will see genuinely famous things. The failure mode is subtler than 'too many tourists'. It is that you spend the trip in the thin commercial layer that has grown up around the famous things, and never touch the ordinary country underneath.

The fix is not to avoid the big cities. It is to change what you do inside them and to give a couple of days to somewhere that is not on the list.

Days 1-3: Tokyo, in one or two neighbourhoods

Resist the urge to tick off districts. Pick a base in a residential-but-connected area — Yanaka, Kagurazaka, Koenji or Kiyosumi rather than Shinjuku — and treat it as a home rather than a hotel.

Spend a full day walking one area properly: the shopping street, the coin laundry, the standing bar, the shrine at the end of the road. Then use the trains for one or two set pieces you actually care about, not five you feel obliged to see. Three days spent this way leaves a stronger impression than a week of highlights.

Jet lag is on your side for the first two mornings. Being awake at five in Tokyo is a gift: the fish market, the temples and the parks are all better before seven, and you will never have that advantage again on the trip.

Day 4: a day trip instead of a transfer

Before leaving Tokyo, spend one day outside it. Kamakura is the obvious choice and deserves it — coastal, full of temples, walkable. Less obvious options include Chichibu in the mountains north-west, Kawagoe for its surviving merchant streetscape, or Sawara for a canal town almost nobody visits.

The point is to see what a smaller Japanese town looks like before your sense of the country is set by two metropolises. It also breaks up the city time without costing you a hotel change.

Days 5-7: Kyoto, mostly on foot and mostly early

Kyoto punishes late starts more than any other Japanese city. The famous sites are close to empty before eight and unpleasant by eleven, so invert the day: temples at dawn, a long lunch, neighbourhoods and river in the afternoon, dinner somewhere with no English menu.

Give one full day to the north — Kurama, Kibune, Ohara — which is where Kyoto stops feeling like a museum. Base yourself away from the station, around Nishijin or Demachiyanagi if you can, and rent a bicycle; Kyoto is flat, compact and far better on two wheels than on its buses, which are slow and full.

Two temples done properly beat six done quickly. The gardens are designed to be sat in, and the difference between twenty minutes and an hour in one of them is the whole point of coming.

Days 8-9: the deviation that makes the trip

This is the part most itineraries skip. Take two days somewhere that is not on the Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka axis. Kanazawa for craft, a garden and a genuinely great market. Matsumoto for the castle and the mountains behind it. Naoshima for contemporary art on a small island. Takayama and the Hida valley for old timber townscape.

Any of these is reachable in half a day and none of them will feel like the previous week. If you only change one thing about a standard itinerary, change this.

Two nights is the minimum that makes it worthwhile. One night in a place like this is mostly travel, and the reason to go is the evening — when the day visitors have left and the town belongs to the people who live in it.

Day 10: Osaka, or don't

Osaka is worth a day for eating and for the fact that it is culturally nothing like Kyoto despite being half an hour away. But it is also fine to skip.

If your flight leaves from Kansai, spend the last day there and eat well. If it leaves from Tokyo, use the last day to go back to whichever neighbourhood you liked most and do nothing in particular. A repeat visit to one place you already understand is a better ending than a new city you will see for six hours.

The practical spine

Two things make this shape of trip work. First, fewer hotel changes: three bases over ten days, not six. Packing and checking in eats more of a trip than people expect, and every move costs you most of a day.

Second, a hard limit of two scheduled items per day, with everything else left open. The parts of a Japan trip people describe years later are almost never the scheduled ones — they are the wrong turn, the shop that was still open, the train window between two places you meant to be.

Send your luggage ahead rather than dragging it. The overnight courier service moves a suitcase between hotels for a couple of thousand yen; hand it to the front desk before noon and collect it the next afternoon. Crossing Japan with a day bag instead of a large case changes what a travel day feels like.

Does the JR Pass make sense for this?

Usually not, and this is worth working out rather than assuming. This itinerary involves Tokyo to Kyoto, a side trip, and a return or an exit from Kansai — which is fewer long-distance legs than the pass needs to break even at current prices.

Price your actual legs against the pass before buying. If the deviation on days 8-9 is Kanazawa or Takayama and you are returning to Tokyo, it may tip. If you are flying out of Kansai, it almost certainly does not, and a regional pass plus individual tickets will cost less.

For everything inside the cities, an IC card covers trains, buses and convenience stores and removes the question entirely.

Booking order and rough budget

Book in this order: flights, then the two nights on days 8-9 if a ryokan is involved, then Tokyo and Kyoto accommodation, then nothing else. Restaurants worth reserving can be handled a week or two out, and the rest should stay open.

As a rough guide outside peak season, a mid-range version of this trip runs somewhere around ¥15,000–25,000 per person per day all in — accommodation, food, local transport and entry fees — with the long-distance train legs on top. Business hotels and ordinary restaurants sit at the lower end; a ryokan night and a good dinner will double a day on their own.

The single biggest saving available is eating like a resident: a set lunch at a good restaurant costs a third of the same kitchen's dinner, and it is one of the best-value meals in the developed world.
