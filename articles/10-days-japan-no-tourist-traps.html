How to Spend 10 Days in Japan Without a Single Tourist Trap

Most first-time visitors follow the same Golden Route — Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka — and come home feeling like they saw a theme park. This itinerary takes a different approach: slower, deeper, and built around the neighborhoods that locals actually use.

The problem with the Golden Route

Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka in ten days is the default because it works: the Shinkansen connects all three, every hotel speaks some English, and you will see genuinely famous things. The failure mode is subtler than 'too many tourists'. It is that you spend the trip in the thin commercial layer that has grown up around the famous things, and never touch the ordinary country underneath. The fix is not to avoid the big cities. It is to change what you do inside them and to give a couple of days to somewhere that isn't on the list.

Days 1–3: Tokyo, in one or two neighbourhoods

Resist the urge to tick off districts. Pick a base in a residential-but-connected area — Yanaka, Kagurazaka, Koenji or Kiyosumi rather than Shinjuku — and treat it as a home rather than a hotel. Spend a full day walking one area properly: the shopping street, the coin laundry, the standing bar, the shrine at the end of the road. Then use the trains for one or two set pieces you actually care about, not five you feel obliged to see. Three days spent this way leaves a stronger impression than a week of highlights.

Day 4: a day trip instead of a transfer

Before leaving Tokyo, spend one day outside it. Kamakura is the obvious choice and deserves it — coastal, full of temples, walkable. Less obvious options include Chichibu in the mountains north-west, Kawagoe for its surviving merchant streetscape, or Enoshima for a slightly scruffy seaside afternoon. The point is to see what a smaller Japanese town looks like before your sense of the country is set by two metropolises.

Days 5–7: Kyoto, mostly on foot and mostly early

Kyoto punishes late starts more than any other Japanese city. The famous sites are close to empty before eight and unpleasant by eleven, so invert the day: temples at dawn, a long lunch, neighbourhoods and river in the afternoon, dinner somewhere with no English menu. Give one full day to the north — Kurama, Kibune, Ohara — which is where Kyoto stops feeling like a museum. Base yourself away from the station and around Nishijin or Demachiyanagi if you can.

Days 8–9: the deviation that makes the trip

This is the part most itineraries skip. Take two days somewhere that isn't on the Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka axis. Kanazawa for craft and a genuinely great market. Matsumoto for the castle and the mountains behind it. Naoshima for contemporary art on a small island. Takayama and the Hida valley for old timber townscape. Any of these is reachable in half a day and none of them will feel like the previous week. If you only change one thing about a standard itinerary, change this.

Day 10: Osaka, or don't

Osaka is worth a day for eating and for the fact that it is culturally nothing like Kyoto despite being half an hour away. But it is also fine to skip. If your flight leaves from Kansai, spend the last day there and eat well in Shinsekai or the streets around Tsuruhashi. If it leaves from Tokyo, use the last day to go back to whichever neighbourhood you liked most and do nothing in particular. A repeat visit to one place you already understand is a better ending than a new city you'll see for six hours.

The practical spine

Two things make this shape of trip work. First, fewer hotel changes: three bases over ten days, not six. Packing and checking in eats more of a trip than people expect. Second, a hard limit of two scheduled items per day, with everything else left open. The parts of a Japan trip people describe years later are almost never the scheduled ones — they are the wrong turn, the shop that was still open, the train window between two places you meant to be.
