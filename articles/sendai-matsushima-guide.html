Sendai and Matsushima: The Easiest Way Into Tohoku

Ninety minutes north of Tokyo by Shinkansen and almost nobody goes. A green city built around a warlord's legacy, and a bay of pine-covered islands that has been considered one of Japan's three great views for four hundred years.

Ninety minutes, and a different country

Sendai is about ninety minutes from Tokyo on the Tohoku Shinkansen — less time than many people spend commuting — and it is the gateway to a part of Japan that international itineraries almost entirely ignore. Tohoku is the northern third of Honshu: colder, emptier, more agricultural, with a stronger dialect and a slower pace.

Sendai itself is a city of about a million people, comfortably laid out, with wide tree-lined avenues that have earned it the name City of Trees. It was flattened by bombing in 1945 and rebuilt on a generous plan, which is why it feels more spacious than most Japanese cities of its size.

It is not a city of monuments. It is a good base, a good place to eat, and the point from which the rest of the region opens up.

Date Masamune, who is everywhere

The city's identity is bound up with Date Masamune, the one-eyed warlord who founded it in 1600 and whose crescent-moon helmet crest appears on everything from manhole covers to beer cans.

Aoba Castle, on the hill above the centre, is mostly foundations — the buildings are long gone — but the site has his equestrian statue and a view over the city that explains why he built there. Zuihoden, his mausoleum, is the more rewarding visit: a small, intensely decorated building in the Momoyama style, brightly painted and carved, set among cedars on a hillside. It is a reconstruction, the original having burned in the war, and it is beautifully done.

The Osaki Hachimangu shrine, lacquered black and gold, is a genuine survivor from his period and a national treasure.

Matsushima Bay

Matsushima is a bay scattered with a couple of hundred small pine-covered islands, traditionally counted as one of the three great views of Japan alongside Miyajima and Amanohashidate. It is about forty minutes from Sendai by local train.

The classic approach is a sightseeing boat around the islands, which takes under an hour and is the only way to see the arrangement properly. From land, the four designated viewpoints on the surrounding hills each give a different composition, and Saigyo Modoshi no Matsu park is the one worth the climb.

Zuiganji, the temple at the centre of the town, is a Zen monastery rebuilt by Date Masamune with rooms of painted screens and a cedar approach. Godaido, a tiny hall on a rocky islet reached by a short red bridge, is the image on the postcards.

The bay's islands are credited with absorbing much of the force of the 2011 tsunami; the town was damaged but far less catastrophically than the coast either side of it.

Eating in Sendai

Gyutan — grilled beef tongue — is the city's dish, and it is not a novelty. It was created here after the war by a chef using an ingredient the occupying forces discarded, and it is now a proper regional cuisine with dedicated restaurants. Thick-cut, salted, grilled over charcoal and served with barley rice and oxtail soup, it is far better than a description of it suggests.

Zunda is the other speciality: young soybeans mashed into a sweet green paste, used on mochi and in shakes. It tastes like nothing else and is the standard souvenir.

Sasa kamaboko, fish cake pressed into a bamboo-leaf shape and grilled, is the third. And the oysters from Matsushima Bay in winter are among the best in the country.

The coast, and what happened in 2011

The Sanriku coast north and east of Sendai was devastated by the tsunami of March 2011. Fifteen years on, the rebuilding is largely complete and the landscape is visibly different: enormous seawalls, raised ground, new towns built on higher land.

There are memorial sites along the coast, including preserved ruined buildings and museums documenting the disaster. They are not tourist attractions in the ordinary sense and the towns are living places rather than exhibits, which is worth remembering in how you go.

The Sanriku Railway, rebuilt after being largely destroyed, runs along the coast and is a straightforward way to see the region. Visiting and spending money in these towns is welcomed; treating them as a spectacle is not.

What Sendai opens up

Yamadera, a temple complex of a thousand stone steps climbing a wooded cliff face, is about an hour west and is one of the most striking places in northern Japan. The poet Basho stopped here and wrote about the cicadas and the silence.

Naruko Onsen, a hot spring town famous for its lacquered wooden kokeshi dolls, is a little over an hour away. Zao, on the border with Yamagata, has the snow monsters — trees frozen into grotesque shapes by wind-driven ice — in deep winter.

And the Tohoku Shinkansen continues north to Morioka and Aomori, so Sendai works as the first stop of a longer run up the spine of the region rather than an end point.

When to go

Summer for the Tanabata festival in early August, which is the largest in Japan and fills the shopping arcades with enormous paper streamers. Winter for the oysters, the snow and the illuminations. Autumn for the colour, which arrives earlier here than further south.

Tohoku's cherry blossom comes weeks after Tokyo's, which is the most useful fact about the region for anyone whose dates missed the season further south.
