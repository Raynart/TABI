What to Buy When You Buy Japanese Sake

Sake is rice wine in the same way that champagne is grape juice. The category is more complex than most people expect, and the range of quality is enormous.

The two main variables

Sake is fermented rice, water, yeast, and koji mold. The main variables: how much the rice has been polished before brewing (the seimaibuai, expressed as a percentage of the grain remaining), and whether distilled alcohol has been added. These two factors create the main classification categories that appear on labels and that are worth understanding before you buy anything.

Polishing matters because the outside of a rice grain holds the proteins and fats, and the centre is nearly pure starch. Milling away the outer layers gives a cleaner, more aromatic, more fragile sake — and throws away most of the grain, which is why a bottle polished to 40% costs what it does. It is not a quality score so much as a style dial: heavily polished sake is delicate and floral, lightly polished sake is savoury and full.

The added alcohol is misunderstood. A small measured addition late in the brew is a traditional technique that pulls out aroma compounds and lightens the body; it is not padding. Cheap bulk sake does use it to stretch volume, which is where the suspicion comes from, but a good honjozo is a deliberate style and often the better match for hot food.

Classifications that matter

Junmai: pure rice sake, no added alcohol. Honjozo: a small amount of distilled alcohol added, which lightens the sake and adds aroma. Ginjo: rice polished to at least 60% remaining, more aromatic. Daiginjo: polished to at least 50%, typically the most delicate and expensive. Combine these: Junmai Daiginjo is pure rice, top-tier polish. Futsushu is table sake — no premium designation, reasonable for everyday drinking.

Those eight combinations are the tokutei meishoshu, the special designations, and roughly a third of all sake made qualifies for one. Everything else is futsushu. A bottle carrying any of the eight names on the label has met a legal standard; a bottle covered in adjectives and carrying none of them has not.

Two further words appear constantly and change what you are buying more than the grade does. Namazake is unpasteurised — fresher, livelier, and requiring refrigeration from the brewery to your glass. Genshu is undiluted, arriving at 18–20% alcohol instead of the usual 15%, which is a different drink entirely and worth knowing before you pour a full cup of it.

Reading the rest of the label

Two numbers turn up on back labels and they are the closest thing to a taste description you will get. The nihonshudo, or sake meter value, runs from roughly -5 to +15: negative is sweeter, positive is drier, zero is neutral. The san-do, or acidity, sits around 1.0 to 2.0, and higher means more structure and a sharper finish.

They interact, which is why neither works alone. A +10 with high acidity is bracingly dry; a +10 with low acidity just tastes clean. If you liked something, photograph the back label and match the pair of numbers next time rather than the brewery name.

The rice variety is sometimes given too. Yamada Nishiki is the prestige grain, grown for brewing and prized for how well it mills; if a shop is pointing at it, they are pointing at a serious bottle.

What to actually buy

For a gift or something to drink on the trip: a Junmai Ginjo or Junmai Daiginjo from a mid-sized regional brewery priced between ¥2,000 and ¥5,000 for a 720ml bottle. The big names — Dassai, Kubota, Hakkaisan — are reliably good and widely available in department stores and specialist shops; they became famous because they're excellent. Dassai's numbers refer to the polish: Dassai 23 is milled to 23% remaining and priced accordingly, while Dassai 39 is similar in character for considerably less.

The more interesting purchase is a brewery you have never heard of from the region you are standing in. There are well over a thousand breweries in Japan, most of them small, and a great many never distribute beyond their own prefecture. A ¥2,500 junmai ginjo from a local kura is the bottle you cannot buy at home, and the shop staff will steer you if you say where you are from and what you like.

If you want to taste before committing, the sake tasting rooms attached to some stations and airports are the efficient route. The Ponshukan at Echigo-Yuzawa is the famous one — a wall of machines pouring small measures from around a hundred local breweries for a token each.

Nigori, sparkling, and aged

Nigori sake — unfiltered, cloudy, with rice sediment left in — is best drunk cold and fresh and doesn't travel as well. If you're drinking in Japan, try it; if you're buying to bring home, stick to clear filtered sake in sealed bottles.

Sparkling sake has grown into a real category rather than a novelty, usually around 8% alcohol, faintly sweet, and the single easiest bottle to hand someone who says they do not like sake. Most of it is meant to be drunk young and cold.

Koshu is aged sake, held for years until it turns amber and develops something closer to sherry or dried fruit. It divides people sharply and is worth a small glass somewhere before you buy a bottle of it.

Temperature, and the glass

Serving temperature is not a detail. Ginjo and daiginjo are aromatic and fragile, and heating them destroys precisely what you paid for — these are chilled drinks. Junmai and honjozo, which are fuller and more savoury, are the ones that reward warming, and warm sake is a genuinely different and underrated pleasure.

The vocabulary is worth a line: reishu is chilled, hiya is room temperature, nurukan is around 40°C, atsukan is hot. In an izakaya, ordering junmai nurukan in winter marks you out as someone who has thought about it.

Drink aromatic sake from a wine glass rather than the small ochoko cup. The cup is for pouring for other people, which is the actual social function; the wide bowl is what lets you smell a daiginjo. Serious sake bars will hand you a wine glass without being asked.

Where to buy

Department store basement floors have the best curated range and staff who can explain what they're selling. Specialist sake shops exist in most cities — Hasegawa Saketen has branches in and around Tokyo Station with English-speaking staff and a standing bar attached, which makes it the easiest single stop for a visitor.

Avoid convenience store sake for gifts; it's functional but unmemorable. The one exception worth making is cup sake — the small glass tumblers with foil lids, sold everywhere for a few hundred yen. They are the correct thing to drink on a long train journey and the printed cups make oddly good small souvenirs.

Look for a shop with a refrigerated section and stock in it. A sakaya that keeps its namazake and its ginjo cold is telling you it takes the stock seriously; a shop with premium bottles standing in a warm window in August is telling you the opposite.

Getting it home

Premium sake is best kept refrigerated and consumed within a few months of purchase. Nigori and namazake degrade fastest. Honjozo and futsushu are more forgiving. Buy what you can drink.

Practical transport notes: bottles cannot go in hand luggage, so anything you buy in the city must be packed and checked. The 720ml standard bottle is glass and heavy — two of them plus packaging is around three kilos. Wrap them in clothing in the middle of the case, or buy the padded bottle sleeves sold at the airport and most department stores.

Duty-free shops after security are the exception, since those bottles are sealed and can be carried on. Check your own country's allowance before you buy four bottles; the limits are lower than most people assume, and sake is taxed as a wine-strength product in most places.
