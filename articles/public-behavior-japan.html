How to Behave in Public in Japan: The Unwritten Rules

Japanese public spaces operate on a set of unspoken rules that make them exceptionally pleasant. Most visitors don’t know them. All of them are learnable in ten minutes.

Don't eat while walking

Eating while walking is generally not done. The exception is festival food from stalls at a matsuri, where the context is explicit and everyone is doing it. Otherwise you finish your convenience store onigiri standing outside the shop, not while walking down the street.

Street food vendors at tourist sites have started providing small standing areas specifically because this norm exists and visitors do not know it. Use them — they are there for you.

The same applies to drinking, though less strictly: a closed bottle carried and sipped is unremarkable, an open cup of coffee while walking is mildly conspicuous.

Queuing is not optional

Japan queues. At ATMs, at crossings, at train doors, at ramen shops, at lifts, at bus stops. Cutting in is an extremely unwelcome behaviour.

If there is no visible queue but a crowd, look for the end of it and join there. Lines here are orderly and self-policing — nobody will say anything if you push in, but everybody will notice, and the social discomfort this produces is immediate and real.

Queues also form in places you might not expect one: at the platform door markings, at the escalator, at the single working ticket machine. When in doubt, assume the loose cluster of people is a line.

Noise levels

Japan is quieter than most countries in public. Not silent — Shinjuku at six in the evening is genuinely loud — but the background noise comes from the environment rather than from individual conversations.

Speaking at a volume that carries beyond your table, laughing in a way that commands the room, or holding a speakerphone conversation in a shop are all behaviours that will cause people to move away from you. That movement is the feedback system; nobody will say anything.

The single most common complaint from Japanese people about foreign visitors is volume, and it is almost always unintentional. A group of four talking normally in a language nobody around them speaks is louder in effect than the same group at home.

Escalators, lifts and pavements

On escalators the standing side is the left and the right is for walking, in Tokyo. Osaka reverses it — stand right, walk left — and the boundary between the two conventions falls somewhere around Kyoto, producing visible confusion. When in doubt, follow the person in front.

Worth knowing: rail operators have spent years asking people to stand on both sides for safety reasons, and the campaign has largely failed. Follow the crowd rather than the poster.

On pavements and in corridors, keep left in most of the country. In lifts, the person nearest the buttons operates them for everyone and holds the door, which is a small job you may find yourself doing.

There are no rubbish bins

Public bins were largely removed after the 1995 sarin attack on the Tokyo subway and never came back. In most streets, parks and platforms there is nowhere to put anything.

You carry your rubbish with you until you reach a convenience store, which has bins by the entrance or the till, or your accommodation. Vending machines have a bin beside them for that machine's own containers. Stations have some, usually past the ticket gates.

Carry a small bag for it. This is less inconvenient than it sounds once you accept that the rubbish is coming with you, and it is a large part of why Japanese streets look the way they do.

Smoking, which works backwards

The rule is close to the inverse of what most visitors expect. Smoking on the street is banned in many central wards and enforced with fines, and there are designated smoking areas — glass boxes, marked corners — where smokers congregate.

Indoors, smoking survives in a way it does not in most of Europe. Rules have tightened considerably, but small bars and some restaurants can still permit it, and a smoking room or a smoking floor in a hotel is normal.

If you smoke, use the designated areas and carry a portable ashtray, which is sold everywhere. If you do not, be aware that a bar you walk into may be smoky and that this is legal.

Small body things that read differently

Blowing your nose in public is considered unpleasant; the convention is to sniff, or to step away and do it discreetly. This genuinely surprises people from countries where the reverse is true.

Pointing at people is impolite — the gesture is an open hand. Beckoning is done palm down with the fingers waving towards yourself, which to Western eyes looks like a shooing motion. Counting on fingers starts with the thumb.

Wearing a mask when you have a cold is normal, unremarkable and appreciated, and has been long before recent years. And physical contact between strangers is minimal: no back-slapping, no hugging, no hand on the shoulder.

Umbrellas, and drinking outdoors

It rains, and there is an entire small infrastructure for it. Shops and museums have umbrella stands at the door, some with locks and keys, and you leave yours there rather than dripping through the building. Many have plastic sleeve dispensers instead. Take the umbrella when you leave; cheap ones do get taken by mistake, which is why you can buy a new one for a few hundred yen at any convenience store.

Drinking alcohol in public is legal and unremarkable in Japan, which surprises visitors from countries where it is not. A beer in a park during hanami, on a bench, or on a long-distance train is entirely normal.

What is not tolerated is being loudly drunk in a way that affects other people. The line is not the drinking; it is the volume and the mess.

Photography of strangers

The norms are broadly those of most countries, with one specific and well-documented problem: the geisha districts of Kyoto, where years of visitors photographing maiko and geiko at close range has led to restrictions, closed private lanes and fines.

Photographing someone in traditional dress without asking, following them for a shot, or blocking a path for a photograph are behaviours that have prompted legal responses in some areas. This is not a general hostility to cameras; it is a response to specific, repeated conduct.

Everywhere else: use judgement, ask when in doubt, and take no for an answer. 'Shashin, ii desu ka' is enough, and the answer is yes more often than not.
