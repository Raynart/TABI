How to Order and Drink Sake in Japan Without Getting It Wrong

Sake is Japan's most misunderstood drink. Most people drink it too warm, in the wrong glass, with the wrong food. This is how to do it correctly.

Sake is not always drunk hot

The most persistent misunderstanding about sake outside Japan is that it is drunk hot. It is drunk across a range of temperatures — cold, room temperature, gently warmed, hot — and which one depends on the sake and on preference.

Premium ginjo and daiginjo are almost always served cold or at room temperature, because heat destroys the delicate aromatics that justify the price. Warming suits fuller, more robust honjozo and everyday futsushu, and warm sake is a genuinely underrated pleasure that gets dismissed because so much bad sake has been served hot to disguise it.

The temperature words are worth having: reishu is chilled, hiya is room temperature, nurukan is around body-warm, atsukan is hot. Asking which is recommended for a specific bottle is a reasonable question and will get a thoughtful answer.

You pour for others, not yourself

The central social convention is that you pour for others and they pour for you. You do not fill your own cup. If someone's cup is empty and the flask is nearest you, pour. If yours is empty, lift it slightly towards whoever has the flask — that is the signal, and it is silent.

Pour with two hands: one on the flask, one supporting it underneath. One-handed pouring is casual and reads as inattentive in any semi-formal setting.

At a very informal dinner among close friends the rule relaxes and people top themselves up. Anywhere with colleagues, hosts or people you have just met, it applies. Pouring for yourself while others sit with empty cups is the version that lands badly.

The receiving gesture

When someone pours for you, do not leave the cup on the table. Lift it, hold it with both hands or support it from below with one, and hold it steady while they pour. This is the same gesture as receiving anything with both hands: it says you are receiving attentively.

Take at least a sip before setting it down, then pour in return. The exchange is the point rather than the drink.

If you have had enough, leave your cup full. A full cup is the polite signal that you are done, and it prevents the loop from continuing. Covering the cup with your hand or turning it over works too, but the full cup is the gentlest version.

The vessels

Sake arrives in a tokkuri, the small ceramic flask, and is drunk from an ochoko, the small cup. Both are sized so that pouring for each other happens constantly, which is the entire design intention.

A masu is the square wooden box, originally a rice measure, and it appears in two ways. Sometimes you drink from it directly, in which case you drink from a corner rather than a flat edge. More often it holds a glass, and the server deliberately overflows the glass so that the masu fills too — this is mokkiri, and the overflow is generosity, not clumsiness. Drink the glass down before lifting it, then tip the box into the glass.

At a serious sake bar you may be given a wine glass instead, which is not an affectation: a wide bowl is the only way to smell an aromatic daiginjo properly.

Ordering

At an izakaya the standard entry is a carafe rather than a bottle. They come in one go, about 180ml, and two go, about 360ml. The small one is the right first order while you work out what you like.

At a specialist, the menu will list classifications — junmai, honjozo, ginjo, daiginjo — often with a region and sometimes a sweetness and acidity figure. The useful question is 'osusume wa nan desu ka', what do you recommend, followed by whether you want something light or rich, dry or fruity. Staff at a sake-focused place enjoy this question.

Tasting sets of three small glasses are common and are the efficient way to find out what you like. Order one before committing to a flask.

Kanpai, and the pace of the evening

The toast is kanpai. Make eye contact, touch cups gently — ceramic chips — and drink together. Wait for it before drinking, even if your glass arrived first, and if you are the most junior person present, let your cup sit slightly lower than the others when they meet.

Japanese drinking is paced around food and conversation rather than around finishing what is in front of you, and the first drink at an izakaya is usually beer with the second round moving to sake. Nomihodai — all-you-can-drink for a fixed time — is common and is not an invitation to race.

Declining a drink is entirely acceptable. 'Kekkou desu' or simply covering the cup works. Nobody will push, and the days when refusing was socially difficult are largely gone.

What to drink it with

Sake is built for food and shows badly on its own. It has less acidity than wine and a great deal of umami, which is why it sits well beside grilled fish, tofu, pickles and anything salty, and why it does not fight delicate flavours the way a tannic wine does.

The one pairing debate worth knowing: sake with sushi is a little contentious among purists, because both are rice, and many sushi chefs prefer you drink tea or beer with the meal and sake before or after. This is a preference rather than a rule and nobody will stop you.

Alongside it, a glass of water — yawaragi-mizu, softening water — is the single best habit to adopt. Drinking it between cups is standard practice at any serious sake bar and is the difference between a good evening and a bad morning.
