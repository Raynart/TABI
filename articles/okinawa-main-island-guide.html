Okinawa: A Different Country That Happens to Be in Japan

Subtropical, formerly an independent kingdom, and shaped by the worst battle of the Pacific war and eighty years of American bases since. Okinawa is the part of Japan that is least like the rest of it.

It was not Japan, and it still does not feel like it

For roughly four and a half centuries the Ryukyu Islands were an independent kingdom, trading between Japan, China and south-east Asia and taking influence from all of them. It was annexed by Japan in 1879, the language suppressed and the culture discouraged.

What survives is genuinely distinct: a different musical tradition built around the sanshin, a three-stringed instrument covered in snakeskin; different architecture with red tiles and stone lion figures on the roofs; different food; different textiles; and a set of Ryukyuan languages, related to Japanese but not mutually intelligible, now endangered.

The climate is subtropical rather than temperate. Winter is mild enough for a jacket rather than a coat, summer is hot and humid with a serious typhoon season, and the sea is a colour that people who have only seen Honshu do not associate with Japan.

Shuri Castle, and watching it being rebuilt

Shuri Castle above Naha was the seat of the Ryukyuan kings. It was destroyed in the 1945 battle, reconstructed over decades, and then burned down again in October 2019 — a fire that was watched live on television across Japan and felt as a genuine loss.

It is being rebuilt again, and the reconstruction has been deliberately made visible: rather than hiding the work behind hoardings, the site has been arranged so that visitors can watch the carpenters and lacquer workers from walkways and through glass panels. The exterior of the main hall was completed during 2025, and the hall is scheduled to reopen to the public in late 2026, with the full project running into 2027.

This means the thing you see depends on exactly when you go, and it is worth checking the current state before visiting. It also means that right now you can watch traditional timber and lacquer techniques being practised at full scale, which will not be possible again.

The surrounding gates, walls and grounds are open, and the Shureimon gate is intact.

The battle, and the bases

The Battle of Okinawa in 1945 lasted almost three months and killed something in the region of a quarter of the island's civilian population. Almost nothing standing survived it. This is the fact underneath everything else on the island.

The Peace Memorial Park at the southern tip, where the fighting ended, has a museum and the Cornerstone of Peace: a set of stone walls carved with the names of everyone who died in the battle, regardless of nationality or which side they were on. It is a quiet, exposed, coastal place and it is the most affecting site on the island.

The Former Japanese Navy Underground Headquarters, a tunnel complex cut into a hill near Naha, is preserved and open, and is a hard visit.

Okinawa hosts a large proportion of the American military presence in Japan on a small fraction of its land, which remains a source of local political friction that visitors will see referenced. Understanding that this is a live issue rather than history is part of understanding the place.

Naha, and getting around

Naha is the capital and the arrival point. Kokusai-dori, the main street, is a kilometre of souvenir shops and restaurants and is worth one walk; the covered market streets running off it, particularly Makishi Public Market, are more interesting.

Tsuboya is the pottery district, where Ryukyuan ceramics — heavy, bold-glazed, often with fish or floral motifs — have been made for three centuries. The kilns and shops are walkable and the pieces are among the best souvenirs in Japan.

The monorail runs from the airport through the city to Shuri and is easy. Beyond that, the island has no railway at all. Buses exist and are slow. To see the island properly you need a rental car, and that is the single most important logistical fact about Okinawa.

An international driving permit is required, and it must be obtained before leaving home.

North of the city

The Churaumi Aquarium on the Motobu peninsula is one of the largest in the world and the main draw for domestic tourists. Its central tank holds whale sharks and manta rays and is genuinely impressive. It is around two hours from Naha by car.

The northern third of the island, Yambaru, is subtropical forest and a national park, with an endemic flightless bird and a great deal of biodiversity found nowhere else. It is UNESCO listed and it is quiet.

Cape Manzamo, Kouri island with its bridge, and the beaches along the west coast are the standard route. The east coast is emptier.

Nakagusuku and Zakimi castle ruins — gusuku, the Ryukyuan fortress form built in dry-stone curves rather than Japanese straight lines — are part of the same UNESCO listing as Shuri and are more atmospheric for being ruins.

The food and the drink

Okinawan food is its own cuisine. Goya champuru, the stir-fry of bitter melon with tofu, egg and pork, is the signature. Rafute is pork belly slow-cooked in awamori and brown sugar. Okinawa soba is neither soba nor ramen but its own wheat noodle in a light pork broth, usually with a piece of stewed rib on top.

Pork is used comprehensively, including the ears and the face, which is a Chinese inheritance. Sea grapes — umibudo, a seaweed with small bubbles that pop — are the local delicacy and unlike anything else.

Awamori is the distilled spirit, made from long-grain Thai rice with black koji, and aged versions are called kusu. It is stronger than shochu and traditionally drunk with water and ice.

The American presence left a distinct layer too: taco rice, canned pork in everything, and A&W root beer.

When to go

Spring and late autumn are the best conditions. Summer is very hot and is typhoon season, which can genuinely strip days out of a trip, and August is peak domestic holiday.

Winter is mild and quiet, and while it is too cool for comfortable swimming for most people, everything else is open and cheap. Whale watching runs in the winter months.

The rainy season arrives earlier here than on the main islands, generally through May and into June.
