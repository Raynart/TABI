Tenugui, Furoshiki and Noren: Japanese Cloth Worth Buying

Flat, light, cheap and useful. Japanese textiles are the best souvenirs nobody thinks to buy, and they weigh nothing in a suitcase already full of ceramics.

The case for cloth

Most souvenir problems are problems of weight, fragility and use. Textiles solve all three: they are flat, unbreakable, weigh almost nothing, and the traditional forms are things people actually use daily in Japan rather than ornaments.

They are also among the cheapest genuinely well-made things you can buy in the country. A hand-printed cotton cloth from a shop that has been printing since the Edo period costs less than lunch.

Tenugui

A tenugui is a thin cotton cloth, roughly ninety by thirty-five centimetres, unhemmed on the long edges so it dries quickly. It has been the standard Japanese hand towel for centuries and it is still in daily use.

The uses are the point: hand towel, dish cloth, head covering in summer, wrapping for a lunch box, cover for a bottle, and — because the designs are frequently beautiful — framed or hung on a wall.

Designs run from traditional patterns with specific meanings, through seasonal motifs that change through the year, to the collaborations and novelty prints that shops now produce constantly. The seasonal ones are worth knowing about: a shop's stock rotates, and a summer pattern bought in summer is a small piece of the calendar.

The cut edges fray for the first few washes and then stop. This is normal and not a defect.

Furoshiki

A furoshiki is a square wrapping cloth, used to carry things by tying rather than by handle. The name comes from bath houses, where they were used to bundle clothes.

There is a whole grammar of folds: different ties for a bottle, a box, a book, a watermelon, and a fold that produces a carrying handle. The techniques take minutes to learn and shops sell instruction cards with the cloth.

Sizes matter. Around fifty centimetres is a lunch box or a gift; seventy to ninety is a bag; over a metre is luggage. Buy at least one large one if you intend to use it as a bag.

They have had a revival as a reusable alternative to gift wrap and shopping bags, and this is a genuinely good thing to bring home: it is useful, it is unlike anything sold elsewhere, and giving a gift wrapped in one is a small pleasure.

Noren

The split curtain hanging in a doorway is a noren. In Japan it is a signal as much as a screen: hung out, the shop is open; taken in, it is closed.

They are sold for domestic use in every size, and a noren in a doorway at home is one of the more transformative small changes an interior can take. Indigo-dyed cotton is traditional; the shop-style ones with a name or a crest are the striking ones.

Check the rod pocket at the top and buy or improvise a tension rod to hang it from.

Indigo, and the dyeing traditions

Aizome, indigo dyeing, is the deepest of the Japanese textile traditions, and Tokushima on Shikoku was the historic centre of it. Real indigo has a depth that synthetic dye does not, it smells faintly of the fermentation vat, and it fades over years into something better than it started.

Shibori is the resist-dyeing technique in which cloth is bound, stitched or folded before dyeing so the bound parts stay pale. Arimatsu near Nagoya is the traditional centre and has a museum and workshops.

Katazome uses stencils cut from paper to apply resist paste; the crispness of a tenugui pattern usually comes from this. Workshops in Kyoto and Tokyo run short courses where you dye your own piece, which produces a better souvenir than anything you can buy.

Other cloth worth a look

Sashiko is the running-stitch technique originally used to reinforce and repair working clothes, now a decorative craft in its own right. Kits with pre-printed patterns are widely sold and make good presents for anyone who sews.

Boro — patched, layered, heavily repaired old textiles — has become collectable and is sold in antique markets. It is the physical embodiment of the mending aesthetic that kintsugi expresses in ceramics.

Kaya, the loosely woven cloth originally used for mosquito nets, is now made into exceptionally absorbent dish towels that are a staple gift.

And a yukata is a textile purchase as much as a garment one, if the fabric is what appeals.

Reading the patterns

Traditional motifs carry meaning, and a surprising amount of it survives in what is sold today. Asanoha, the hemp-leaf lattice, was used on children's clothing because hemp grows fast and straight. Seigaiha, the layered wave, stands for waves of good fortune. Shippo, interlocking circles, means harmony and connection.

Cranes and turtles are longevity, pine bamboo and plum together are the auspicious trio for New Year, and the seasonal flowers each belong to a specific few weeks of the year.

None of this is required knowledge for buying, but it turns a nice pattern into a deliberate choice — and it makes the difference between a generic gift and one selected for the person receiving it.

Where to buy

Specialist tenugui shops exist in Tokyo and Kyoto and are the enjoyable option: walls of folded cloth arranged by season and pattern, with staff who will explain the motifs.

Department store craft floors carry the established makers. Temple and shrine shops sell their own designs, which make specific and personal souvenirs.

Antique and flea markets — the temple markets in Kyoto, Oedo in Tokyo — are where old indigo, boro and vintage kimono fabric turn up, often cheaply.

Avoid the airport. The selection is thin and the designs are the generic ones.
