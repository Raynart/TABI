Your First Ryokan: What to Expect, What to Do, and What Not to Wear

A traditional Japanese inn is not a hotel. The sooner you accept that, the better the stay.

What you are actually booking

A ryokan is not a hotel with tatami. It is a package: a room, a bath, dinner and breakfast, staffed by people whose job is to move you through an evening in a particular order. The price is quoted per person including both meals, not per room, which is the first thing that confuses visitors comparing it against hotel rates.

There is a hierarchy of names worth knowing. A ryokan is the full version. A minshuku is the family-run, simpler equivalent — think bed-and-breakfast, shared bathroom, home cooking, considerably cheaper. An onsen hotel is a large modern building with hot springs and buffet meals, which is what a lot of Japanese families actually book and is a different, less formal experience.

Cancellation policies are strict, because the kitchen buys for you specifically. Expect meaningful charges from a week out and full charge within a day or two.

Check-in, checkout, and why timing matters

Check-in is usually 3pm and checkout is 10am. These are not suggestions. Your room is being transformed twice a day — futon laid out during dinner, rolled away before breakfast — and that choreography is coordinated with the bath and meal schedule.

Arriving at 6pm and expecting the room to be ready the way a hotel room is always ready will produce confusion on both sides, and if dinner is set for 6:30 it will produce a genuine problem. Tell them your arrival time when you book, and call if you are running late.

The early checkout catches people too. Breakfast at 7:30 and out by 10 is a short morning, and it is deliberate — the staff need the room. Plan the following day accordingly rather than expecting a slow start.

Shoes, slippers, and one rule about the toilet

You remove your shoes at the entrance and switch into slippers. The slippers go everywhere except tatami rooms — on tatami you go barefoot or in socks, never in slippers.

There will be a separate pair of toilet slippers in the bathroom. Put them on to enter, take them off to leave. Forgetting and wandering the hallway in toilet slippers is the ryokan equivalent of walking through a dinner party in a life jacket, and it happens to almost everyone once.

Your shoes will be put away for you and produced again when you leave. This is normal and you do not need to keep track of them.

Your room, and the yukata

Your room will have a low table, zabuton cushions, a tokonoma alcove with a seasonal arrangement, a yukata folded somewhere, and a kettle with tea and a small sweet. There will be no bed visible, because it is in the cupboard.

The yukata is your uniform. Wear it to the bath, to dinner if the place is traditional, back to your room, and to bed. It is a cotton robe, not a costume, and in an onsen town it is also correct outdoor wear for walking to the other bathhouses. Left side over right — right over left is how the dead are dressed.

There is usually a heavier jacket called a tanzen to go over it in winter, and wooden geta at the entrance. Amenities are comprehensive: toothbrush, razor, towels, often skincare. You need to bring very little.

The onsen

The bath operates on a schedule — typically gender-segregated, sometimes with the men's and women's baths swapping over during the night so that everyone gets both. The rules: wash thoroughly at the seated shower station before entering the communal water, and enter clean.

Do not put the towel in the bath. The small hand towel goes on your head or on the side. Do not swim, do not splash, keep long hair tied up, and do not get in if you have been drinking heavily — people faint, and it is a problem for everyone.

Two things worth checking before you book. Tattoos are still refused at a proportion of traditional baths, though this is easing; ryokan with private baths attached to rooms, or with bookable family baths, sidestep the issue entirely. And if the water is a strong sulphur spring it will tarnish silver jewellery, so leave it in the room.

Kaiseki dinner

Dinner is kaiseki — a multi-course meal built around seasonal and local ingredients, arriving in a sequence you do not choose and cannot rush. It takes ninety minutes to two hours, the plates are small, and you will briefly feel you are not getting enough food. You are getting enough food.

Do not ask for the menu in advance and do not request variations casually. But do declare genuine allergies and dietary restrictions at the time of booking, not on arrival — the kitchen plans and shops for each guest, and a vegetarian request made at 6pm cannot be accommodated. Made three weeks earlier, it usually can.

It is often served in your room by an attendant, which is part of what you are paying for. In larger ryokan it is in a dining room at a set time. Either way the time is fixed when you check in, and being late is a real inconvenience to a kitchen cooking to the minute.

Breakfast, and the evening in between

Breakfast follows the same logic: rice, miso soup, pickled vegetables, grilled fish, tofu, a small egg dish, arriving at a fixed time between roughly 7:30 and 9. It is also among the best breakfasts in the world.

The evening between dinner and sleep is the part first-timers underuse. In an onsen town, this is when you put on the yukata and geta and walk — to the other bathhouses, to a bar, to a shrine, clattering along in wooden sandals with everyone else doing the same. In an isolated mountain ryokan it is a second bath and a book.

There is no tipping in Japan and a ryokan is no exception. There is an old custom of kokorozuke, a small amount in an envelope to the room attendant, which is optional, increasingly rare, and never expected of a foreign guest. Do not leave cash on a tray.

What it costs

Prices run from around ¥8,000 per person at a decent mid-range place with a bath and breakfast, to ¥80,000 and well beyond at the top with full kaiseki and a private open-air bath.

The sweet spot is ¥15,000–25,000 per person, particularly in onsen towns without the premium attached to Hakone or Kyoto — Kinosaki in Hyogo, Gero in Gifu, and a long list of places in Tohoku that are genuinely excellent and cost half what the famous names do.

Winter is materially cheaper than autumn for the same room, and midweek cheaper than weekends by a wide margin. One night is enough to understand it; two nights in the same ryokan is when it becomes restful rather than novel.

On language

The staff may not speak much English, and that is genuinely fine. The experience requires very little language to work, because the sequence is fixed and everyone knows what happens next.

Arigatou gozaimasu and oishii will handle most of it. The rest comes from gestures, patience, and the fact that people who run a ryokan have been doing this for decades and know how to communicate without words. If anything, the places with no English are the ones worth seeking out.
