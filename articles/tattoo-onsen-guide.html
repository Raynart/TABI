Tattoos and Onsen in Japan: What the Policy Actually Is

Most onsen ban tattoos. Fewer than you'd think actually enforce it. Here is how to navigate it honestly.

In this article

  1. Why the ban exists
  2. The official policy versus practice
  3. The options, in order of reliability
  4. How to ask, and when
  5. It is not only onsen
  6. The honest approach

Why the ban exists

The tattoo ban at Japanese onsen traces to the historical association between full-body tattoos and organised crime. It was introduced not as a statement about tattoos generally but as a practical measure to keep criminal organisations out of communal bathing facilities, and it has persisted for decades largely through institutional inertia.

It is worth knowing that this association is relatively recent in a long history. Tattooing in Japan goes back thousands of years and had a substantial decorative tradition in the Edo period among firefighters, labourers and merchants, before being banned outright by the Meiji government as part of a broader project of appearing modern to Western eyes. The criminal association hardened during the twentieth century.

Attitudes have shifted significantly since Japan began preparing for large numbers of international visitors, and the shift is ongoing. The situation now is meaningfully different from ten years ago, and it will be different again in another ten.

The official policy versus practice

Official policy at most large resort onsen and hotel spa facilities is no tattoos, regardless of size. Enforcement varies enormously. At a small neighbourhood sento run by the same family for forty years, nobody is checking anything. At a large facility near a tourist area, staff may ask at the desk, and there are sometimes signs with illustrations at the entrance.

The underlying concern has always been the comfort of other bathers rather than the tattoo itself. A visible, extensive tattoo in a shared bath makes some Japanese guests — particularly older ones — genuinely uneasy, and the facility is managing that rather than expressing an opinion about your arm.

Some places have moved to a no-visible-tattoo policy rather than a blanket ban, which in practice means a small tattoo that a cover patch will hide is fine and a full sleeve is not.

The options, in order of reliability

A private bath is the cleanest answer. Kashikiri-buro — family baths bookable by the hour — are offered by a great many ryokan and onsen hotels, and there are no other guests and therefore no policy. Rooms with their own open-air bath attached cost more and solve the problem permanently. This is often the more relaxing option regardless of whether you have tattoos.

Tattoo-friendly facilities exist and their number grows every year. Several online directories maintain lists; treat them as a starting point and confirm directly, because policies change and listings go stale.

Covering works for small tattoos only. Waterproof cover patches are sold at some facilities and at drugstores, and they need to actually stay on in hot water. A patch peeling off mid-bath is a worse outcome than not going in.

The last option is simply the day-use bath at a place that does not care. Small municipal sento in ordinary neighbourhoods are the most relaxed category in the country, and they are also cheap, unglamorous and among the better things to do in Japan.

How to ask, and when

Ask before you book, not on arrival. Most facilities have handled the question many times and will give you a straight answer. Email works better than the phone for a foreign visitor, and hotel concierges will call on your behalf.

The phrase is 'tattoo wa daijoubu desu ka' — is a tattoo all right. If you want to be precise, 'chiisai tattoo ga arimasu' means I have a small tattoo. Bringing a photograph on your phone is more useful than any description.

Arriving, undressing, and being asked to leave at the threshold of the bath is unpleasant for you and for the staff member who has to do it. That scenario is entirely avoidable and avoiding it is the whole of the advice here.

It is not only onsen

The same restriction turns up in places visitors do not anticipate: public swimming pools, hotel gyms and pools, water parks, some beaches with managed facilities, and occasionally capsule hotels and gyms.

Water parks and municipal pools tend to be stricter than onsen, not more relaxed, and they are the ones most likely to enforce at the entrance. If a day at a pool is part of your plan, check that specifically.

Conversely, ordinary daily life is entirely unaffected. Nobody cares about a visible tattoo on the street, on a train, in a restaurant or in a shop, and the idea that Japan is generally hostile to tattooed visitors is not accurate.

The honest approach

Ask first, respect the answer, and do not attempt to argue the policy at a front desk. The person you would be arguing with did not write it and cannot change it, and the conversation has a guaranteed outcome.

It is reasonable to find the rule outdated. It is also somebody else's bath, in somebody else's country, and the practical position — book a private bath, find a facility that welcomes you, or use a neighbourhood sento — costs you almost nothing and works every time.

The direction of travel is clear. More facilities open to tattooed guests every year, tourism bodies have been pushing for exactly that, and younger Japanese people increasingly have tattoos themselves. It is a problem that is slowly solving itself.
