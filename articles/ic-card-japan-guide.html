The IC Card Is the Best Thing in Japan. Here's How to Actually Use It.

You can survive Tokyo's train network without one. You just won't want to.

  2. Which card, and does it matter
  3. Getting hold of one

  5. Loading and tapping

Get one before you leave the airport

Find a ticket machine and get an IC card. Do not deliberate, do not check whether you really need one. You need one.

It is a rechargeable transit card — the same basic idea as London's Oyster or Hong Kong's Octopus — except that in Japan it works on practically everything: trains, subways, buses, some ferries, taxis in major cities, convenience stores, vending machines, coin lockers, and the self-checkout at Don Quijote at two in the morning.

Which card, and does it matter

Suica is issued by JR East and covers Tokyo and eastern Japan; ICOCA is the JR West equivalent for Osaka, Kyoto and Kobe. There are ten major cards in total, including PASMO in Tokyo, TOICA, manaca, SUGOCA and others.

They are mutually interoperable for travel and shopping across almost the whole country, so the practical answer is to take whichever is sold where you land. A Suica works fine in Kyoto and an ICOCA works fine in Tokyo.

The exceptions are narrow but real: a handful of rural private railways and local buses take cash only, and refunds are only possible at the issuing company's offices — an ICOCA cannot be cashed in at a JR East window.

Getting hold of one

A physical card costs a ¥500 deposit on top of whatever you load. Availability of standard unregistered cards has been intermittent in recent years because of a semiconductor shortage, with sales suspended and then partially resumed, so check the current position rather than assuming.

The reliable fallback for visitors is the tourist version — Welcome Suica and its ICOCA equivalent — sold at airport counters and major stations, with no deposit and a validity of about 28 days. It works identically and any remaining balance is simply lost at the end, so do not over-load it.

The better answer if your phone supports it is a mobile card, which sidesteps the physical stock entirely.

Mobile Suica is the upgrade

Recent iPhones and many Android phones can hold a Suica in the wallet app, and on iPhone it can be created and topped up with an overseas card from your own sofa before you fly. Setup takes about ten minutes and the process is in English.

The advantage is not novelty. You top up from the app instead of finding a machine, you can see the balance without tapping, and you cannot leave it in a jacket pocket. It also removes the deposit and the refund question entirely.

One caution: the phone must have enough battery to work at the gate. Japanese phones use a mode that functions on reserve power for a short period; not all handsets do. A completely dead phone is a card you cannot use.

Loading and tapping

Load at any ticket machine: insert the card, press Charge — there is an English option on every machine — and add ¥1,000 to ¥10,000. Machines take cash, and most take only notes and coins rather than foreign cards. Keep at least ¥2,000 on it; the maximum balance is ¥20,000.

Tap flat on the reader going in, tap again going out. A double beep and a red light means insufficient balance: step aside and use the fare adjustment machine before the gates rather than holding up the queue.

Do not tap twice on entry, and do not put two IC cards in the same wallet — the reader sees both and rejects them. This is the single most common gate failure among visitors.

Everything else it does

Convenience stores, supermarkets, chain restaurants, many vending machines and most coin lockers take it, and paying this way is noticeably faster than cash. In practice a visitor can run most small daily spending off the card and keep cash for the places that take nothing else.

It is also the tidiest solution to the coin problem. Japan's cash economy generates handfuls of change constantly, and a station machine will swallow all of it onto the card in one go.

What it does not cover: Shinkansen and limited express reserved seats, which need a separate ticket or a registered mobile setup, and long-distance JR travel beyond regional boundaries. It is a local card, and crossing between company regions on one journey can fail even where both ends accept it.

When you leave

Keep it. Physical cards do not expire for ten years from last use, and having one ready for the next trip is genuinely convenient. Balances survive.

If you want the money back, a JR office of the issuing company will refund the balance and the deposit, minus a handling fee of a couple of hundred yen. If the balance is small, the fee eats most of it and keeping the card is the better call. Welcome Suica cards are not refundable at all.

The larger point is that the card removes an entire class of friction. You never read a fare table, never choose a destination on a machine, never work out which company's ticket you need at a station served by three of them. Get it at the airport, and everything else in Japanese transport gets easier.
