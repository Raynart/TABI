The Only Japanese Phrases You Actually Need

Japanese is difficult. A handful of phrases isn’t. The ten expressions below will handle the majority of situations a visitor encounters, and they take about 20 minutes to learn.

Why bother

English in Japan is available, patchily. Major chain restaurants have English or picture menus. Train stations in Tokyo and Kyoto are multilingual. Hotel staff at international properties speak English. Beyond these contexts — at the neighbourhood izakaya, the market stall, the onsen town's local restaurant — Japanese is what you have.

A few phrases, used with reasonable pronunciation and a willingness to repeat them, will get you through almost everything. Nobody expects fluency. They do notice the attempt, and the shift in warmth when a visitor opens with sumimasen rather than 'excuse me' is immediate and consistent.

There is also a practical point. Many Japanese people studied English for years and can read it far better than they can speak it, and the barrier is often confidence rather than vocabulary. Slowing down, using short sentences and writing things down unlocks a surprising amount.

The four daily essentials

Sumimasen — excuse me, sorry to bother you. The Swiss Army knife of Japanese phrases. Use it to get attention in a restaurant, to apologise for bumping into someone, to start a question, to ask for help. If you learn one word, learn this one.

Arigatou gozaimasu — thank you very much. The full form; the bare arigatou is casual and slightly abrupt to a stranger. Hai — yes, and also a general acknowledgement that you have heard and are following. Iie — no, softer than it sounds, though in practice Japanese speakers avoid a flat no and so can you.

Add onegaishimasu — please, in the sense of 'if you would' — and you have five. It attaches to almost any request and makes it polite: point at a menu item and say onegaishimasu and the transaction is complete.

These, repeated calmly and clearly, handle a remarkable proportion of daily interaction.

At restaurants

Kore wo kudasai — this please. Point at the menu item. Three words, works everywhere. Hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu — one, two, three, for quantities. O-kaikei kudasai — the bill please; you can also trace a rectangle in the air with both index fingers and everyone understands.

Two more are worth having because they are social rather than transactional. Itadakimasu is said before eating, to no one in particular, and gochisousama deshita is said on leaving — it thanks the kitchen for the meal and it is the single phrase most likely to get a genuine reaction from a small restaurant. Oishii, delicious, used mid-meal, does the same job.

For a bar: nama hitotsu onegaishimasu gets you a draught beer, and osusume wa nan desu ka asks what they recommend, which is usually the right question in a place with a handwritten menu.

Getting around

[Destination] wa doko desu ka — where is [destination]. Fill in the place name, show it written down or on a map, and you will receive directions, usually very thorough directions, occasionally with an escort who walks you there.

Chotto matte kudasai — please wait a moment, for when someone is talking faster than you can process. Mou ichido onegaishimasu — once more please. Yukkuri onegaishimasu — slowly please. Those last two do more work than any amount of extra vocabulary.

For taxis, the useful sentence is not a sentence: show the address in Japanese, or better, read out the destination's phone number, which Japanese car navigation systems search on directly.

Shopping

Ikura desu ka — how much is it. Most shops will type the figure on a calculator or point at the tag, but the question opens the interaction correctly. Kore wa nan desu ka — what is this, for market stalls and menus where the item is unidentifiable. Expect a demonstration rather than an explanation.

Two that save real trouble: kore, arimasu ka — do you have this — while showing a photograph, and kaado, tsukaemasu ka — can I use a card, which is worth asking before you order rather than after.

When a shop assistant greets you with irasshaimase, no response is required. It is an announcement, not a question, and visitors often stall trying to answer it.

Declining, and getting help

Daijoubu desu is the most useful phrase nobody teaches. It means roughly 'it's fine' and functions as a soft no — to a bag at the till, to another drink, to an offer of help you do not need. Kekkou desu is the firmer version.

Wakarimasen — I don't understand. Nihongo ga wakarimasen — I don't understand Japanese. Eigo ga hanaseru hito wa imasu ka — is there someone who speaks English. That last one is more useful than asking an individual whether they speak English, because it lets them fetch a colleague rather than decline.

And the one to know and hopefully not use: tasukete kudasai, please help. For anything official, koban means a police box, and there is one near every major station.

Pronunciation

Japanese vowels are consistent: A is 'ah', I is 'ee', U is 'oo', E is 'eh', O is 'oh'. They do not shift the way English vowels do. Arigatou is ah-ree-gah-toh, not ah-rig-uh-toe. Getting the vowels right matters more than anything else and takes five minutes of practice.

Two more adjustments. Japanese is flat in stress — English speakers instinctively stress a syllable and it is the main thing that makes a simple word unrecognisable. And the u at the end of words is often barely voiced: desu is closer to 'dess', and gozaimasu ends 'mahss'.

Japanese people will meet you much more than halfway as long as the vowels are approximately correct. Speaking slowly and evenly beats speaking accurately and fast.

The mistakes worth avoiding

Sayonara is not 'bye'. It carries a sense of parting for a long time, and using it when leaving a shop is odd. Use arigatou gozaimasu, or shitsurei shimasu when leaving somewhere formal.

Do not attach -san to your own name; it is a title you give other people. Do not use anata for 'you' — it reads as distant or intimate depending on context and neither is what you want; use the person's surname plus -san. And you do not need watashi wa at the start of every sentence, because Japanese drops the subject when it is obvious.

Konnichiwa is daytime only. Ohayou gozaimasu in the morning, konbanwa in the evening. Getting this wrong is harmless and mildly funny, which is a reasonable description of most beginner errors here.

The characters worth recognising

You do not need to read Japanese, but eight or ten characters will save you real time. 入口 is entrance and 出口 is exit. 押 is push and 引 is pull, on every door. 男 is men and 女 is women, on every lavatory and every onsen curtain, and confusing those two is the one error with genuine consequences.

営業中 means open for business and 準備中 means preparing, which is the sign on a restaurant that will open in an hour. 無料 is free of charge. 円 is yen. 予約 is a reservation. 禁煙 is no smoking.

On a menu, 大 is large and 小 is small, and 定食 means a set meal with rice and soup — which is usually the thing to order.

Translation apps, and where they fail

The camera translation in Google Translate handles printed menus and signs well enough to be genuinely useful, and downloading the Japanese pack before you arrive means it works without a connection. Conversation mode works for simple exchanges if you hand the phone over rather than pointing it at someone.

Where it fails is handwriting, which is most of the menus in the restaurants worth eating in, and the compressed shorthand used on signs. It also renders dish names into nonsense with confidence — a machine translation promising 'burnt bird' is yakitori.

The best use of a phone is not translation but display: a photograph of the dish, a map pin, the destination written in Japanese, the item you want. Showing beats saying, nearly every time.
