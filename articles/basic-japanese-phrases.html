The Only Japanese Phrases You Actually Need

Japanese is difficult. A handful of phrases isn’t. The ten expressions below will handle the majority of situations a visitor encounters, and they take about 20 minutes to learn.

In this article

  1. Why bother
  2. The four daily essentials
  3. At restaurants
  4. Getting around
  5. Shopping
  6. Pronunciation

Why bother

English in Japan is available, patchily. Major chain restaurants have English menus or picture menus. Train stations in Tokyo and Kyoto are multilingual. Hotel staff at international properties speak English. Beyond these contexts — at the neighborhood izakaya, the market stall, the onsen town’s local restaurant — Japanese is what you have. A few phrases, used with reasonable pronunciation and a willingness to repeat them, will get you through almost everything. Nobody expects fluency. They do notice the attempt.

The four daily essentials

Sumimasen — excuse me, sorry to bother you. The Swiss Army knife of Japanese phrases. Use it to get attention in a restaurant, to apologize for bumping into someone, to start a question, to ask for help. Arigatou gozaimasu — thank you very much. The formal version. Useful for anything beyond casual thanks. Hai — yes. More affirmative than it translates; also used to acknowledge that you heard something. Ie / iie — no. Softer than it sounds. These four phrases, repeated calmly and clearly, handle a remarkable proportion of basic daily interactions.

At restaurants

Kore wo kudasai — this please. Point at the menu item. Three words, works everywhere. Hitotsu / futatsu / mittsu — one / two / three. Ordering quantities. O-kaikei kudasai — the bill please. You can also do the universal rectangular-finger-gesture (tracing a rectangle in the air with both index fingers) and everyone understands. Oishii — delicious. Using this mid-meal in a small restaurant will reliably cause the chef to emerge from the kitchen.

Getting around

[ Destination ] wa doko desu ka? — Where is [ destination ]? Fill in the location name. Show the name written down and follow this phrase with the map on your phone and you will receive directions, usually very thorough directions, sometimes with an escort. Chotto matte kudasai — please wait a moment. For when someone is talking faster than you can process.

Shopping

Ikura desu ka? — How much is it? Most shops will simply type the price on a calculator or show you the price tag, but the question opens the interaction correctly. Kore wa nan desu ka? — What is this? For menus and market stalls where the item is unidentifiable. Expect a demonstration.

Pronunciation

Japanese vowels are consistent: A is ‘ah,’ I is ‘ee,’ U is ‘oo,’ E is ‘eh,’ O is ‘oh.’ They don’t shift the way English vowels do. ‘Arigatou’ is pronounced ah-ree-gah-toh, not ah-rig-uh-toe. Getting the vowels right matters more than anything else and takes five minutes of practice. Japanese people will meet you much more than halfway on pronunciation as long as the vowels are approximately correct.
