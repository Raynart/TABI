Why You Should Never Tip in Japan — and What to Do Instead

In Japan, the service is built into the price. Adding a tip doesn’t reward the server — it creates an awkward situation for both of you.

The rule

Do not tip in Japan. Not at restaurants, not in taxis, not at the ryokan, not at the hotel. The rule applies everywhere, with essentially no exceptions.

This is not a matter of the tip being too small or the service not meriting it. Tipping is simply not part of the service culture, and attempting it creates confusion rather than goodwill.

Why tipping is not done

Service in Japan is understood as part of what you already paid for. The waiter refilling your water, the taxi driver operating the door from the front seat, the ryokan attendant laying out your futon — all are doing the job to a high standard because that is the expectation, not because a financial incentive is available for exceeding it.

Tipping implies the service was beyond what was expected, which implies the expectation was low. That is not the message most people intend to send.

There is a structural reason too: hospitality staff here are paid a wage rather than being expected to make it up in gratuities, so the tip is not filling a gap in anyone's income. The practice has no function to perform.

What happens if you try

If you leave cash on a restaurant table, staff will frequently chase you down the street to return it, assuming you forgot it. If you hand money to a server directly, it will usually be declined, sometimes with visible discomfort.

The genuinely awkward version is at a small owner-run place, where refusing costs the owner face and accepting costs them something else. You have created a problem for someone who was having a perfectly good evening.

None of these outcomes is the one you were hoping for. The generous act here is to pay the bill and say thank you.

Charges that are not tips

Two things on Japanese bills get mistaken for tipping and neither is.

Otoshi, at an izakaya, is a small dish that arrives unordered at the start along with a per-person charge of a few hundred yen. It is a seating and cover charge and it is not optional, not a scam, and not something to send back. If you are surprised by an extra ¥500 a head on an izakaya bill, this is what it was.

A service charge — sabisu-ryo, often ten per cent — appears at high-end restaurants, hotel restaurants and ryokan, and is stated on the menu or the tariff. Where it appears it is already in the total. It is not a tip and it does not mean you should add one.

Consumption tax is separate again and is now generally shown included in the displayed price, so the number on the menu is the number you pay.

The ryokan exception, properly named

There is one traditional practice that resembles a tip, and it is worth naming correctly: kokorozuke. It is a small amount given to the room attendant at a ryokan, on arrival rather than departure, as a gesture of thanks for the personal service through the stay. The typical amount is ¥1,000–3,000.

The form matters more than the sum. It goes in a small envelope — the plain ones sold at any convenience store or stationery shop are correct — and it is presented with both hands. Loose cash is not kokorozuke; it is just money on a table.

It is optional, increasingly rare, and never expected of foreign guests. Plenty of ryokan will politely decline it. If the whole idea makes you uneasy, skip it; nothing about your stay will be different.

The same logic applies to a private guide or a driver you have hired for a day, where a small envelope at the end is accepted more readily than a handful of notes.

In taxis, bars and hotels

Taxi fare is metered. You pay the meter, you do not round up, and the driver will produce exact change and expect you to take it. Saying 'keep it' lands as strange rather than generous.

Bars work the same way, including the ones where a bartender spends four minutes making one drink. Hotel porters and housekeeping are not tipped. Nor are hairdressers, delivery drivers or anyone else.

The one place money changes hands unusually is a hostess bar or similar, where the pricing structure is its own subject and has nothing to do with gratuity.

What to do instead

What the culture does value in place of a tip is acknowledgement. 'Gochisousama deshita' on leaving a restaurant thanks the kitchen for the meal and is the single most effective thing you can say — far more so than money, and it costs nothing but the effort of learning six syllables.

'Arigatou gozaimashita' with a small bow works everywhere else. Saying it to the specific person who helped you, rather than to the room, is what makes it land.

Beyond words: returning to the same restaurant twice in a trip is understood as a compliment, and telling a chef which dish you liked is the kind of feedback small places rarely get from visitors. If you genuinely want to reward good service, a review or a recommendation reaches further than ¥1,000 ever would.
