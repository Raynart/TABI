Okinawan Food: Pork, Bitter Melon and the Longevity Myth

Not Japanese food as you know it. Chinese technique, south-east Asian ingredients, American canned pork, and a set of dishes built around a bitter gourd most first-timers approach with suspicion.

Why it is so different

Okinawan cooking descends from the Ryukyu Kingdom's position as a trading state between China, Japan and south-east Asia, not from mainland Japanese cuisine. The techniques lean Chinese — stir-frying, long braises, the comprehensive use of pork. The ingredients are subtropical. The seasoning is lighter on soy and dashi than mainland cooking and heavier on pork stock, salt and awamori.

Then the twentieth century added another layer. The American administration that ran Okinawa until 1972 left canned pork, root beer, taco seasoning and a durable fondness for all of them.

The result genuinely surprises people who arrive expecting a regional variation on the food they had in Tokyo.

Goya champuru, and the bitter melon problem

Champuru means mixed, and it describes a family of stir-fries combining tofu, egg, some vegetable and usually pork. Goya champuru, made with bitter melon, is the emblematic dish of the islands.

Goya is genuinely bitter — not subtly, not as a background note. Salting the sliced gourd and rinsing it reduces it, and the egg and tofu soften it, but it remains the point of the dish rather than an obstacle to it. Most visitors dislike it initially and a good proportion come round within a few days.

Other champuru use papaya, bean sprouts or the local tofu, which is firmer and denser than mainland tofu and is sold warm from the maker in the morning.

Pork, used completely

There is a saying that Okinawans eat every part of the pig except its squeal, and it is close to accurate. Ears are sliced thin into salads. Face and trotters are braised. Offal goes into soup.

Rafute is the dish to try first: belly pork simmered slowly in awamori, soy and brown sugar until it collapses. It is close to kakuni but sweeter and more aromatic from the spirit.

Soki is stewed rib, most often seen on top of noodles. And then there is spam, which arrived with the American military and became genuinely embedded — spam onigiri wrapped with a folded omelette is sold in every convenience store on the islands and is unironically good.

Okinawa soba, which is neither

Okinawa soba contains no buckwheat and is not soba in the mainland sense. The noodles are wheat, thick, slightly chewy and closer to udon; the broth is light, pork and bonito based; the toppings are stewed rib or belly, pickled ginger, spring onion and a little fish cake.

Regional versions differ. Soki soba has the rib. Yaeyama soba on the southern islands uses rounder, finer noodles. Miyako soba hides the meat under the noodles, for reasons that are explained differently depending on who you ask.

It is the everyday meal here, sold in small shops that often close when the day's stock runs out. Koregusu — chilli peppers steeped in awamori — sits on every table as the standard condiment and should be added a drop at a time.

The sea, and the vegetables

Umibudo, sea grapes, are a seaweed of tiny translucent spheres on a stem that burst with a mild salinity. They are served raw with a dipping sauce and are the single most distinctive thing on an Okinawan table.

Mozuku, another seaweed, comes in vinegar as a small starter. Irabu-jiru, sea snake soup, exists and is a traditional delicacy that most visitors will hear about rather than eat.

Beni-imo, the purple sweet potato, is everywhere in both savoury and sweet form and is the basis of the standard souvenir tart. Shikuwasa, a small sour citrus, goes into juice, ponzu and cocktails.

Awamori

Awamori is Okinawa's distilled spirit and it predates shochu. It is made from long-grain Thai rice using black koji, distilled once, and traditionally aged in clay pots. Aged for three years or more it is called kusu, and the good ones are remarkable — rounder and more complex than the young spirit.

It is stronger than shochu, commonly around thirty per cent and sometimes far more. It is normally drunk with water and ice, in roughly equal parts, which is not dilution but the standard serve.

Habushu, awamori with a pit viper in the bottle, is real and is sold as a curiosity. It is not what anyone drinks.

The longevity story, honestly

Okinawa became famous as a place with an exceptional number of centenarians, and the traditional diet — heavy in sweet potato and vegetables, light in rice, with pork eaten on occasions rather than daily — is a plausible part of that.

The complication is that this advantage has been eroding for decades. The post-war shift towards imported and processed food hit Okinawa harder and earlier than the rest of Japan, and younger generations no longer show the same outcomes.

So the food is worth eating because it is good and unlike anything else in Japan. The health claims attached to it in the wider world describe a diet that the islands themselves have largely stopped eating.

Where to eat it

Public markets are the best entry point — Makishi in Naha has stalls downstairs and restaurants above that will cook what you buy. Small neighbourhood shokudo serve champuru and soba at low prices and close early.

Izakaya in Naha and Ishigaki serve the full range with awamori and often live sanshin music. And for the American layer, the diners around the base towns serve taco rice and steak in a setting that is a preserved piece of the 1960s.
