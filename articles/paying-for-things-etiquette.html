The Little Tray: How Paying for Things Works in Japan

There is a small dish beside every till and it is not decorative. Money is placed, not handed, and the change comes back with a precision that visitors mistake for suspicion.

The tray

Beside the register in almost every shop in Japan there is a small tray, often lined with rubber or felt. Money goes in it. The cashier takes it from the tray, and the change and receipt come back in the tray rather than into your hand.

This is not a hygiene measure or a distancing device. It exists because it makes counting unambiguous and hand-to-hand fumbling unnecessary, and because placing rather than passing is the more courteous form.

Handing cash directly is not an offence and will be accepted, but the tray is right there and using it marks you as someone paying attention. Cards go in it too.

The change is counted, always

Change is counted out visibly, frequently twice, and often with the notes fanned so the count can be seen. Coins are placed separately. Receipts are given without being asked for.

This is not distrust of you. Cash handling accuracy is treated as a professional standard, and the visible count is part of doing the job properly.

Wait for it rather than sweeping the money up mid-count. It takes four seconds and walking away early interrupts something the cashier is required to complete.

Both hands, and why

Anything given or received formally — a business card, a gift, a document, money in a non-retail context — goes with both hands. It is the physical equivalent of giving something your full attention.

In everyday shop transactions one hand is fine because the tray is doing the work. In any situation with a person rather than a counter, two hands.

The same logic applies to receiving: take a bag, a card or a purchase with both hands where practical, and with a nod.

Cash, and how much of it to carry

Card acceptance has expanded a great deal, but Japan is still a country where cash resolves every situation and cards resolve most. Small restaurants, temples, markets, some buses and many independent shops remain cash only.

You will accumulate coins at a startling rate. The one-yen and five-yen coins are close to useless; the hundreds and five hundreds are valuable for lockers, vending machines and buses. Keep a coin purse rather than fighting a wallet.

Notes come out of ATMs in ten-thousand-yen denominations and nobody minds you breaking one for a small purchase. This surprises visitors from countries where paying for a coffee with a large note is provocative.

Splitting a bill, and paying for other people

Splitting evenly is the norm among friends and is called warikan. Itemised splitting is uncommon and asking a restaurant to divide a bill across several cards is often not possible, particularly in smaller places.

The practical method is that one person pays and the others transfer or hand over cash afterwards, which is why everyone carries some.

In a group with a clear senior person — a host, an older relative, a boss — that person may pay for everyone, and the polite response is a token protest followed by acceptance and clear thanks. Insisting on splitting can be the less courteous option.

Paying at the table is unusual. In most restaurants you take the slip left on your table to the register by the door and pay there on the way out.

Tipping, in one line

There is none. Not in restaurants, not in taxis, not for hotel staff. Leaving money will produce confusion and frequently someone chasing you down the street to return it.

The exception, and it is a narrow one, is the envelope of cash sometimes given to a ryokan attendant at a traditional inn, which is a different social act entirely and is not expected of visitors.

The ordering machines

Many ramen, curry and gyudon shops take payment before the food through a vending machine by the door: buttons for each dish, insert cash or tap a card, take the printed ticket to the counter.

The convention is that the top-left button is the shop's signature dish. Machines increasingly have English and photographs.

The system exists to remove the transaction from the meal entirely, which is why nobody brings you a bill and why leaving is simply a matter of standing up.

Queueing at the till, and bagging

Queues at a register form in a single line and are respected absolutely. If there are several registers, look for whether the queue is one line feeding all of them or a separate line per till; both arrangements exist and the floor markings will say which.

You bag your own purchases in supermarkets and convenience stores, at a separate counter beside the till rather than at the register itself. The cashier will put your items back into a basket and slide it along; take the basket to the packing bench.

Bags are charged for and are not given automatically, so say whether you want one when you reach the till.

In department stores the opposite applies: staff will wrap, bag and frequently walk the bag around the counter to hand it to you, and in some cases accompany you to the door.

Small things that make it smoother

Say the number of items rather than pointing repeatedly; the counting words are worth ten minutes of learning.

Have your IC card or your cash ready before you reach the front. Queues move quickly because everyone does this.

Take your receipt. You will need it for tax-free processing and for anything you might return.

And say gochisosama on the way out of a restaurant. It is not a payment ritual, but it is the sentence that ends the transaction properly.
