Vegetarian, Vegan, Halal and Allergies: Eating in Japan With Restrictions

The hard part is not finding restaurants. It is that dashi is in almost everything savoury, and 'no meat' does not communicate that to anyone.

Start with dashi, because everything else follows from it

The single most important fact for anyone avoiding animal products in Japan is dashi. It is the stock underneath an enormous proportion of savoury Japanese cooking, and the standard version is made with katsuobushi — dried, smoked, fermented bonito — usually alongside kombu seaweed.

That means miso soup is generally not vegetarian. Nor is the broth in most udon and soba, the simmering liquid for vegetables, the dipping sauce for tempura, most curry roux, tamagoyaki, or a great deal of what looks like a plain vegetable dish. Fish is not thought of as 'meat' in this context, and a dish can be entirely free of visible animal products and still be built on fish stock.

There is a purely plant-based version, kombu dashi, made from kelp alone, and shiitake dashi from dried mushrooms. Restaurants that cater to vegetarians use them. Restaurants that do not are not deceiving you — they simply do not consider stock to be an ingredient in the sense you mean.

Say what you cannot eat, not what you are

Announcing that you are vegetarian is much less effective than listing what must not be in the food. The category does not map cleanly onto Japanese culinary thinking, and a well-meaning kitchen may remove the pork and leave the broth.

The useful formulation names the specific things: no meat, no fish, no fish stock, no bonito flakes. In Japanese, the phrase for fish stock is katsuo dashi, and being able to point at those words is worth more than any general statement.

Carry it written down. A card in Japanese listing your restrictions, ideally with the specific ingredients named, is the single highest-value preparation for this trip. Staff can read it, take it to the kitchen, and give you an honest answer — which is frequently 'we cannot do that here', and that honest no is far more useful than an accommodating yes that was not understood.

Where it is easy

Shojin ryori, the Buddhist temple cuisine, is entirely plant-based by doctrine and is the oldest vegan cooking tradition in the country. It is served at temple lodgings, notably on Koyasan, and at specialist restaurants in Kyoto. It is also genuinely excellent rather than a substitute for something else.

Dedicated vegetarian and vegan restaurants exist in reasonable numbers in Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka, and the listings apps that cover them are reliable. This is the low-effort route in the big cities.

Indian restaurants are widespread, staffed by people who understand the requirement immediately, and are a dependable fallback anywhere.

Convenience stores are better than they sound. Onigiri with pickled plum or kombu, plain rice, salted edamame, fruit, nuts, plain bread. Read the ingredient list, which is comprehensive by law, though it is in Japanese.

And soba shops sometimes have a plain zaru soba where the noodles come cold with a dipping sauce — but the sauce is dashi-based, so the noodles alone with soy is the workable order.

Halal

Pork is common in Japanese cooking and is often present where it is not announced — in ramen broth, in gyoza filling, in the stock for many dishes. Alcohol is also a routine ingredient: sake and mirin are in a great many sauces and simmered dishes, which matters for a strict interpretation.

Certified halal restaurants exist in the major cities and near the large mosques, and their numbers have grown considerably with visitor numbers. Tokyo, Osaka and Kyoto all have a workable selection, and some universities and larger shopping centres have halal options.

Seafood is the practical everyday answer: sushi, sashimi and grilled fish avoid both problems, and are available everywhere. Vegetarian dishes carry the dashi caveat but not the pork or alcohol one.

Prayer rooms have become more common at airports, major stations and large shopping centres.

Allergies, which are a different problem

This is the one area where improvisation is genuinely unsafe, and it should be treated with more care than dietary preference.

Japanese labelling law requires the declaration of a specific set of allergens on packaged food, which makes supermarket and convenience store shopping tractable if you can read the terms. Learn the characters for your allergen, or photograph them to compare.

Restaurants are harder. Small kitchens often cannot guarantee against cross-contamination and, to their credit, will say so rather than take the risk. Chains are frequently better than independent restaurants here, because many publish full allergen charts.

Soy, wheat and buckwheat deserve particular attention. Soy is pervasive — soy sauce is in nearly everything. Buckwheat allergy is taken very seriously in Japan precisely because soba is so common, and a soba restaurant will have flour in the air.

Carry a written card, carry your own medication, and know how to say that this is an allergy rather than a preference — the distinction exists in Japanese and changes how the request is treated.

Ordering, practically

Restaurants with plastic food displays or photographic menus let you point, which removes a layer of difficulty, though it tells you nothing about the stock.

Ask before you sit down rather than after. Walking out of a small restaurant after being seated is awkward for everyone; asking at the door is completely normal and the answer is quick.

Department store food halls let you inspect and ask about individual items and are a good option for assembling a meal.

Self-catering solves the hardest days. An apartment with a kitchen, or even a hotel room and a supermarket, removes the problem entirely for a day when you are tired of negotiating it.

A realistic expectation

Japan is not a difficult country to eat in with restrictions, but it is a country where the restriction has to be communicated precisely rather than approximately. The food culture is not built around accommodation and substitution the way some others are, and a kitchen that cannot make your dish will usually say so plainly rather than improvise.

Prepare the written card, learn the four or five words that matter, use the temple cuisine and the dedicated restaurants when you want to stop thinking about it, and treat the convenience store as the safety net it is. On those terms it works, and the food is worth the effort.
