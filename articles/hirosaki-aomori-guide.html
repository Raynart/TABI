Aomori and Hirosaki: Late Blossom, Big Festivals, Deep Snow

At the top of Honshu, the cherry trees flower when Kyoto has finished, the summer festival is the loudest in Japan, and the winter snow is measured in metres. This is the region that rewards going at the wrong time of year.

The far north of the main island

Aomori prefecture is the northern tip of Honshu, facing Hokkaido across a strait. It is one of the least visited parts of Japan by international travellers and one of the most distinctive: heavy snow, a strong dialect that other Japanese people struggle with, apple orchards, and a cluster of festivals that are genuinely among the country's best.

The Tohoku Shinkansen reaches Shin-Aomori in about three hours from Tokyo. Hirosaki, the more attractive of the two main towns, is about half an hour further by local train.

Hirosaki Castle and the late blossom

Hirosaki has one of the twelve original castle keeps left in Japan — a small three-storey building rather than a grand one, but genuine seventeenth-century construction rather than a concrete reconstruction.

What it is really famous for is the cherry blossom. The castle park has more than two thousand trees, and by broad agreement it is one of the finest hanami sites in the country. The trees are pruned using techniques borrowed from apple cultivation, which produces unusually dense flowering.

The timing is the point. Hirosaki blooms at the end of April and into early May, weeks after Tokyo and Kyoto have finished. For anyone whose travel dates missed the blossom in the south, this is the answer, and it coincides with Golden Week, which means booking early.

The image people come for is the moat covered so thickly in fallen petals that the water is not visible — hanaikada, the flower raft. It happens at the end of the bloom, which is a good argument for arriving slightly late rather than early.

Nebuta, and the festivals of early August

Nebuta Matsuri in Aomori city, in the first week of August, is one of the great festivals of Japan. Enormous illuminated paper floats depicting warriors and mythological figures are paraded through the streets at night, accompanied by drums, flutes and thousands of costumed dancers called haneto.

The dancers are the part that surprises people: anyone can join, provided you wear the correct costume, which can be rented in the city. This is not a spectator arrangement grudgingly extended to visitors — participation is the point.

The Nebuta Museum Wa Rasse by the station displays several of the full-size floats year-round with the construction techniques explained, which is the next best thing outside festival week.

Hirosaki holds its own Neputa festival at the same time, with fan-shaped floats rather than three-dimensional ones, and Goshogawara has the Tachineputa, which are enormous vertical floats over twenty metres tall. All three happen in the same week, which is the single best week of the year to be in this prefecture and the hardest one to find a room in.

Snow, in quantities that are hard to picture

This region gets some of the heaviest snowfall of any inhabited place on earth. Cold air crossing the Sea of Japan picks up moisture and dumps it on the mountains of the western coast, and towns in the interior routinely deal with several metres.

Hakkoda, south of Aomori city, has a ropeway and some of the best backcountry snow in Japan, along with the frost-covered trees the region calls snow monsters. Sukayu Onsen nearby has a famous enormous mixed-bathing cedar bath and is buried for months.

Winter here is a real proposition rather than a scenic one: trains stop, roads close, and the useful skill is flexibility. It is also spectacular, and largely empty of visitors.

Jomon, and something much older

The Sannai-Maruyama site outside Aomori city is the remains of a large settlement occupied for well over a thousand years during the Jomon period, from roughly five and a half thousand years ago. Reconstructed pit dwellings and an enormous six-pillared timber structure stand on the site, with a museum of what was excavated.

It is part of a UNESCO listing covering the Jomon sites of northern Japan, and it reframes the region entirely: this was a densely populated, settled, sophisticated place while much of the rest of the archipelago was not.

The Aomori Museum of Art next door holds Nara Yoshitomo's enormous Aomori-ken dog and the Shiko Munakata woodblock prints, and the two together make a full day.

Apples, scallops and the food

Aomori grows the majority of Japan's apples, and the apple products — juice, pie, cider, dried fruit — are everywhere and genuinely good. The orchards around Hirosaki in blossom in May are a quieter pleasure than the castle.

Mutsu Bay scallops are the seafood speciality, along with the tuna landed at Oma at the northern tip, which fetches the highest prices at the Tokyo auctions.

Kenoshiru is the traditional dish: a vegetable soup of chopped roots and mountain vegetables, eaten in winter. Ichigoni, a sea urchin and abalone soup from the eastern coast, is the luxurious one.

How to fit it into a trip

Three hours from Tokyo means this is not a day trip. Two or three nights, either as a destination in itself or as the northern end of a run up through Sendai and Morioka, is the sensible shape.

The obvious pairing is with Hokkaido: the Shinkansen continues under the strait to Hakodate, so Aomori sits naturally between Tohoku and the north island rather than at a dead end.

Go for the first week of August or the end of April if the festivals or the blossom are the draw, and accept that both require booking months out. Go in February if empty and extraordinary matters more than convenient.
