Matsuyama and Dogo Onsen: Japan's Oldest Hot Spring

A bathhouse mentioned in eighth-century chronicles, a wooden building from 1894 that reopened in 2024 after five and a half years of restoration, and a castle on a hill above both.

The oldest one

Dogo Onsen has a serious claim to being the oldest hot spring in continuous use in Japan. It appears in the Nihon Shoki, the eighth-century chronicle, and in poetry older than that, with legends of injured animals and ancient emperors bathing here.

Whatever the precise history, the important thing is that it is not a resort built around a spring. It is a bathing district in a working city, with a public bathhouse at its centre that local people use, and a tram running to it from town.

Matsuyama is the largest city in Shikoku and is easy to reach — a ferry across the Inland Sea from Hiroshima, a limited express across the island, or a direct flight.

The Honkan, and its restoration

The Dogo Onsen Honkan is a three-storey wooden bathhouse built in 1894, topped with a small watchtower and drum, and designated an Important Cultural Property. It is a genuinely extraordinary building: a warren of staircases, tatami resting rooms, and bathing halls in stone.

It underwent major seismic and preservation work from 2019, staying partially open throughout with sections closed in rotation, and fully reopened in July 2024. The whole building, including the upper resting rooms, is now in use again.

The bathing options are tiered: a simple bath in the Kami-no-Yu, or a package including a tatami room, a yukata and tea afterwards, which is the version worth paying for. The upstairs rooms are the point of the building — bathing, then sitting in a robe drinking tea by an open window above the street, is the experience it was designed around.

It is often said to have inspired the bathhouse in Spirited Away. Studio Ghibli has never confirmed this and it is best treated as an appealing rumour rather than a fact.

The other baths

The Honkan is not the only option and is frequently the busiest. Tsubaki-no-Yu nearby is a larger, plainer, modern public bath used mostly by residents and is considerably cheaper and quieter.

Asuka-no-Yu, opened more recently, is built in the style of the Asuka period with craftwork from Ehime prefecture throughout, and offers private rooms. It is the most comfortable of the three and the least atmospheric.

There is also a free foot bath by the tram terminus and a hot spring drinking fountain, and most of the ryokan in the district have their own baths fed from the same source.

Standard onsen etiquette applies throughout: wash thoroughly before entering, no swimwear, no towel in the water.

Matsuyama Castle

Matsuyama Castle is one of the twelve surviving original keeps, on a steep hill in the middle of the city with views over the whole plain and out to the Inland Sea.

A ropeway and chairlift run up, or there are paths for anyone who wants the climb. The complex at the top is unusually complete — not just the keep but gates, turrets and walls — which makes it one of the better castles in Japan for understanding how the defences were meant to work.

The keep interior is original wood with steep stairs, and the exhibits are modest, which is a relief. The building is the exhibit.

Ninomaru, the garden below on the site of the lord's residence, is laid out to show the footprint of the vanished buildings, which is an elegant solution to the problem of a site with nothing left on it.

Botchan, and the literary town

Matsuyama has a strong literary identity. Natsume Soseki taught here briefly and turned the experience into Botchan, one of the most widely read novels in Japan, which is affectionately rude about the town and which the town has cheerfully embraced. A steam locomotive replica called the Botchan Train runs on the tram network.

Masaoka Shiki, the poet who reshaped haiku at the end of the nineteenth century, was born here, and there is a museum devoted to him. Post boxes around the city are shaped for submitting haiku, and the competitions attached are taken seriously.

The combination of a bathhouse, a castle and two major writers is a lot of identity for a city of this size, and it carries it lightly.

Around the city

Ishite-ji, temple fifty-one on the eighty-eight temple pilgrimage, is a short walk from Dogo and is one of the most atmospheric on the circuit — a national treasure gate, a cave passage lined with statues, and a slightly eccentric collection of later additions.

The Shimanami Kaido, the cycling route across a chain of islands and suspension bridges between Shikoku and Honshu, starts at Imabari about an hour away and is among the best cycle routes in Asia. Bikes can be hired and dropped at either end.

The Inland Sea ferries from Matsuyama to Hiroshima are a pleasant alternative to the train and take a couple of hours.

Where to stay and how long

Staying in the Dogo district rather than by the station is the better choice: the ryokan there have their own hot spring baths, and the district is at its best in the evening when the day visitors have gone and people walk between the baths in yukata and wooden sandals.

Two nights covers the baths, the castle and Ishite-ji comfortably. Add nights if the Shimanami Kaido or the pilgrimage is part of the plan.

It also works well as one end of a Shikoku loop, paired with Takamatsu and the art islands on the other coast.
