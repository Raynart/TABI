How to Eat at an Izakaya Without Looking Completely Lost

The curtain over the door. The laminated menu with pictures on maybe two pages. The small charge you didn't order. You'll be fine.

What an izakaya is

An izakaya is a Japanese pub — not a restaurant, not a bar, but what happens when the two are combined without either winning. You drink and eat in roughly equal measure, the food comes in small plates meant to be shared, and nobody orders a main course.

The lighting is low, the room is loud, and you will probably be sharing it with people who have loosened their ties, which is always a good sign.

There are three broad kinds. Chains, which are cheap, reliable, have picture menus and are a completely reasonable first visit. Independents, which are where the good food is. And tachinomi, standing bars, which are tiny, cheap, fast and the most fun of the three once you have the confidence.

Getting in and sitting down

Look for the noren, the short split curtain over the door — if it is hanging, the place is open, and it is taken in at closing. Push through it and someone will shout irasshaimase, which requires no reply.

You will be asked how many people. Holding up fingers works; 'futari desu' is two, 'sannin desu' is three. You may also be asked whether you want a table or the counter — the counter is better on your own or as a pair, and it is where you can see what other people are eating.

Popular independents fill up by seven and many do not take reservations for small groups. Going early, at half past five, is the reliable move and is when locals go anyway.

Otoshi: the charge you did not order

A small charge appears on the bill without explanation. It is called otoshi, it runs ¥200–600 a head, and in return a small dish arrives that you did not order — pickled vegetables, a little salad, sometimes something genuinely excellent.

It is a seating and cover charge, it is standard almost everywhere, it is not a scam, and it is not optional. Sending the dish back does not remove the charge.

Knowing this in advance removes the single most common moment of suspicion foreign visitors have about Japanese restaurants. The bill is correct.

The opening round

The convention is that everyone orders a drink first, together, and food follows. 'Toriaezu nama' — a draught beer for now — is close to a national catchphrase and is the correct thing to say while you look at the menu.

Then two or three small dishes to start. Edamame, karaage and potato salad are almost universally available and almost universally good. They are not exciting. They are correct. Order them while you work out the rest.

Order in waves rather than all at once. Dishes arrive as they are cooked, in no particular order, and a table that orders everything at the start gets everything at the start.

What to order after that

Yakitori, grilled chicken on skewers — order a mixed set, and specify tare for the sweet soy glaze or shio for salt. Dashimaki tamago, a rolled omelette made with stock, soft and savoury. Sashimi, if the place looks like it takes fish seriously, which you can usually tell from whether the locals at the counter are eating it.

Beyond that: agedashi tofu, grilled fish, motsuni for the adventurous, and whatever is on the handwritten paper stuck to the wall, which is the day's special and the best thing on offer.

'Osusume wa nan desu ka' — what do you recommend — solves any menu. Someone will point at something and whatever they point at will be good.

Shochu, chuhai and nomihodai

Shochu is probably what is in your cocktail. Distilled most often from sweet potato or barley, served over ice, with hot or cold water, or mixed with soda. It is gentler than whisky and goes with everything on the menu.

Chuhai is shochu with fruit soda — the izakaya equivalent of a spritz, and perfectly respectable. A highball is whisky and soda and is what a great many people drink all evening.

Nomihodai is all-you-can-drink for a fixed period, usually ninety or a hundred and twenty minutes, often bundled with a set food course. It is good value with a group and it is not a challenge; the time limit exists so the table turns over, and last orders are announced ten minutes before the end.

Paying and leaving

To get the bill, catch someone's eye and trace a rectangle in the air with your index fingers, or say 'o-kaikei onegaishimasu'. In many places you take the slip from your table to the register by the door and pay there rather than at the table.

Groups usually split evenly rather than itemising — this is warikan, and trying to calculate individual shares goes against the spirit of it. Cash is safest; plenty of independents still do not take cards.

There is no tipping. Say gochisousama deshita on the way out.

The golden rule

The more local and less translated the menu, the better the food. The izakaya in a residential neighbourhood near a station that is not on any tourist map will beat the one with an English sign near the temples every time.

Your hotel is on the tourist circuit. The izakaya should not be. Walk fifteen minutes in a direction with no sights in it and look for a lit noren and a bit of noise.

And if a small place is full and the owner waves you away, that is not rudeness — a six-seat counter with a queue cannot seat you, and there will be another one round the corner.
