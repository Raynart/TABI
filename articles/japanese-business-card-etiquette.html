Meishi: The Japanese Business Card Ritual and Why It Matters

In Japan, the business card is not a piece of paper. It is a physical representation of the person. Treating it as stationary is the fastest way to start a professional relationship poorly.

What a business card represents

In Japan, the meishi (business card) exchange is a ritual that establishes who you are, your position, and your company — in one gesture, at the start of every professional interaction. Cards are not handed out casually. They are exchanged at the beginning of a meeting with a formality that can feel significant to those not used to it. Understanding why makes the ritual easier: the card is standing in for a formal introduction that would otherwise take several minutes of verbal exchange.

The card also settles the question that Japanese professional interaction cannot proceed without: relative seniority. Japanese has different registers of politeness depending on who outranks whom, and you cannot construct a normal sentence until you know. The card tells everyone in the room how to speak to everyone else, which is why it is exchanged before the conversation rather than after it.

Note the order of information printed on it. Company first, then division, then title, then name. That sequence is not a design choice — it is the order of importance, and it is the opposite of the Western convention. You are being introduced as a representative of an organisation who happens to be a particular person.

Before the meeting: what to have ready

Bring more cards than you think you need. A single meeting with a mid-sized company can involve six people, and running out halfway around the table is the failure mode you are trying to avoid. Twenty in the case for a day of meetings is not excessive.

Carry them in a meishi holder — a slim leather or metal card case — not loose in a wallet or a jacket pocket. The case matters for a practical reason as well as an aesthetic one: cards produced from it are flat, clean and unbent, which is the entire point. Cases are sold in any department store and in most stationery shops.

Have the card printed with Japanese on one side and English on the other. Any print shop in Japan will do this in a day or two, and several online services will do it before you travel. If you cannot manage a translated side, an English-only card is acceptable; a card with a Japanese side you had machine-translated and never checked is worse than no Japanese side at all.

How to present your card

Hold the card with both hands, text facing the recipient so they can read it immediately, and offer a slight bow as you present it. Say your name and company as you do. If you are handing out cards in a group, the most senior person goes first. Do not hand out cards across a table — walk around or hand directly.

Hold it by the corners or the lower edge. Covering the company name or your own name with your thumbs is the small error people make most often — you are presenting the information, so leave it visible.

Height carries meaning. The more junior party offers their card slightly lower than the more senior one. You are not expected to calibrate this precisely as a visitor, but if you are clearly the one asking for the meeting, presenting a little low reads as courteous and costs you nothing.

Very often both people present at the same time. The convention is to offer with the right hand while receiving with the left, then immediately bring the second hand to the received card. It feels awkward the first time and stops feeling awkward around the fourth.

How to receive a card

Receive the card with both hands. Take a moment to read it — not a cursory glance, but actually look at it. Acknowledge the person's name and company.

Say something. Reading the name aloud to confirm the pronunciation is welcome, because Japanese surnames are written in characters with multiple possible readings and even Japanese colleagues ask. A remark about the office location or the division is enough. What you are demonstrating is that you read the card rather than pocketed it.

The cards stay on the table

If you are sitting down at a meeting, place the cards on the table in front of you, arranged to mirror where people are sitting, with the most senior person's card at the top of your arrangement. Keep them there for the duration.

This is not ceremony for its own sake. It is a seating chart. Six unfamiliar names in a language you do not read are impossible to hold in your head, and everyone in the room is using the same trick. Referring down to the cards during the meeting to get someone's name right is expected, not a lapse.

Put them away only once the meeting has finished and people are standing up, and put them into the case rather than a pocket. The card on the table means the person is still present in the meeting, literally and symbolically.

What not to do

Do not write on a business card in front of the person who gave it to you. Do not fold it. Do not put it in your back pocket and sit on it. Do not place anything on top of it (not a cup, not another card). These actions read as treating the card — and by extension the person — carelessly.

Do not slide a card across a table, do not deal them out like playing cards to several people at once, and do not hand one over with a drink in your other hand. If you need to make a note about someone, make it afterwards and out of sight.

After the meeting, file cards appropriately. Japanese professionals use meishi holders and binders specifically for this purpose, and many will genuinely still have your card two years later.

Reading the titles

A card is only useful if you can decode it, and Japanese corporate titles map onto a fairly consistent ladder. From the top: shacho (president), senmu and jomu (executive directors), bucho (department head), kacho (section head), kakaricho (subsection head), and shain (staff, usually with no title printed at all).

The one worth recognising on sight is bucho. A department head is typically the most senior person who will attend a working meeting, and is usually the person whose agreement actually decides the outcome. Kacho is the level that does most of the real coordination.

Also look for a name in katakana or romaji alongside the kanji. If it is there, the person has put it there so foreigners can read it, and using that reading is what it is for.

If you don't have cards

Arriving at a Japanese business meeting without cards is not a catastrophe, but it is noticed. A brief explanation — 'I apologize, I have run out of cards' — is appropriate. Most Japanese businesspeople will accept this gracefully.

Receiving a card when you have none to give creates a small debt, and the graceful way to settle it is to follow up by email the same day with your full details. If you are in Japan for a week and expect more meetings, get cards printed while you are there; same-day services exist in every business district.

Where the ritual is relaxing

The formality is not uniform. Startups, creative agencies, foreign-owned firms and younger teams handle meishi far more loosely than a bank or a manufacturer will, and in some rooms the exchange is barely a pause. Digital card apps — Eight and Sansan are the common ones — are now normal, and being asked to scan rather than swap is not a snub.

The safe approach is to arrive prepared for the full version and follow the room. Nobody has ever been thought worse of for handling a card carefully. The reverse is not true.

It is also worth keeping the ritual in proportion. Getting it slightly wrong will not end a negotiation, and your Japanese counterparts know you are a visitor. What the effort communicates is that you looked into how things are done before you arrived — which is, in the end, the same thing the ritual itself communicates.
