Fukuoka: The Japanese City That Gets Everything Right

An international airport eleven minutes from the city centre, the best ramen in the country, and street food stalls along the river. Fukuoka is consistently rated the most liveable city in Japan and consistently skipped by visitors.

The airport is the first thing you notice

Fukuoka Airport is roughly eleven minutes by subway from Hakata Station and about fifteen from Tenjin, the main shopping district. There is no other major Japanese city where the airport is effectively a subway stop, and it changes the shape of a trip completely: you land, you take a train that is already going where you want to go, and you are in the centre before you would have finished the queue for the Narita Express.

This is the strongest practical argument for treating Fukuoka as an entry point to Japan rather than an add-on. Flights from elsewhere in Asia are frequent and cheap, and starting here and working east is a more interesting route than the standard Tokyo-first itinerary.

The city itself has two centres. Hakata is the station side, older and more workmanlike. Tenjin is the shopping and nightlife side. They are about ten minutes apart by subway and the walk between them, along the Naka river, is one of the pleasant things to do here.

Yatai, which exist nowhere else like this

Yatai are the open-air food stalls that set up on the pavements in the evening — a counter, a handful of stools, a tarpaulin, and a cook working in a space smaller than most kitchens. Fukuoka has by far the largest concentration left in Japan; other cities have effectively regulated them out of existence.

The main cluster is along Nakasu, the island in the river, with more scattered around Tenjin and Nagahama. They open in the early evening and run late. You sit down beside strangers, order a few things, drink a beer, and move on or stay.

The etiquette is simple and worth knowing. Space is limited, so a long occupation of a stool while nursing one drink is poor form. Order food as well as drink. Cash is often the only option. And the ones with a queue of local office workers are a better bet than the ones with an English menu on a board.

What to order varies by stall: ramen, oden, yakitori, motsunabe, gyoza. The gyoza here are small, thin-skinned and served by the dozen, which is a distinct local style.

Hakata ramen is the local religion

Tonkotsu — the milky, collagen-heavy pork bone broth — was invented in this region, and Fukuoka is where it is taken most seriously. The noodles are thin, straight and served firm, and the standard portion is deliberately small so it can be eaten before the noodles soften.

That is what kaedama is for: you finish the noodles, call for a refill, and a second portion arrives to go into the broth you still have. Ordering it is expected rather than greedy, and shops have a system for it — often a button on the counter or a slip of paper.

Shops around Hakata Station and Nagahama are the traditional heartland. A bowl is inexpensive, most shops seat you at a counter and some have the individual booths that let you eat without interacting with anyone at all, which is a considered piece of design rather than an oddity.

Beyond food

Ohori Park is a large pond with a walking path around it, built from the moat of the old castle, and it is where the city goes in the evening. The adjacent Maizuru Park has the castle ruins — stone walls rather than a keep — and is one of the better cherry blossom sites in Kyushu.

Kushida Shrine is the spiritual home of the Hakata Gion Yamakasa festival and has one of the enormous festival floats on display year-round, which is worth seeing even outside July.

Canal City is a large shopping complex built around an artificial canal, which sounds unpromising and is actually a landmark of 1990s architecture worth walking through.

And the Kyushu National Museum, in Dazaifu, is one of only a handful of national museums in the country and takes a regional rather than a national view of Japanese history.

Dazaifu, half an hour away

Dazaifu Tenmangu is dedicated to Sugawara no Michizane, the scholar deified as the god of learning, which makes it the shrine students visit before examinations. The approach is lined with shops selling umegae mochi, a grilled rice cake with a plum blossom stamp, eaten hot.

The shrine has been undergoing major renovation of its main hall, during which a striking temporary structure with a garden growing on its roof has stood in front of it. It is worth checking the current state before going, since the thing you see depends on where the work has reached.

The train from Tenjin takes about half an hour on the Nishitetsu line, and the museum, the shrine and lunch make a comfortable half day.

Using Fukuoka as a base

The city sits at the top of Kyushu and everything else on the island is reachable from it. Beppu and its steaming hot springs are a couple of hours east. Nagasaki is a couple of hours south-west. Kumamoto and the enormous caldera at Aso are directly south. Yufuin, Takachiho and the Kunisaki peninsula are all day trips or overnight trips.

The Kyushu Shinkansen runs south towards Kagoshima, and JR Kyushu sells its own regional passes which are far better value than the national pass for a trip that stays on the island.

For a first visit to Japan that wants to avoid the crowded Golden Route entirely, a week based here with two or three overnight trips is a genuinely strong itinerary.

Why so few visitors come

There is no mystery to it. Fukuoka has no single overwhelming sight — no Fushimi Inari, no Itsukushima torii, no castle on every postcard — and international itineraries are built around those. What it has instead is a city that works: compact, well fed, friendly in a way that people who live in Tokyo comment on, and cheap by Japanese standards.

That is a harder thing to put in a photograph and a better thing to spend three days in.
