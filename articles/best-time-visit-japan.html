The Best Time to Visit Japan: An Honest Month-by-Month Guide

Spring cherry blossoms and autumn foliage are spectacular. They are also the two most crowded periods of the year. Here is what every month actually offers.

January and February: crowds at minimum

January is Japan's quietest month for tourism. After New Year, which is busy with domestic travel until around the 3rd, the country empties. Hotels are cheaper, Shinkansen seats are available, and famous sites are accessible at human scale. Tokyo and Kyoto sit around 4–10°C, cold but dry and mostly clear — Japan's winter is much sunnier than northern Europe's.

Onsen towns are at their best, because sitting in outdoor hot water is a different proposition when the air is near freezing. Hakone, Kinosaki and Beppu all reward a winter visit in a way they do not in July. February adds Sapporo's Snow Festival in the first week and the best of the ski season.

The trade-offs are real: short days, some mountain routes closed, and Kyoto gardens at their barest. If what you want is the country without the crowd premium, this is when to come.

March and April: cherry blossom

Sakura season typically runs from late March to mid-April depending on location and year. Tokyo usually peaks in the last days of March, Kyoto about a week later, and northern Japan and higher elevations into late April and beyond. The flowers last roughly a week to ten days at any one place, less if it rains hard.

This is the most visited season, the most expensive, and the most beautiful. Book accommodation four to six months ahead. The famous viewpoints are genuinely crowded; the less-visited spots — riverside embankments, canal paths, ordinary neighbourhood parks — offer the same flowers with a fraction of the people, and there are cherry trees on practically every street.

The forecast is published from January and revised weekly, and it is the thing to watch. Be aware that a trip booked to hit peak bloom on a fixed date is a gamble: the front moves by up to a week in either direction, and the only real hedge is being willing to travel north or uphill to catch it.

May: the sweet spot

Late May, after Golden Week has cleared, is one of the best times to visit Japan and it is consistently underrated. Temperatures sit at 15–22°C across central Japan, the humidity has not arrived, and the rates have dropped back to normal.

The specific pleasure of this month is the new green. Shinryoku — the fresh leaf on the maples before summer darkens it — is a season Japanese people actively travel for and almost no foreign visitor has heard of. The same Kyoto gardens that are impassable in November are quiet and luminous in the third week of May.

Roughly May 10 to 30 is the window. It is the answer for a first visit with no fixed seasonal target.

June to August: rain, then heat

June brings the rainy season to most of Honshu — several weeks of grey skies and intermittent rain from early June. It is frustrating for sightseeing and it is also the cheapest and quietest period outside winter, and hydrangea season, which is genuinely lovely in temple gardens.

July and August are hot and humid in a way that surprises people: 30–36°C in Tokyo and Osaka with humidity above 80%, and nights that do not cool down. This is not merely uncomfortable, it is a health consideration — heatstroke cases spike every summer, and walking a full sightseeing day in August is a bad plan.

The compensations are the summer festivals, which are spectacular and are what the season is for, and the fireworks displays. If you must come in summer: go to Hokkaido, which is cool and green and dry; or restructure the day around early mornings, air-conditioned afternoons and evenings outside.

September to November: typhoons, then the best weather

September carries the highest typhoon risk of the year, mostly affecting Kyushu, Shikoku and Okinawa but capable of shutting down trains and flights nationally for a day or two. It is worth travel insurance and a flexible day in the itinerary rather than a reason to avoid the month; the heat breaks and late September is pleasant.

October is arguably the best weather in the Japanese year: clear, dry, 15–25°C, minimal rain. It is the month to come if the weather itself is what matters and you do not need a specific natural event.

November brings the autumn colour, peaking across central Honshu in the middle of the month, and with it the second great crowd of the year. Kyoto in the third week of November requires the same planning discipline as cherry blossom season. Late November in Tokyo, by contrast, is beautiful and manageable.

The weeks to avoid regardless of season

Three periods are about domestic travel rather than weather, and all three are worth planning around. Golden Week, from April 29 to May 5, when the whole country travels at once. Obon in mid-August, when it does the same in worse heat. And New Year, December 29 to January 3, when the issue is not crowds but closure — restaurants, museums and small businesses simply shut.

None of the three is unmanageable if you are already committed. All three will cost you more, book out earlier and move slower than the weeks either side of them.

The country is longer than you think

Japan spans about three thousand kilometres from north to south, and national seasonal advice hides a lot. Okinawa is subtropical: cherry blossom in January, swimmable sea from April, and a summer that is hot but less oppressive than Tokyo's because there is wind.

Hokkaido runs on a different calendar entirely — a genuine Northern European summer in July and August, autumn colour in early October, and a winter cold enough to support the best powder skiing in the world.

Which means the honest answer to 'when should I visit Japan' is partly 'where in Japan'. August is a bad month for Kyoto and an excellent one for Hokkaido; February is bleak in Tohoku and pleasant in Okinawa.

What to pack, briefly

Winter: layers rather than one heavy coat, because buildings and trains are heated hard. Spring and autumn: a light jacket and something waterproof. Summer: the lightest clothing you own, a hand towel — which is what the small cloth everyone carries is for, since public lavatories often have no dryer — and sun protection.

Year-round: shoes you can walk fifteen kilometres in and remove easily, because you will be taking them off at temples, some restaurants and any traditional accommodation. This is the single most consequential packing decision for a Japan trip and most people get it wrong.

The recommendation

For a first visit with no fixed seasonal preference: late May or October. Both give you good weather, normal prices and manageable crowds, which is a combination the famous seasons cannot offer.

For a trip structured around a natural event: cherry blossom in late March to early April, autumn leaves in the first half of November, and book far ahead for either. For minimum crowds and maximum value: January, accepting the cold, or June, accepting the rain.

The worst time to book without planning is Golden Week or mid-August — not because they are bad, but because accommodation disappears months in advance and the prices reflect it.
