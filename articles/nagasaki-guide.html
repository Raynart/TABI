Nagasaki: The Port That Was Japan's Only Window on the World

For two centuries, when the country was closed, one artificial island in Nagasaki harbour was the entire point of contact with Europe. The city that grew from that is unlike anywhere else in Japan — and it carries the second atomic bombing.

Two hundred years of being the only door

From the 1630s Japan was effectively closed to foreigners for more than two centuries. The exception was Dejima, a small fan-shaped artificial island in Nagasaki harbour, where a Dutch trading post was permitted to operate under tight supervision. Everything Japan learned about European science, medicine and technology during that period came through this one address.

Dejima has been progressively reconstructed and is now a walk-through site of restored warehouses and residences, no longer an island since the harbour was filled in around it. It is a small place with an outsized significance, and the exhibits do a good job of conveying how strange and constrained the arrangement was for everyone involved.

The consequence is a city with a genuinely mixed inheritance: Chinese, Portuguese and Dutch influence layered into the food, the architecture and the festivals in a way that is not true of anywhere else in the country.

The Peace Park and the museum

On 9 August 1945 Nagasaki became the second city to be attacked with a nuclear weapon. The hypocentre is marked by a black monolith in a quiet park, with the Peace Park and its statue on the hill above and the Atomic Bomb Museum nearby.

The museum is smaller than Hiroshima's and structured differently, moving from the destruction of the city into the wider history of nuclear weapons. It is direct, unsparing and not a comfortable visit, which is the intention.

The one-legged torii — a shrine gate left standing with only half its structure — and the remains of Urakami Cathedral are within walking distance. Urakami matters particularly here: this was the centre of Japanese Catholicism, and the bomb detonated almost directly above the largest Christian community in the country.

The hidden Christians

Christianity arrived in Japan in the sixteenth century, took root most deeply around Nagasaki, and was then banned and violently suppressed for over two hundred years. Communities survived in secret on the surrounding islands, disguising their practice — statues of the Virgin carved to resemble Kannon, prayers preserved orally through generations without priests.

When the ban lifted in the nineteenth century, some of these communities revealed themselves at Oura Church in Nagasaki, an event that astonished the European missionaries who had assumed the faith extinguished. Oura is the oldest church in Japan and part of a UNESCO listing covering the hidden Christian sites of the region.

It is one of the more remarkable survival stories anywhere, and the sites on the Goto islands and the Shimabara peninsula are what the listing is really about, if you have the days for it.

Glover Garden and the hillside

Nagasaki is built on steep slopes around a harbour, which makes it a city of staircases and views. Glover Garden, on the hill above the harbour, preserves a group of Western-style residences from the nineteenth-century merchant community, including the oldest surviving Western building in Japan.

The connection to Madama Butterfly is heavily advertised and largely invented, but the houses themselves and the view over the harbour are genuinely worth the climb — and there are escalators up the hill, which the city is sensible about.

The night view from Mount Inasa, reached by ropeway, is the other set piece. It is frequently included on lists of the best night views in the world, which is the sort of claim that invites scepticism until you see the harbour and the hills lit up from above.

Gunkanjima, if the sea allows

Hashima — universally called Gunkanjima, battleship island, for its silhouette — is an abandoned coal-mining island a short boat ride offshore. It housed thousands of people in concrete apartment blocks until the mine closed in 1974, and it has been decaying since.

Tours land on a small permitted section and follow a fixed walkway. The rest is unsafe and off limits. Landings depend entirely on sea conditions and are cancelled often, so book early in your stay to leave room for a second attempt.

The island's history includes the use of forced Korean and Chinese labour during the war, which is a live matter of dispute around its UNESCO listing, and which the better tours address rather than skirt.

The food is its own cuisine

Champon is the dish to eat: a thick tangle of noodles in a rich pork-and-seafood broth with cabbage, squid, pork and whatever else, developed by a Chinese restaurateur in the city for students who needed feeding cheaply. Sara udon is its crisp-noodle sibling.

Castella, the sponge cake, arrived with the Portuguese in the sixteenth century and became a Nagasaki speciality. The good versions are dense, moist and sold in wooden boxes by shops that have been making it for generations.

Shippoku is the formal expression of the city's mixture — a shared multi-course meal combining Japanese, Chinese and European dishes on one table, served at a handful of traditional restaurants and worth booking for one dinner.

Getting there and how long to stay

The Nishi Kyushu Shinkansen runs to Nagasaki, though the route currently requires a transfer partway from Hakata, which is a quirk of an unfinished line rather than a hardship. Buses from Fukuoka are direct and inexpensive.

The city's trams are the best way to get around: cheap, frequent, easy to understand, and they reach almost everything a visitor wants.

Two nights is the right length. One day for the peace sites, which deserve a whole day and are emotionally heavy, and one for Dejima, the hillside and the harbour.
