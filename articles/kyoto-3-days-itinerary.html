Kyoto in Three Days: What to Actually See and What to Skip

Kyoto has over 1,600 temples and shrines. You will not see them all. Here is how to choose the ones that matter — and what makes them worth choosing over the others.

Day one: the east side

Start at Fushimi Inari. The torii gate path that runs up Mt. Inari is the most photographed image in Japan, and the photographs don't exaggerate it. The path continues for four kilometers past the main tourist zone, thinning to almost nothing by the second kilometer. Arrive at 6:30am or earlier and walk past the first major platform — the mountain path above it is genuinely quiet until 9am. In the afternoon: Kinkaku-ji (the Golden Pavilion). Arrive between 2pm and closing. It is always crowded; go anyway, because the building and its reflection in the pond are one of those things that maintains its impact despite the crowds. This is rarer than it sounds.

Day one: evening in Gion

After 5pm, walk through Gion. The most famous streets (Hanamikoji, Shimbashi) are genuinely beautiful in the evening light. In summer (July), the Gion Matsuri preparations fill the streets with yamahoko floats. In autumn, the maple trees along Shimbashi turn. Come to look, not to photograph — or at minimum, look before you photograph. The neighborhood has been fighting the worst effects of mass tourism for several years, and the experience of being there is different when you are present rather than performing.

Day two: the northwest

Arashiyama is the west side's major district, accessible by the Sagano Scenic Railway or the Randen tram. The bamboo grove is a ten-minute walk from the Arashiyama station and genuinely spectacular — and very crowded by 10am, nearly empty at 7am. After the grove: Tenryu-ji, one of Kyoto's great Zen gardens, with a pond framed by borrowed scenery (shakkei) of the mountains behind it. In autumn the maple trees around the pond are what the photographs in every Japan feature show. After Tenryu-ji, walk the Sagano path north to Jojakko-ji — a moss-covered temple on a hillside that appears in almost no itineraries and is about as far from the crowd as you can get while staying in Arashiyama.

Day two: Nishiki Market

Return to central Kyoto in the afternoon for Nishiki Market — a covered arcade called Kyoto's Kitchen, narrow, parallel to Shijo Street, with stalls selling pickled vegetables, grilled mochi, tofu, Kyoto-style dashi components, fresh yuba, and specialty food items. Eat as you walk (this is one of the exceptions to the no-eating-while-walking rule, since the market format makes stationary eating difficult). The market closes early — most stalls done by 6pm.

Day three: what most itineraries miss

Philosopher's Path (Tetsugaku no Michi): a two-kilometer canal-side walkway between Ginkaku-ji and Nanzen-ji in the northeastern hills. In cherry blossom season the canal is lined with sakura. At any time of year it is quieter than the central tourist circuit and lined with small cafés, tofu restaurants, and craft shops. Walk it slowly, south to north. At the northern end: Nanzen-ji, a Zen temple complex with an aqueduct running through the grounds — one of the odder visual combinations in Kyoto and one of the most memorable.

How to move around, which decides everything

Kyoto's buses go everywhere and are slow, packed and stuck in traffic during any busy season. The two subway lines are fast and cover very little. The trains — JR, Keihan, Hankyu, Eizan, Randen — are excellent along their own corridors and useless between them.

The answer most residents will give you is a bicycle. The city is flat, compact and on a grid, and almost everything in this itinerary is within a twenty-minute ride. Rentals are inexpensive and widely available; park only in marked bays, because Kyoto impounds bikes left on pavements.

Where you stay matters more here than in Tokyo, because crossing the city eats an hour. Staying near Karasuma or Kawaramachi puts you within reach of both halves. Staying at the station is convenient for arrival and inconvenient for everything else.

The season changes the plan

In cherry blossom season and in the second half of November, everything in this itinerary is two to three times busier, and the early-morning rule stops being an optimisation and becomes the only way it works. Aim to be at the first site at opening and finished with it by nine.

Both seasons add evening illuminations at several temples, which are ticketed, crowded and genuinely worth doing once — and which give you something to do in the hours when the daytime sites are unpleasant.

The rest of the year, and especially the fresh green of late May and the quiet of January, the same three days are a completely different and much gentler trip.

What three days cannot include

Three days buys you one side of the city per day and a little slack. It does not buy the northern mountains — Ohara, Kurama and Kibune each want most of a day — and it does not buy Uji, which is where the tea and the Byodo-in phoenix hall are.

It also does not buy Nara, which is forty-five minutes away and deserves its own day rather than an afternoon. Attempting Nara on day three of a Kyoto trip produces a rushed version of both.

If you have a fourth day, spend it north rather than adding more temples in the centre. If you have a fifth, spend it in Nara. The marginal value of a seventh temple in Higashiyama is close to zero.

What to skip

Nijo Castle: historically interesting, architecturally significant (the nightingale floor squeaks deliberately to detect intruders), and fairly dry as a visitor experience compared to everything else Kyoto offers. Come if you have four or more days. Toei Kyoto Studio Park (a movie set theme park): enthusiastically recommended by some and entirely optional for everyone who isn't specifically interested in period samurai dramas. Philosopher's Path during peak cherry blossom season: beautiful, but the crowd density removes the contemplative quality that is the entire point.
