What a Japan Trip Actually Costs, Line by Line

Japan has a reputation for being expensive that is thirty years out of date in some categories and entirely accurate in others. Here is where the money really goes.

The reputation is out of date, unevenly

Japan acquired its expensive reputation during the bubble years and has not fully shed it, but the country has had decades of very low inflation and a weak currency for much of the recent period. For a visitor earning in dollars, euros or pounds, several categories are now cheaper than at home.

Food is the clearest case: eating well is inexpensive in Japan in a way that surprises almost everyone. Transport is the opposite — intercity rail is genuinely expensive. Accommodation sits in the middle and is good value for what you get. Attractions are cheap.

So the honest answer to 'is Japan expensive' is that the flight and the long-distance trains are, and most of the rest is not.

Accommodation, the largest line

This is the biggest variable and the one most worth thinking about. A business hotel room in a major city is the baseline, and it buys a small, immaculate, well-located room. A capsule or a hostel dormitory is a fraction of that. A ryokan with dinner and breakfast is a multiple of it, and should be budgeted as accommodation plus two meals rather than compared directly.

Prices move sharply with the calendar. Cherry blossom season, the autumn foliage weeks, Golden Week and New Year all push rates up substantially and reduce availability, and the same room can differ by a factor of two or three between a Tuesday in February and a Saturday in early April.

Booking earlier helps more in Japan than in many countries, because supply in the good locations is genuinely limited rather than dynamically priced downward at the last minute.

Food, where Japan is cheap

A bowl of ramen, a gyudon, a set lunch at a neighbourhood restaurant, a convenience store breakfast — these are all inexpensive, and none of them are compromises. The set lunch in particular is the great value of Japanese eating: many restaurants serve at lunch, at a substantial discount, food close to what they serve at dinner.

Department store food halls in the last hour before closing discount prepared food heavily, which is a genuine strategy rather than a hardship one.

The expensive end exists and is very expensive — a counter sushi omakase or a high-end kaiseki will cost what it would cost anywhere. The point is that this is a choice you make a few times, not a floor you cannot get under. It is entirely possible to eat extremely well in Japan for very little, and this is where the country most confounds expectations.

Transport, where it is not

Intercity rail is the line that hurts. A single Shinkansen journey between major cities runs to a meaningful fraction of a day's total budget, and this is where a trip's costs concentrate.

The mitigations are: fewer, longer hops rather than many short ones; regional rail passes where your trip stays in one area; domestic low-cost flights for the longest legs, which are frequently cheaper than the train; and overnight buses, which are cheap, surprisingly comfortable and save a night's accommodation.

Within cities, transport is cheap. Subway and bus fares are modest, an IC card removes the friction, and most sightseeing days involve a few hundred yen of travel rather than a few thousand.

Taxis are expensive and should be treated as an occasional convenience rather than a mode of transport.

Attractions cost very little

Temple and shrine entry, castle admission, garden tickets and most museums are inexpensive by international standards — typically the price of a coffee, occasionally the price of a lunch. A day of dedicated sightseeing rarely accumulates a large total.

The expensive attractions are the branded ones: the theme parks, the observation decks, the ticketed digital art venues. These are priced like their equivalents anywhere, and they are optional.

Onsen day-use fees are trivial. A public bath costs less than a train ride.

The costs people forget

Luggage. Coin lockers daily, and courier fees if you send bags between cities, which is worth every yen but is a line item.

Connectivity. An eSIM or pocket wifi for the trip.

Cash withdrawal fees, which mount if you take out small amounts often. Take larger sums less frequently.

Coins. Not a cost, but a phenomenon: cash payment generates a startling volume of coins, and they are useful for lockers and vending machines rather than something to convert back.

And souvenirs, which in Japan are a genuine budget line rather than an afterthought — the shopping is good and the culture of bringing things back is strong.

How to build the number

Work it as: flights, plus accommodation per night times nights, plus a daily allowance for food and local transport and entry fees, plus a separate line for intercity travel, plus a contingency for the two or three things you will decide to do once you are there.

The daily allowance is the part that varies least and surprises people most — it is usually lower than they budgeted. The intercity line is the part that varies most and is usually higher.

Then decide where you want to spend deliberately. One extraordinary meal, or one night in a ryokan, will occupy a noticeable share of the total and will be what you remember. That is a good use of money; a larger hotel room in a city you are barely in is not.
