The Japanese Breakfast: What It Is and Why It Will Change Your Morning

A traditional Japanese breakfast is not a quick meal. It is a composed sequence of small things done well, and eating it once will make every hotel breakfast you eat for the rest of your life feel like a concession.

What a Japanese breakfast is

A traditional Japanese breakfast consists of steamed white rice, miso soup, a piece of grilled fish, a small selection of pickled vegetables, a square of tofu, a small egg dish, and nori. Everything arrives simultaneously. Nothing is sweet. Coffee, if it appears, comes afterwards.

The structure has a name — ichiju-sansai, one soup and three dishes — and it is the underlying grammar of Japanese meals generally rather than a breakfast-specific idea. Rice is the centre, the soup supports it, and the small dishes are there to make the rice interesting.

This is the standard morning meal at ryokan, traditional hotels and in the homes of people who still cook breakfast, though it is worth knowing that a great many Japanese people eat toast and coffee on a weekday like everyone else.

The grilled fish

The fish is the anchor. A properly grilled piece of salmon or mackerel — salted, left, then grilled slowly — has crisp skin, a slightly smoky interior, and a flavour that is to a hotel buffet as a proper espresso is to an office coffee machine.

At a ryokan it was grilled minutes before it arrived, sometimes on a small charcoal grill at your table. Eat it immediately and eat the skin, which is the best part and which visitors routinely leave.

The bones are left in and are removed at the table with chopsticks, working along the spine. This takes practice and nobody is judging the outcome.

Miso soup and the small dishes

Miso soup at a proper breakfast is made from dashi — kombu and dried bonito — with miso stirred in off the heat, not from powder. The difference is obvious. It arrives hot in a lacquer bowl with a lid; lift the lid, invert it beside the bowl, and drink directly from the bowl, using chopsticks for the solids.

The pickles are not a garnish. They are there to punctuate the rice, and eating a little between mouthfuls is the intended rhythm. Takuan, the bright yellow daikon, is the most common and the most divisive.

Tamagoyaki, the rolled omelette, is slightly sweet and served cold or warm. Onsen tamago — an egg cooked slowly in its shell until the white is barely set — is the other common version and is poured over rice.

Natto, and whether you have to

Fermented soybeans, brown, sticky, strong-smelling, and stringy in a way that has to be seen. It is the single most divisive item on a Japanese breakfast tray and it appears constantly, especially east of Nagoya.

The preparation matters: open the pack, add the sachets of mustard and soy-based sauce, and stir hard for twenty or thirty seconds until it goes pale and foamy. Unstirred natto is much harder going. Then tip it over rice, or eat it alongside.

You do not have to eat it and nobody expects you to like it. It is genuinely good for you, it is genuinely cheap, and about half of foreign visitors who try it properly stirred find it fine. The other half do not, and that is a completely normal outcome that Japanese people from Osaka will sympathise with.

How to eat it

Everything arrives at once and you move between the dishes rather than finishing one and starting the next. A mouthful of rice, a piece of fish, a sip of soup, a pickle, back to the rice. The rice is the constant and everything else is there to keep it interesting.

The rice bowl and the soup bowl are lifted; the plates stay on the table. Do not put anything on top of the rice unless it is meant to go there — natto, raw egg and the seaweed do, the fish generally does not.

The nori is for wrapping: take a sheet, dip a corner in soy sauce, pick up a little rice with it, eat. At a ryokan the nori arrives in a sealed packet to keep it crisp, and opening it at the last moment is the point.

Where to eat one

The best is at a ryokan, where it arrives in your room or a dedicated dining room at a fixed time and takes forty-five minutes to eat properly. It is frequently the meal people remember longest from a trip.

The second-best is a business hotel that has opted for a proper Japanese breakfast rather than a buffet, which is a common and underrated feature of chains like Dormy Inn. A teishoku shop — a set-meal restaurant — will serve the same thing to office workers for under ¥1,000, and some are open from six in the morning.

The other genuinely Japanese breakfast is the kissaten morning set: coffee with toast, a boiled egg and sometimes a small salad, thrown in free or nearly free with the price of the coffee before eleven. It is a different tradition entirely, it dates to the post-war cafés of Nagoya, and it is excellent.

The convenience store version — onigiri, a boiled egg, a carton of miso soup — is fast, cheap and perfectly decent. It is not the same experience and it is what most of Tokyo actually eats.

What it teaches you

Eating one properly, without hurrying, resets certain assumptions about what mornings can be. The meal is light enough that you feel alert afterwards rather than heavy. The flavours are savoury and complex at seven in the morning in a way that feels strange until it feels obvious.

The effort is visible in every dish, and none of the dishes is large. That is the argument the whole meal is making: several small things done carefully beat one big thing done quickly.

Arrive at the dining room at the time you were given, sit down properly, and give it the three quarters of an hour it is asking for.
