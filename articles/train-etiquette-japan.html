The Unwritten Rules of Japanese Trains — Read This Before You Board

Japan’s trains run on time to the minute and operate on a set of social rules that nobody posts on a sign. Here’s what they are.

  1. Silent mode means silent
  2. Phone calls are not done
  3. Priority seats and offering a seat
  4. Platform queuing and boarding

  6. Eating and drinking

Silent mode means silent

The first thing operators ask is that you set your phone to manner mode. That means no ringtone, no notification sounds, no audio of any kind. It does not mean vibrate — in a quiet carriage a buzzing phone on a hard surface is clearly audible to the six people nearest you.

Put it fully silent. It takes two seconds and prevents one of the more uncomfortable social experiences available to a visitor.

Headphones count too. Audio leaking from earbuds is one of the standard announcements on Japanese trains, and it carries further than the wearer ever believes.

Phone calls are not done

Taking a call on a commuter train is a social violation roughly equivalent to eating a sandwich in a library. The standard approach is to let it go to voicemail, or answer, say quietly that you are on a train, and end the call immediately.

On long-distance Shinkansen, calls are acceptable in the vestibule between carriages, and that is where everyone goes to make them. In the carriage itself the same rules apply.

Texting, reading, gaming and watching things silently are all completely normal. It is the voice that is the problem, not the phone.

Priority seats and offering a seat

The seats marked near the doors, usually in a different colour, are for elderly passengers, pregnant women, people with young children and anyone with an injury or disability. On a crowded train they fill like any other; on an empty one the norm is to leave them free.

The nuance is time of day. At eleven on a Friday night nobody is enforcing anything. At eight on a Monday morning, a healthy young person sitting in a priority seat while an elderly passenger stands is making a choice that the carriage notices.

Offering your seat is less common here than visitors expect, and offers are frequently declined — refusing is a way of not being treated as old or frail. If you offer and are turned down, sit back down without fuss; it is not a rejection of the gesture.

Pregnant passengers often carry a small pink maternity badge on their bag, which exists precisely because early pregnancy is not visible. It is worth recognising.

Platform queuing and boarding

Most platforms have markings on the ground showing where the doors will open and where to stand. You queue behind the lines in two columns, and you let everyone off before you board. Look at the floor, find the lines, stand in them.

This works entirely by social agreement and no barriers, which is why it collapses the moment a group of people who do not know the system arrives at the door.

Do not run for a closing door. The doors close hard and on time, another train is along in three minutes, and the sight of someone sprinting down a platform is genuinely unusual here.

Bags, backpacks and luggage

On a crowded train a backpack worn on the back takes up the space of another person and hits someone every time you turn. Take it off and hold it in front of you, or put it on the overhead rack. This is announced on trains constantly and is the visitor behaviour most likely to irritate a Japanese commuter.

Large suitcases go in the space at the end of the carriage, not in the aisle and not on a seat. On the Shinkansen, the overhead rack takes a surprising amount, and the space behind the last row of seats is the place for a big case — which is why booking that row is worth doing.

One rule with a penalty attached: on the Tokaido, Sanyo and Kyushu Shinkansen, baggage over 160cm in total dimensions requires an oversized baggage seat reservation, free in advance and ¥1,000 if you turn up without one.

Eating and drinking

Eating on local commuter trains is generally not done. Drinking from a closed container is fine.

On long-distance trains, eating is expected — the entire ekiben culture of station lunch boxes exists for it, and buying one before boarding the Shinkansen is part of the journey rather than a lapse. Beer with it is normal.

The distinction is between a ten-minute metro ride and a two-hour intercity journey. If nobody around you is eating, you probably should not be either. Strong-smelling food is the one thing to avoid even where eating is fine.

Rush hour, and women-only carriages

Peak-hour crowding in Tokyo is real and is unlike anything most visitors have experienced. Roughly 7:30 to 9:00 in the morning is the crush; if you can travel outside it, do. Pushing is normal, apologising is not expected, and the way out at your station is to say sumimasen and move.

Many operators run women-only carriages during morning peak, marked with pink signs on the platform and stickers on the doors, introduced in response to groping on crowded trains. The restriction usually applies only at certain hours and is printed on the sign. Men wandering into one by accident is a common visitor error and is met with pointing rather than anger.

If you are travelling with luggage, avoid peak hours entirely. A suitcase in a rush-hour carriage is genuinely difficult for everyone including you.

Gates, fares and small things

Tap your IC card flat on the reader on the way in and out; the gates close fast on anyone who hesitates. If the gate rejects you on exit — usually an insufficient balance — do not force it; use the fare adjustment machine before the gates, or the window at the side.

Sleeping on trains is completely normal and nobody thinks anything of it, including people asleep on a stranger's shoulder. Setting an alarm for your stop is sensible.

Each station has its own departure melody, which regular commuters recognise before they look up. It is one of the small pleasures of the system.

Why these rules matter

Japan's rail network carries millions of people daily with near-zero conflict. The reason is not enforcement — there are no transit police watching for phone calls or checking priority seats.

The reason is that everyone in the carriage has implicitly agreed to the same rules, and the rules work because they are followed consistently. A visitor who does not know them is not being judged harshly; they are simply outside the system.

Knowing them before you board means you spend the journey looking at the city rather than at an awkward situation.
