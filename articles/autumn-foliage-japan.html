Autumn Leaves in Japan: Where to Go and When the Timing Actually Works

Koyo — autumn foliage — is not a fixed event. It starts in Hokkaido in September and ends in Kyushu in December. The timing requires a decision.

When koyo happens where

Japan's autumn colour season moves southward and downhill from September through late November. Hokkaido peaks first — late September to mid-October — while central Honshu peaks in early to mid-November. Kyushu and the warmer lowlands finish last, sometimes into December. 'When is the best time for autumn leaves?' depends entirely on where you're going, and the answer shifts by two to four weeks depending on whether you're in the mountains or at sea level.

Altitude moves faster than latitude. A mountain at 2,000 metres in Nagano turns weeks before the valley floor below it, which means a single day trip can take you from bare branches through peak colour to still-green trees. If your dates are fixed and the colour has not arrived where you are, going up is usually quicker than going north.

What triggers it is the cold: the leaves turn when night temperatures drop reliably below about 8°C, and the best years are those with warm days, cold nights and enough rain. A mild autumn delays everything by a week or more, which is why last year's dates are a weak guide to this year's.

How to check the forecast

Japan tracks the autumn colour front the same way it tracks the cherry blossom front, and the forecasts are published, updated weekly through the season, and free. The private weather companies run the most useful versions, with maps showing which locations are beginning to turn, at peak, and past it.

The vocabulary is worth learning because it appears on every site and most signs: mikoro means the colour has started, midokoro or migoro means it is at its best, and ochiba means the leaves are falling. Individual temples and parks publish their own status too, often with a photograph taken that morning, and that is the most reliable source of all.

Check three or four days out rather than three weeks. The front moves faster than long-range forecasts can track it, and a peak predicted in mid-October can shift a week either way by the time you get there.

Kyoto in November

Kyoto's season typically peaks in the second and third weeks of November. The problem: so does Kyoto's tourist density. Tenryu-ji in Arashiyama, Eikan-do near Nanzen-ji, the garden at Tofuku-ji — all spectacular, all packed. The answer is not to avoid them but to arrange your visit around timing. These gardens look different at 7:30am than at 11am. The light is better and the ratio of leaves to people is significantly higher.

Several temples open early specifically in November, and a handful run evening illuminations — Eikan-do and Kodai-ji are the well-known ones — where the maples are lit after dark and reflected in the ponds. The illuminations are crowded and they are also genuinely spectacular; buy tickets ahead where that is possible and go on a weekday.

The quieter alternative within Kyoto is to go north. Ohara, an hour out by bus, has Sanzen-in and a valley of maples with a fraction of the city's crowds. Kurama and Kibune, on the mountain railway, offer the same trade.

Nikko: the underrated option

Nikko, two hours by express from Asakusa or Ueno in Tokyo. The cryptomeria avenue leading to Tosho-gu, surrounded by beech and maple turning gold and orange, is one of the better koyo experiences in Japan and receives a fraction of Kyoto's November visitors.

The specific spot is the Irohazaka switchback road up to Lake Chuzenji — forty-eight hairpin bends, each labelled with a character of the old Japanese alphabet, through forest that turns completely. It is worth the drive if you have transport, or the bus if you don't, and the ropeway at the top opens the view across the whole valley.

One warning: Nikko peaks earlier than Kyoto, generally late October at altitude and early November lower down, and the Irohazaka traffic on an autumn weekend is legendarily bad. Go midweek and go early.

The Japanese Alps

For serious foliage, the high country peaks in October. Kamikochi, a river valley under the Hotaka range, is among the most beautiful places in Japan in autumn and closes for the winter in mid-November — the season is short and the reward is proportionate. The Tateyama Kurobe Alpine Route crosses the mountains by cable car, bus and ropeway through colour at several altitudes at once.

Shirakawa-go's thatched farmhouses surrounded by autumn maples is the kind of composition that doesn't require a good photographer; the scene does the work. It is also extremely popular, so the same early-morning rule applies.

These are mountain destinations with mountain weather. Snow at altitude in October is normal, roads and routes close without much notice, and the last buses run early. Check operating status before travelling, not after.

If you only have Tokyo

Tokyo turns late — the last week of November into early December — which is useful, because it means a trip that arrives after Kyoto has finished can still catch colour.

Rikugien and Koishikawa Korakuen are the two classic gardens, both compact, both with maples over water, and Rikugien runs an evening illumination. Meiji Jingu Gaien's ginkgo avenue is the other Tokyo image: three hundred metres of yellow trees in a straight line, peaking in early December and photographed by everybody.

Neither is a substitute for a mountain valley, and both are twenty minutes from a train station in the middle of a city of fourteen million, which is its own kind of remarkable.

What you are actually looking at

Two trees do most of the work and they are worth being able to tell apart, because signage names them. Momiji is the Japanese maple: small, deeply cut leaves, turning through yellow and orange to a deep red, and the tree that every temple garden is planted around. Icho is the ginkgo, fan-shaped leaves turning a uniform bright yellow, planted along city avenues rather than in gardens.

The distinction matters practically because they peak at different times — ginkgo runs one to two weeks behind maple in the same location. A city that has finished its maples may still have a fortnight of ginkgo ahead.

Ginkgo also drops fruit, which smells appalling. This is not a fault in your planning.

Rain and overcast days

What nobody says: rain makes autumn foliage better. The colours are more saturated, wet bark goes near-black and throws the leaves forward, and the light through wet maple leaves is different from the light through dry ones.

An overcast day in Kyoto in November is not a failed day; it is a different photograph and a quieter street. Bright midday sun is in fact the hardest light to work with, blowing out the reds and filling the gardens with people.

Adjust expectations and pack accordingly: a small umbrella, shoes that tolerate wet gravel, and a cloth for the lens. Temple gardens in the rain are among the best experiences available in Japan and most visitors sit them out in a café.

The practical window

If you have flexibility, aim for November 10 to 20 for central Honshu. Before that the colour is incomplete; after it, the leaves have begun to fall and you are watching the end of something.

Build in slack. A trip designed to hit peak colour on one specific day at one specific temple will fail about half the time, because the front is weather-driven and moves. A trip with three candidate locations at different altitudes over five days will succeed at one of them nearly always.

And book accommodation early. Kyoto in the third week of November is one of the two weeks of the year when the city genuinely runs out of rooms, and prices reflect it.
