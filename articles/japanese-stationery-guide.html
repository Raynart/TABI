Japanese Stationery Is Worth the Suitcase Space

Pens, notebooks, tape, paper clips. Japan has turned the mundane into an argument for why other countries aren't trying hard enough.

In this article

  1. Why this is worth half a day
  2. Where to go
  3. Notebooks and planners
  4. Pens worth buying
  5. Tape, and the small stuff
  6. The suitcase argument

Why this is worth half a day

The Pilot Hi-Tec-C is a 0.3mm gel pen that costs a couple of hundred yen at any stationery shop in Japan and writes with a precision that comparable pens elsewhere have not matched at any price. This is not enthusiasm. It is a statement about the difference between the Japanese stationery industry and everyone else's, and it is the reason to set aside half a day for stationery shopping.

The reason the gap exists is that paper never stopped being a serious consumer category here. Handwriting remains normal in Japanese offices and schools, the writing system rewards a fine, controlled line in a way the Latin alphabet does not, and a domestic market that cares produces manufacturers that compete on tenths of a millimetre. Pilot, Uni, Zebra, Pentel, Tombow and Sakura are all still fighting over the same pencil case.

Where to go

Itoya in Ginza is the institution — twelve floors, operating since 1904, with a whole floor given over to loose paper and another to fountain pens. It is for people who want to be deliberate and spend two hours doing it. Loft is the department-store format: wide selection, efficient, branches everywhere. Hands, formerly Tokyu Hands, has the broadest range if you want craft supplies alongside the stationery.

Two smaller places are worth the detour. Sekaido in Shinjuku is an art-supply store over several floors with prices well below the department stores, and it is where students actually shop. Kakimori in Kuramae builds custom notebooks — you choose the cover, the paper, the rings and the closure, and they assemble it while you wait, which is the single best stationery souvenir available in Tokyo.

Outside the specialists, the stationery aisle of any large supermarket or the ¥100 shops are both better than the best stationery shop in most countries. Daiso and Seria sell perfectly good notebooks, pens and desk accessories for a hundred yen, and for buying in bulk for other people they are unbeatable.

Notebooks and planners

The Hobonichi Techo is the cult object: an A6 daily planner with a page per day, published by the company Shigesato Itoi built, and sold in several formats including the larger Cousin and the slimmer Weeks. Its reputation rests on the paper, which is unusually thin and takes fountain pen ink without bleeding through. The edition year matters, and the covers are sold separately.

Midori's MD notebooks are the plainer, cheaper alternative and arguably the better everyday book: cream paper, exposed binding that lets the book lie flat, no branding on the cover at all. The A5 blank is a few hundred yen and it is the notebook to buy several of.

The Traveler's Notebook is the third of the big three — a leather cover holding replaceable paper inserts, designed to be beaten up and refilled for years. There is a dedicated shop at Nakameguro station. Kokuyo's Campus notebooks are what every Japanese student actually uses, cost almost nothing, and are excellent.

Pens worth buying

For everyday gel pens: the Uni-ball One in 0.38mm and 0.5mm, which has a denser black than anything in its price class, and the Pilot Juice line for colours. The Zebra Mildliner is the highlighter to buy — dual-tipped, in muted tones that do not glare off the page — and it is cheap enough to buy the full set.

Multi-pens are a Japanese speciality and underrated abroad. The Uni Jetstream 4&1 puts four ballpoint colours and a pencil in one barrel that actually writes well, which is not true of multi-pens generally. Bodies and refills are sold separately, so you can build one.

For fountain pens, the entry point is the Pilot Kakuno at around a thousand yen — a genuinely good nib in a plastic body with a smiling face stamped on it. Above that, Pilot, Sailor and Platinum all make serious pens at prices well below the European brands, and Pilot's Iroshizuku inks are the ones people bring home in quantity. Ask to try a nib before buying; every fountain pen counter expects it.

Tape, and the small stuff

Washi masking tape is the category Japan invented as a consumer product, and mt is the brand that did it — the manufacturer made industrial masking tape until a group of customers asked for colours. It comes in hundreds of patterns and widths at a few hundred yen a roll, removes cleanly, repositions, and takes ink. Department stores and the larger stationery shops carry deep selections, and limited regional designs turn up in station shops.

The unglamorous items are where the quality gap is most obvious. Tombow Mono erasers actually erase. Kokuyo's Harinacs staples paper without staples by punching and folding it. Correction tape here runs smooth and does not tear. Sakura's Pigma Micron pens are the archival drawing pens most illustrators outside Japan also use.

Also worth a look: the seal and sticker aisles, the letter sets and envelopes at Itoya, and the enormous range of pencil cases. None of this is expensive and all of it is better made than it needs to be.

The suitcase argument

Stationery is light and flat. A full day's shopping fits in a document bag. The pens weigh nothing, the Hobonichi is the size of a passport, and a stack of MD notebooks takes up less room than a pair of shoes.

This is the one gift category where there is no excuse not to buy for everyone you know, and where the ¥300 item is genuinely as good a present as the ¥3,000 one. A Mildliner set and two good pens costs under two thousand yen and will be used every day for a year, which is more than most souvenirs manage.
