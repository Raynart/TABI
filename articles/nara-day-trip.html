Nara Beyond the Deer: What to Do After the First Hour

Yes, the deer are free-roaming and yes, they will bow to get crackers from you. This takes about 40 minutes. Nara has a full day's worth of things after that.

The deer

Nara Park has approximately 1,200 sika deer that have been designated national treasures and have, over generations of contact with tourists, learned that a particular bowing gesture triggers cracker-giving. You can buy shika-senbei (deer crackers) from vendors at ¥200 a pack. The deer will absolutely approach you. Some will grab your bag. One will probably bite you lightly on the jacket. This is fine and expected and takes under an hour before it becomes routine. The question is what you do with the rest of the day.

Two practical warnings. Do not tease them with the crackers — holding food above your head to draw out the bowing produces butting and nipping from animals that weigh fifty kilos, and the injury statistics are not zero. Buy a pack, hand it out promptly, show your empty hands, and they lose interest immediately.

And keep paper out of reach. They eat maps, tickets, and the corners of guidebooks with real enthusiasm. Anything in an outside pocket is fair game. The crackers themselves are made without any packaging for exactly this reason.

The deer are wild animals with legal protection rather than a managed attraction, which is why there are no fences and why they wander into the road, the shops and occasionally the train station. Autumn is rutting season and the males are genuinely unpredictable; the park cuts their antlers each October in a public ceremony that dates back to the 1600s.

Todai-ji

Todai-ji Temple: one of the largest wooden buildings in the world and home to a 15-meter bronze Buddha (the Nara Daibutsu) that has been sitting in approximately the same position since 752 AD. Entrance is ¥600. The scale is genuinely difficult to convey — the Buddha's nostril is the size of a small door, and there is a pillar inside the building with a hole at the base that's the same diameter as the nostril, which children (and optimistic adults) attempt to crawl through as a matter of ritual.

The building you are standing in is the third one. The first two burned in wars, in 1180 and 1567, and the current hall dates from 1709 — at which point the country could not afford the original width, so it was rebuilt at about two thirds of the earlier scale. That is worth pausing on, because it means the original was half again as wide as the enormous building in front of you.

Do not walk straight through the Nandaimon, the great gate on the approach. Set into its two sides are a pair of eight-metre guardian figures carved in 1203 by Unkei and Kaikei and their workshop, in a couple of months, out of thousands of pieces of joined wood. They are among the greatest sculptures in Japan and most visitors photograph the gate from outside and never look up into the alcoves.

Nigatsudo, the part people miss

Up the hill behind the Daibutsu hall, five minutes on a stone staircase, is Nigatsudo. It is free, it is open at all hours, and its wooden veranda looks out west over the whole of Nara with the temple roofs in the foreground. It is the best view in the city and it is nearly always quiet.

It is also the site of Omizutori, a fire and water ritual performed every year for over 1,250 years without interruption — through wars, fires and the pandemic. For two weeks in March, monks run along that veranda at night carrying six-metre burning cedar torches and shower sparks over the crowd below. If you are in Kansai in early March, reorganise your evening for it.

The lane between Todai-ji and Nigatsudo, walled and stone-paved, is the prettiest short walk in Nara and takes about eight minutes.

Kasuga Taisha

Kasuga Taisha: a Shinto shrine established in 768 AD, famous for its 3,000 stone and bronze lanterns that are lit twice a year (Setsubun in February and Obon in August). The path through the cedar forest to reach it is as good as the shrine itself — moss-covered stone lanterns lining the approach, deer wandering between the cedars. Entry to the outer grounds is free; the inner precinct is ¥500.

Pay the ¥500. The inner corridors are where the bronze lanterns hang in ranks under the eaves, and there is a dark room where a small number of them are kept lit permanently between mirrored walls, which gives you the lantern festival on any ordinary Tuesday.

The shrine was rebuilt to an identical design every twenty years for most of its history, in the same tradition as Ise, which is why a building founded in 768 looks new. The forest behind it has been protected from logging since the ninth century and is now primary woodland in the middle of a city — a genuinely rare thing.

Kofuku-ji and the National Museum

Kofuku-ji sits at the western edge of the park, closest to the stations, and its five-storey pagoda is the second tallest in Japan and the building most people photograph without knowing what it is. The grounds are open and free to walk through.

The reason to pay to go inside is the Ashura statue in the treasure hall: a 1,300-year-old dry-lacquer figure of a three-faced, six-armed guardian with the face of a teenager and an expression that is not the fierce one the form calls for but something closer to sorrow. It is one of the most famous objects in Japanese art and it is startlingly modern in feeling.

The Nara National Museum next door holds the country's best collection of Buddhist sculpture. For two weeks each autumn it also opens the Shosoin exhibition — objects from the eighth-century imperial treasure house, including Persian glass and Central Asian instruments that arrived along the Silk Road and have never been buried or excavated, only stored. If your visit coincides, prioritise it over everything else on this page.

Naramachi

Naramachi: the preserved merchant town directly south of Nara Park. Narrow alleys, machiya townhouses, textile shops, sake breweries, and craft cafés — the kind of neighborhood that was here 200 years ago and has been left largely intact. It's quieter than the park area and full of places to eat.

Nakatanidou, on the approach to the park, pounds mochi in public several times an hour — two men, one enormous wooden mallet, and a rhythm fast enough that it looks unsafe. A warm yomogi mochi rolled in kinako, eaten standing up, costs a couple of hundred yen and is one of the better small pleasures in Kansai.

Look for the small red cloth balls hanging outside the old houses. They are migawari-zaru, substitute monkeys, one for each member of the household, hung to take on misfortune in their place. Once you have noticed one you will see them on every street in the district.

Getting there and timing

Nara is 45 minutes from Kyoto by JR (¥730 or JR Pass) or about the same on the Kintetsu Line. It works as a half-day or full-day trip from either Kyoto or Osaka.

The choice of line matters more than it looks. Kintetsu Nara Station is a five-minute walk from the park. JR Nara Station is about twenty minutes away, or a short bus ride. If you are not using a JR Pass, Kintetsu is the better option; if you are, factor the walk in.

The main park and Todai-ji can be crowded in the morning; the deer and crowds thin significantly after 3pm. If you're coming on a weekday and can arrive at 8:30am, the park before the day-trippers show up is a genuinely different experience. Todai-ji opens at 7:30am for much of the year, which is early enough to have the Daibutsu almost to yourself.

A workable order for one day

Arrive early and go straight to Todai-ji before the coaches, then up to Nigatsudo for the view while it is still quiet. Walk the wooded path south to Kasuga Taisha, which puts you in the forest during the best light of the morning. That is the heavy half of the day and it is done by about noon.

Come down through the park to Kofuku-ji, eat in Naramachi, and spend the afternoon in the district at whatever pace you like. If the museum is showing something you want, put it after lunch. Head back to the station via Nakatanidou.

This leaves out Mt. Wakakusa, the grass hill on the eastern edge, which is worth an hour if you have it — a short steep climb, a ¥150 entry, and a view back over the temple roofs. It is burned to the ground every January in a festival nobody has a confirmed explanation for.
