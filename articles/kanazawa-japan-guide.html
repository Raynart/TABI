Kanazawa: The City Most Japan Itineraries Skip — and Shouldn't

Three hours from Tokyo by bullet train. A fraction of Kyoto's crowds. Arguably better seafood. There is no good reason it's on so few itineraries.

Why Kanazawa is overlooked

Kanazawa is on the wrong side of Japan. That appears to be the only explanation for why it receives a fraction of Kyoto's visitors while having a castle, two preserved geisha districts, one of the country's three great landscape gardens, and what a lot of serious food people consider the best seafood market outside Tokyo.

It was never bombed in the war and never had a major earthquake, which is why the old districts are genuinely old rather than reconstructed. Under the Maeda clan it was the wealthiest domain in Japan after the shogunate itself, and the money went into crafts rather than fortification. The city is still living on that.

Getting there

The Hokuriku Shinkansen from Tokyo takes about two and a half hours. From Kyoto it is two and a half to three with a change, and from Osaka similar.

The obstacle is psychological rather than practical: most visitors arrive with a Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka triangle already built, and Kanazawa sits outside it. The natural way to absorb it is on the way between Tokyo and Kyoto rather than as a there-and-back detour, and the Hokuriku Arch Pass exists precisely for that routing.

The station itself is worth two minutes of attention: the enormous wooden Tsuzumi gate and glass dome out front regularly appear on lists of the world's most beautiful stations, which is a strange thing for a provincial city to have.

Kenroku-en and the castle

Kenroku-en is one of Japan's three great gardens, developed over two centuries by the Maeda and covering about eleven hectares. In cherry blossom season it is crowded. In late November, when the pines are wrapped in rope cones against the snow in a technique called yukizuri, it is extraordinary and very nearly empty.

It opens early — before the ticket office in some seasons, when entry is free for the first hour or so. That hour is the best time to be in it by a wide margin.

Across the road, Kanazawa Castle is a careful timber reconstruction using traditional joinery rather than the usual concrete, and the grounds are large and free to walk. Gyokusen'inmaru, the small restored garden inside them, is lit in the evenings and almost nobody goes.

The old districts

Higashi Chaya is the best-preserved geisha quarter, smaller and less managed than Gion, with wooden lattice fronts that have not changed structurally since the early nineteenth century. Some of the teahouses still operate. Two are open to visitors, including one with a gold-leaf tea room upstairs. Come early morning or at dusk and the street is effectively yours.

Kazue-machi, along the river below it, is the quieter second district and is arguably the better walk. Nishi Chaya on the other side of town is the third and sees almost nobody.

Nagamachi is the samurai quarter: earth walls, narrow lanes and water channels, with one restored residence open and a garden inside it. And Myoryu-ji — universally called the ninja temple, though it has nothing to do with ninja — is a defensive building full of hidden staircases, trapdoors and a concealed well. It can only be seen on a guided tour in Japanese, and it must be booked in advance.

The food

Kanazawa sits on the Sea of Japan and receives fish from waters that do not reach the Pacific side in the same condition. Omicho Market, a covered market the size of a small neighbourhood, has been running since the eighteenth century and is where the city actually shops.

In winter: crab, specifically the male snow crab, which arrives in November and is the reason a lot of Japanese people come here at all. In spring: firefly squid and sweet shrimp. Year round, a sashimi set from a mid-range place near the market will beat most of what you would eat in a Tokyo tourist district at half the price.

The local cuisine has its own name, Kaga ryori, and its signature dish is jibuni — duck dusted in flour and simmered with wheat gluten in a thickened broth. It is not what anyone expects from Japanese food and it is very good.

Crafts, and the modern half

Gold leaf has been made here since the Edo period and effectively all of Japan's supply still comes from Kanazawa workshops. You can beat your own sheet at several studios, and the city sells gold-leaf ice cream, which is ridiculous and photographs well and tastes exactly like ice cream.

Kutani porcelain, with its heavy overglaze colours, and Kaga yuzen silk dyeing are the other two traditions, and both have museums and working shops rather than only displays.

The 21st Century Museum of Contemporary Art, a circular glass building by SANAA, is one of the better contemporary museums in Japan and draws a local crowd rather than only tourists. Leandro Erlich's swimming pool — which you can appear to stand at the bottom of — is the piece everyone comes for and is worth booking ahead.

Where to stay, and how long

The centre is small enough that almost anywhere is walkable, and the loop bus covers the rest. Katamachi is convenient and has the restaurant density; the area around the station is cheaper and duller.

Two nights minimum. The city rewards an unhurried second day in a way a single overnight cannot capture, and it is also the base for Shirakawa-go, the thatched village about ninety minutes away by bus.

Bring an umbrella regardless of the forecast. There is a local saying that you should not forget your lunch but you should never forget your umbrella, and the weather here justifies it.

What Kanazawa has that Kyoto lost

The feeling of visiting somewhere people actually live. The geisha quarter is not a theme park, the old town is genuinely old and genuinely in use, and the market is a market rather than a food court.

If that is what you are looking for — and increasingly it is what travellers say they want — Kanazawa is close to the correct answer.
