Osaka in 3 Days: The Eating and Wandering Itinerary

Osaka has a reputation as Japan's kitchen, but that barely scratches the surface. This city rewards those who eat their way through it without a plan — we've structured the chaos into three days that still feel spontaneous.

Osaka runs on a different setting

Kyoto is thirty minutes away and might as well be another country. Osaka is louder, blunter and much more interested in feeding you. People talk to strangers. Shop staff joke rather than perform. The local dialect is faster and warmer than Tokyo Japanese, and the city's self-image is built around food and comedy rather than history. Three days is enough to get the shape of it, provided you spend most of them eating and walking rather than travelling between attractions.

Day 1: Namba, Dotonbori and the streets behind them

Start in the middle. Dotonbori — the canal with the running-man sign — is the most photographed strip in the city and worth twenty minutes before you leave it behind. The value is in the streets immediately around it: Hozenji Yokocho, a two-block lane of stone paving and tiny restaurants beside a moss-covered statue that visitors ladle water onto; the covered arcades running north and south; and the Kuromon market for lunch. Eat takoyaki standing up, then again somewhere else, because the difference between a good and bad one is larger than it sounds.

Day 1 evening: Shinsekai

South of Namba, Shinsekai was built as a vision of the future in 1912 and has been comfortably decaying ever since. The Tsutenkaku tower sits at the centre of a grid of kushikatsu restaurants — skewered, breaded, deep-fried everything — where the one rule is that you do not double-dip in the communal sauce. It is slightly seedy, entirely safe, and the closest thing in Japan to a city centre that hasn't been tidied up. Go hungry.

Day 2: Kita, coffee and the department store basements

Spend the morning around Umeda in the north. This is the business half of the city and it is where the food gets serious: the basement floors of the big department stores hold some of the best prepared food in Japan, and lunch sets in the restaurant floors above are heavily subsidised versions of dinner. Nakazakicho, a few minutes east, is a pocket of prewar houses converted into coffee shops and secondhand stores that survived redevelopment more or less by accident. It is the best walking in the city.

Day 2 evening: Tenma and the longest arcade

Tenjinbashisuji is the longest shopping arcade in Japan, running for around two and a half kilometres, and the section around Tenma station turns into a dense grid of standing bars in the evening. This is where Osaka eats when it isn't performing for anyone. Order by pointing, expect to share a counter, and accept that you will end up talking to whoever is next to you. Budget less than you think — the point of the format is volume and cheapness.

Day 3: one castle, one deviation

Osaka Castle is a concrete reconstruction with a lift inside, which disappoints people who wanted the original. Go anyway, but go for the park: the outer moat and stonework are genuinely enormous, and the grounds are where the city relaxes. Then take the afternoon somewhere off the tourist map. Tsuruhashi, around the station, is the centre of Osaka's Korean community and has the best grilled meat in the city. Shinsaibashi's back streets have the secondhand clothing. Either is a better final afternoon than a second castle.

How to eat here without a plan

The Osaka approach is kuidaore — roughly, eating yourself into ruin — and it is best served by ordering less at each stop and making more stops. Three small things at three places beats one large meal. Look for queues of locals rather than English menus, sit at counters rather than tables, and if a place has one item on the sign, order that item. Cash is still useful in the older arcades. Almost nothing here needs a reservation, which is precisely why the city is easy to travel in.
