Don Quijote: What to Buy, What to Skip, and How to Survive the Store

Don Quijote is five floors of everything at prices that make no sense. The key is knowing what you came for before you walk in.

What Don Quijote is

Don Quijote — called Donki by everyone who shops there — is a discount variety store that operates on the principle that if you put enough products in a small enough space at a low enough price, people will buy things they didn't know they needed. This works. The stores are open until 5am or around the clock. The in-store jingle plays on loop. The lighting is aggressive. It is not a relaxing shopping experience. It is an effective one.

The chaos is engineered. The merchandising strategy has a name — atsuryoku chinretsu, compression display — and it is a deliberate policy of stacking goods floor to ceiling in narrow aisles so that customers slow down, look around, and find things by accident. The handwritten signs shouting prices in three colours are part of the same design. Nothing here is disorganised; it is organised to feel that way.

The chain has been running since 1989 and now has hundreds of branches, including larger MEGA Donki formats in the suburbs that lean more towards groceries. The city-centre branches are the ones visitors want, and several of them have added tourist floors with tax-free counters and souvenir sections in the last few years.

Skincare and cosmetics

Donki carries Japanese drugstore brands — Hada Labo, Kose, Shiseido, Biore — and some Korean imports at prices below almost anywhere else in Japan. The Hada Labo Gokujyun hyaluronic lotion is the specific item that beauty writers have been recommending for years, and the Donki price is genuinely competitive with anything in Tokyo.

Two things worth knowing before you fill a basket. Sunscreen is the category where Japan is furthest ahead and the prices are lowest — the Biore UV and Anessa lines cost a fraction of what the same bottles fetch abroad. And sheet masks sold in bulk packs of thirty are the highest value-per-suitcase-kilo item in the store.

Check dates on cosmetics. Donki's model involves buying up surplus and end-of-line stock, which is where some of the discount comes from, and occasionally that means an older batch. It is not a problem for most things and it is worth a glance on anything you plan to keep for a year.

Snacks, and the tax-free rule that catches people

The food section has Kit Kat flavours, Pocky, regional crisps and gift-packaged snack sets. If you're buying snacks to take home, this is more convenient than tracking down a specialty shop and often cheaper.

The tax-free counter is worth using — bring your passport, and the threshold is a purchase of ¥5,000 or more in a day. What surprises people is the rule on consumables: food, drink, cosmetics and medicines bought tax-free are sealed into a transparent bag that you are not supposed to open until you have left the country. If you were planning to eat the snacks on the trip, buy those separately and pay the tax.

Consumables and general goods are also counted separately toward the threshold, which occasionally means a basket that looks like it qualifies does not. The staff at the counter will sort it out; it just takes longer than you expect at 2am.

Electronics, and what not to buy

The electronics section has genuine deals on portable chargers, travel adapters, USB cables and small appliances. The accessories are fine and the prices are good.

Do not buy anything complex. Rice cookers, cameras, and appliances sold for the domestic market run on 100V, carry Japanese-language menus that often cannot be switched, and come with warranties valid only in Japan. Voltage is the practical problem: 100V equipment on a 230V supply fails immediately and sometimes dramatically. Some branches stock export models specifically for visitors, and those are clearly labelled — if it is not labelled, assume domestic.

Skip the branded luxury goods in the glass cases near the registers. They are legitimate, but the selection is thin and the prices are not the bargain the rest of the store is. For whisky, sake or shochu worth buying, go to a dedicated liquor shop.

The things Donki is actually best at

Beyond cosmetics, three categories justify the trip. Costume and party goods — Donki is where Tokyo buys its Halloween outfits, and the year-round selection is enormous. Kitchen gadgets: single-purpose Japanese tools, egg moulds, onigiri presses, vegetable cutters, all cheap and all flat enough to pack.

And the own-brand line, Jonetsu Kakaku, which covers everything from socks to electric fans at prices that undercut everyone. The quality is variable and the packaging is loud, which is rather the point.

It is also the most reliable place in Japan to buy something you forgot at eleven at night — an umbrella, a charger, a shirt, a suitcase to hold everything else you bought. That utility is a large part of why locals actually shop there.

Navigation and which branch

The store layout is deliberately non-intuitive, and products are stacked floor-to-ceiling in a way that turns navigation into something between a maze and an archaeological dig. Accept this. Give yourself at least 90 minutes for your first visit, go with a list of categories rather than products, and let the store find you things.

The Shibuya and Shinjuku branches are the largest and most complete in Tokyo. Akihabara is useful if you want the electronics without the full experience. The Dotonbori branch in Osaka has a Ferris wheel attached to the building, which tells you most of what you need to know about the chain's sense of restraint.

Go late. The stores are busiest in the early evening and pleasantly empty after eleven, the tax-free queue is shortest around then, and Donki at one in the morning under those lights is an experience in its own right.
