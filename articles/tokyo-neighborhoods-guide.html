Where to Stay in Tokyo: Honest Assessments of Each Neighborhood

Every first-time visitor books Shinjuku because someone told them to. Some of those people are right.

Why neighborhood choice matters

Tokyo has 23 special wards and dozens of distinct neighbourhoods, each with a different character and a different argument for why you should sleep there. The decision matters more than most hotel comparison sites suggest. An hour on the Yamanote Line from Ueno to Shibuya is not the same as a ten-minute walk from wherever you are standing. Where you sleep shapes what you do in the morning and, more importantly, what you do at eleven at night.

The single most useful filter is the Yamanote Line, the loop that connects most of the places a visitor wants to go. Staying on it, or one stop from it, removes almost every transport problem. Staying twenty minutes outside it saves money and costs you an hour a day.

The second filter is the last train. Tokyo's network stops around midnight and restarts around five, and a neighbourhood you can walk back to changes how the evenings work.

Shinjuku

The right choice for first-timers who want maximum convenience. Shinjuku Station connects to almost everything, including the Narita Express and the airport buses. The west side has the skyscraper district and the bus terminal for day trips; the east side has Kabukicho, Golden Gai and Omoide Yokocho, which are three quite different things to do after dark.

Hotels run from capsules at a few thousand yen a night to the top of the market. The area is not charming and it does not pretend to be. It works.

The one warning is the station itself, which is the busiest in the world and has upwards of two hundred exits. Note which exit your hotel is near before you arrive, because the difference between the correct exit and the wrong one is fifteen minutes and a lot of stairs.

Asakusa and the east

Asakusa is the tourist district, with one of the city's best sightseeing densities within walking distance — Senso-ji, Nakamise, the Sumida River. It is also the best-value area for accommodation, with a concentration of well-run hostels and small hotels.

The downsides: it is quiet outside tourist hours, the transport options are thinner than in Shinjuku or Shibuya, and it does not have the restaurant and bar density of the west side. Good for concentrated sightseeing and a vaguely traditional atmosphere. Less good for eating late or getting to Shibuya quickly.

Nearby, Kuramae has become the quiet alternative — a former wholesale district now full of small coffee roasters and craft workshops, ten minutes from Asakusa and considerably calmer.

Shibuya and the west

Better for people on a second or third visit. The neighbourhood has been under continuous redevelopment for over a decade and will be for several more years, which means construction hoardings and changed walking routes.

The crossing is iconic and worth seeing once; it is not worth basing a week around. The real advantage is access to the private lines heading west — Shimokitazawa on the Inokashira Line, and Nakameguro and Daikanyama within walking distance, which are among the more pleasant parts of the city to spend an evening.

Ebisu, one stop south, is the version of this for people who would rather eat well than go clubbing: same access, better restaurants, considerably calmer.

Ginza, Tokyo Station and Nihonbashi

Ginza is expensive, convenient and quiet at night. If your priorities are ease of movement and you are not interested in nightlife, the business hotels here are excellent value relative to the address. It is walking distance to Tsukiji and one line from Shibuya. It can feel sterile, because it largely is.

Marunouchi, around Tokyo Station, is the choice if you are taking Shinkansen day trips or leaving from Narita by train — you are inside the station rather than travelling to it. It is a business district and it empties at the weekend, which some people find restful and others find eerie.

Nihonbashi sits between the two and is the underrated one: old merchant district, several centuries-old shops still trading, good access, and prices below Ginza for the same convenience.

Ueno, Yanaka and the old city

Ueno is the most practical choice for travellers arriving with luggage from the north, since the Shinkansen stops there. The area around the park is dense with museums and cheap restaurants, and Ameyoko market underneath the tracks is a genuine survivor of post-war Tokyo.

It has none of Shinjuku's energy and more neighbourhood character than Shinjuku has. Yanaka and Nezu are walkable from here, and they are among the most interesting surviving pieces of old Tokyo — low wooden houses, a large cemetery full of cherry trees, a shopping street that has not changed much in fifty years.

This is the area to choose if what you want from Tokyo is the version that is not on the postcards.

Ikebukuro, Akihabara, Roppongi, Shinagawa

Ikebukuro is Shinjuku with the prices reduced and the polish removed. Excellent transport, huge amount of cheap food, considerably less pretty. If budget is the binding constraint and you still want a Yamanote station, this is the answer.

Akihabara is a destination rather than a base unless electronics and anime are the point of your trip, in which case it is the obvious choice. Roppongi is where the international nightlife concentrates, along with two of the best art museums in the city; it is also the one area of Tokyo where visitors regularly report being hassled by touts, which is worth knowing.

Shinagawa is the pragmatic choice for a short trip flying into Haneda: fifteen minutes from the airport, on the Shinkansen, on the Yamanote. Nobody stays there for the atmosphere and nobody regrets it on a two-night stop.

What kind of room to book

The default is the business hotel: a small, clean, efficient room with everything working, priced per person rather than per room, and available everywhere. Expect it to be genuinely small — a double room of twelve square metres is normal and is not a mistake in the listing.

Capsule hotels are cheaper, better than they sound, and now frequently designed for tourists with separate floors by gender. Hostels in Tokyo are unusually good. At the other end, the international luxury hotels here are among the best in the world and priced accordingly.

Two practical warnings. Check-in is often 3pm or later and enforced, though nearly every hotel will store your bags from the morning. And short-term apartment rentals are regulated in Japan — a legal listing carries a registration number, and unlicensed ones do get shut down, occasionally while guests are booked in.

The general rule

Within the Yamanote loop, you are never more than about twenty minutes from anything important. The choice of neighbourhood is therefore not really about access; it is about what you walk outside into in the morning and what is still open when you come back at night.

Pick for the atmosphere you want at breakfast and at eleven at night, and trust the train system to handle the rest. And if you are in Tokyo for more than five nights, consider splitting between two neighbourhoods — it costs you one afternoon of moving and gives you two quite different cities.
