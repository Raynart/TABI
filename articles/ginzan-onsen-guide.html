Ginzan Onsen: The Snow Town That Had to Start Turning People Away

A single street of wooden inns along a river, lit by gas lamps, buried in snow. It became too photogenic for its own good, and the town now controls who can walk down it in winter.

What it actually is

Ginzan Onsen is a hot spring town in the mountains of Yamagata prefecture, built along both banks of a narrow river in a steep valley. The inns are three and four storey wooden buildings from the Taisho era, the 1910s and 1920s, with decorated plaster facades, and they face each other across the water.

The name means silver mountain: this was a silver mining settlement, and when the mine failed the hot springs kept it alive. The architecture survives because the town was too remote to be worth redeveloping.

In winter, with snow piled on the roofs and the gas lamps lit along the street at dusk, it is one of the most photographed places in Japan. That is both the appeal and the problem.

The winter restrictions, which you must plan around

The town is a single narrow street with nowhere to absorb a crowd, and the volume of winter day-trippers reached a point where it was unsafe and unpleasant for everyone. The local association now controls access during the winter season.

The arrangement introduced for recent winters has run from around 20 December to the beginning of March, and works roughly like this. During the day, until late afternoon, entry is unrestricted. In the evening, when the lamps are lit and everyone wants to be there, numbers are capped and entry requires a ticket bought in advance, with a limited number of slots per hour. Later in the evening and overnight, only residents, staff and guests with a room booked are admitted.

Cars cannot drive into the town in winter. There is a park-and-ride arrangement at a car park outside, with a shuttle bus in, and the evening ticket typically includes the shuttle.

The details — the dates, the price, the number of slots, the booking channel — have been revised each year since the scheme began. Check the town's own current announcement before booking anything else, because the trip only works if this part is arranged first.

The exemption is the important part: if you are staying the night, none of this applies to you.

Which is why you should stay the night

Staying overnight converts the whole experience. The day visitors leave, the street empties, the lamps come on, and the town becomes the quiet place the photographs imply rather than the crowded one they conceal.

The ryokan are small, old and mostly expensive, and they book out months ahead for winter weekends. Several have been operating for generations and one is a registered cultural property. Rooms are traditional, meals are included and served at set times, and the buildings are wooden and creak.

If the town itself is full, there are more modern places a short distance down the valley with shuttle arrangements, which is a reasonable compromise.

Book as early as you can. This is not a place to improvise.

The bathing

The water is sulphurous and hot. Each inn has its own baths for guests, and there are a couple of public bathhouses on the street for day visitors, along with a free foot bath by the river that is the most sociable spot in the town.

There are also two small communal baths that can be booked privately by the hour, which is the practical solution for anyone with tattoos or travelling as a family.

The etiquette is standard onsen etiquette: wash thoroughly at the taps before entering, no swimwear, no towel in the water, tie long hair up.

Getting there

The route is Yamagata Shinkansen to Oishida station, then a bus up the valley, which takes around forty minutes. The buses are timed to the trains but are not frequent, and the last one back is early — a fact that has stranded people who planned a leisurely dinner.

From Tokyo the whole journey is around four hours. From Sendai it is a couple of hours across the mountains.

In deep winter, allow for trains and buses being delayed by snow, and do not plan a tight connection onwards on the day you leave.

The rest of the year

Winter is the famous season and the difficult one. The rest of the year the town is quiet, unrestricted and considerably cheaper.

The valley is green and cool in summer, and there is a walk up past the old mine workings to a waterfall behind the town that most winter visitors never see because it is under snow. Autumn colour in the valley is excellent and the crowds are a fraction of the winter numbers.

If the photograph is what you want, go in winter and stay the night. If the town is what you want, go in October.

The wider point

Ginzan is a small example of something happening across Japan: places that were quiet for a century becoming globally famous through a single image, and having to invent crowd management from nothing. Several towns have introduced entry fees, caps or bans on photography in particular streets.

None of it is aimed at foreign visitors specifically, and all of it is worth checking before you travel. The pattern is now common enough that assuming a small famous place can simply be turned up at is the mistake.
