Beppu: The Onsen City That's Stranger and Better Than You Expect

Beppu produces more geothermal steam per day than anywhere in Japan and possibly anywhere outside Iceland. The city treats this as completely normal.

What Beppu is

Beppu is on the north-east coast of Kyushu, about an hour and three quarters from Fukuoka by limited express. The city has eight distinct onsen districts, each with different mineral compositions and water colours — clear, milky white, reddish brown, and the vivid cobalt of the Kannawa area.

The steam rising off the streets on a cool morning makes it look as though the city is slowly cooking from below, which is more or less accurate. There are thousands of individual sources here, and the town produces more geothermal water per day than anywhere else in Japan.

The eight districts are collectively the Beppu Hatto, and they are genuinely different from each other. Beppu proper is the flat, slightly worn town centre by the station. Kannawa, up the hill, is the atmospheric one, full of steam vents and old bathhouses. Myoban is higher again and smells strongly of sulphur. Choosing which one to base yourself in matters more than choosing a hotel.

The Hell Tour

The Jigoku Meguri — the Hell Tour — is the headline attraction: seven pools of geothermally active water that cannot be bathed in because they are too hot, at close to boiling, or too acidic.

They are genuinely remarkable. Umi Jigoku, the Sea Hell, is a deep cobalt blue from dissolved iron sulphate. Chinoike Jigoku, the Blood Pond Hell, is exactly as red as it sounds, from iron oxide in the clay. Oniyama Jigoku keeps crocodiles, because the groundwater is warm enough to maintain tropical reptiles year-round, which is either delightful or dubious depending on your view.

A circuit ticket covers the set for around ¥2,200 and they are spread across two clusters, so allow half a day with a bus between them. It is unashamedly a tourist attraction and it is worth doing once — but it is not why you stay.

The baths are the actual reason

The bathing is why you stay overnight. Kannawa and the older neighbourhoods have small municipal baths — kyodo yokujo — for as little as ¥100–300. Coin-operated, simple tiled rooms, no facilities beyond a basket and a tap, and water that does things to your skin that a hotel bath does not replicate. Bring your own towel and soap; these places supply nothing.

Hyotan Onsen near Kannawa is the well-known larger option, with several pools including waterfall baths where hot water falls on your shoulders, and it stays open late. It holds a Michelin star for an onsen, which is a category most people do not know exists.

The water genuinely differs between districts. Myoban is sulphurous and milky and will tarnish silver; Kannawa is mineral-heavy; some of the coastal sources are salty. Bathing in three different districts in a day is the local sport and it is the correct way to spend one here.

Sand, steam and mud

Takegawara Onsen in the town centre is the famous sand bath: you lie in a shallow pit in a yukata and staff bury you in geothermally heated sand up to the neck. It sounds alarming and is instead comfortable in a way that is hard to convey. Ten to fifteen minutes is the standard session, and the weight of the sand is as much of the effect as the heat. The building itself dates from 1938 and is worth seeing regardless.

In Kannawa, the steam is used for cooking. Jigoku-mushi — hell steaming — means putting vegetables, eggs, seafood or meat into a stone box over a natural vent and letting the steam do it in ten minutes. Several places let you cook your own, and it is a genuinely good lunch as well as a novelty.

Myoban is where the yunohana huts are: thatched sheds where mineral crystals are harvested from the ground over months, a technique that has been going since the eighteenth century. You can buy the crystals to make onsen water at home, which is the best souvenir in the city.

Beppu or Yufuin

The two are half an hour apart and are constantly compared. Yufuin is the pretty one — a small town under a mountain, boutique inns, craft shops, art museums, and a main street that is essentially a promenade of gift shops. It is genuinely lovely and it is heavily curated.

Beppu is bigger, messier and considerably less polished, with a slightly faded seaside-resort quality and far more actual bathing. It is also much cheaper.

If you want a single elegant night, Yufuin. If you want to spend two days getting into as many different waters as possible, Beppu. Doing both is easy and is what most Japanese visitors do.

Tattoos, and the small baths

Beppu is unusually relaxed by Japanese standards, largely because so much of its bathing happens in tiny municipal baths run for local residents rather than in resort facilities. Many of the ¥100 baths have no staff at all.

That is not a guarantee. Larger facilities and hotel baths apply the standard policy, and the Hell circuit's associated baths are among the stricter. Ask, or book a room with a private bath, which is cheap here.

The private-bath option is genuinely inexpensive in Beppu — many ryokan have several family baths free to guests, which removes the question entirely.

Getting there and staying

The Sonic limited express from Hakata in Fukuoka takes around two hours and is covered by the JR Pass and by the Kyushu regional passes. From Osaka there is an overnight ferry, which is a slow and rather wonderful way to arrive.

The city is small enough that the main districts are reachable by local bus or a short taxi ride, and the bus day pass is worth buying on arrival. Kannawa is the best base for atmosphere, the station area for convenience and cheapness.

Ryokan here are genuinely affordable compared with Hakone — ¥10,000–15,000 per person including dinner and breakfast is achievable, and the food is Kyushu seafood. Beppu makes a strong case for being the most accessible serious onsen town in Japan.

One addition if you have a spare hour: Takasakiyama, just outside town, is a mountain with a resident population of well over a thousand wild macaques that come down to be fed twice a day. It is not an enclosure and the monkeys are entirely uninterested in you.
