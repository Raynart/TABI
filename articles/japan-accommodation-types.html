Where to Sleep in Japan: Every Accommodation Type, Honestly Compared

Business hotel, capsule, ryokan, hostel, minshuku, apartment. They are not points on a price ladder — they are different products, and the cheap one is often the right one.

The business hotel is the default, and it should be

The single most useful thing to know about accommodation in Japan is that the business hotel — the chains found beside every major station — is very good at what it does, and what it does is most of what a traveller needs.

The rooms are small. Genuinely small: a double bed with enough space to walk down one side of it, a compact unit bathroom moulded in one piece, a desk, a kettle. What you get in exchange is a spotless, quiet, reliably functioning room a few minutes from a station, with a bed that is properly made and hot water that works, at a price well below the equivalent in most Western cities.

The chains are consistent enough that booking one you have never seen carries no risk. Several include a simple breakfast, and some have a large communal bath on the top floor, which is a considerable upgrade over the unit bathroom.

For a trip that is mostly about being out of the room, this is the correct choice, and choosing something more expensive frequently buys you a bigger room you will not be in.

Capsule hotels are not a stunt

A capsule is a moulded sleeping berth in a rack, with a curtain or a blind rather than a door, in a room of several dozen. Your luggage goes in a locker. There are shared washrooms and usually a communal bath.

They are clean, safe, cheap and quiet — the noise rules are taken seriously — and they are a completely reasonable way to spend a night, particularly a night you did not plan on. Missing the last train is a normal event in Japan and the capsule hotel is the normal answer to it.

The caveats are real though. Many are single-sex or have only a small women's floor, so book rather than turn up. They are not designed for large suitcases. And they are for sleeping: there is nowhere to sit and no privacy beyond the curtain, so a rest day in one would be miserable.

Ryokan is a different product entirely

A ryokan is not a hotel with tatami. It is a package: a room with a floor you sit on, a communal bath, a kaiseki dinner served at a set time, breakfast the next morning, and a rhythm you fit into rather than direct.

That means the price is not comparable to a hotel room, because it includes two substantial meals and an experience that is the point of the stay. It also means a ryokan is a poor choice if you intend to be out all evening — you will be paying for and missing the best part.

One or two nights in the middle of a trip, in an onsen town rather than a city, is the shape that works. Book earlier than you think; the good ones fill months ahead for weekends and holidays.

Hostels and guesthouses have changed

The Japanese hostel is not the European backpacker hostel. Design-led guesthouses with good coffee, quiet common rooms and dormitories with proper individual berths are common, and a lot of them are in older buildings in neighbourhoods worth staying in.

They are the best option for solo travellers who want to talk to someone, and many have private rooms as well as dorms — often at a price close to a business hotel but in a more interesting building and a less convenient location.

The trade is usually noise, shared bathrooms, and staff who are there for part of the day rather than all of it.

Minshuku, shukubo and the rural options

A minshuku is a family-run guesthouse, usually with tatami rooms and shared facilities, typically with dinner and breakfast included. It is the rural equivalent of a bed and breakfast and it is often the only accommodation in a small place. Expect home cooking, an early dinner and a household rhythm.

Shukubo is temple lodging, most famously on Koyasan. You sleep in a temple, eat the vegetarian Buddhist cuisine, and can usually attend the morning service. It is a genuine experience rather than a themed one, and it books up.

Both are worth planning a night around rather than treating as a fallback.

Apartments and the rental rules

Short-term rentals are legal and regulated, and the regulation is stricter than in many countries — licensed properties display a registration number, and the number of nights they may operate is capped. Book through platforms that show the licence, and be wary of anything advertised well below the going rate.

For a family, or a stay of a week or more in one city, an apartment with a washing machine and a kitchen changes the trip materially. For two or three nights the check-in logistics rarely justify it.

How to choose, in one paragraph

Match the accommodation to what the day looks like. Out from breakfast until late, in a city: business hotel, near a station. A night in the mountains built around dinner and a bath: ryokan. Travelling alone and wanting company: guesthouse. Somewhere rural with no hotels: minshuku. A week in one place with children: apartment. A night you did not plan: capsule.

And book the station before you book the room. In Japanese cities, five minutes from the right station beats a nicer room twenty minutes from the wrong one, every time.
