The Tokyo Subway: How to Navigate It Without Getting Lost

Tokyo’s train network is the most complex urban rail system in the world. It is also, once you understand the basics, extremely easy to use.

It is not two networks, it is about a dozen

The Tokyo Metro has thirteen lines and the Toei Subway has four, and between them they are what most people mean by the subway. But JR East runs the surface network on top of it, and half a dozen private railways — Tokyu, Odakyu, Keio, Seibu, Tobu, Keikyu, Keisei — run the lines out to the suburbs.

They are separate companies with separate fares, and transferring between them costs more than staying on one. An IC card handles all of this invisibly, which is the main practical reason to have one.

The complication that catches people is through-service: a Tokyo Metro train frequently continues past the end of its own line onto a private railway and keeps going under a different name. You have not made a mistake and you do not need to change; but you may have left the network your ticket covers, which is another reason for the card.

The Yamanote Line is your backbone

The Yamanote is not a subway — it is a JR surface loop around central Tokyo connecting Shinjuku, Shibuya, Harajuku, Ebisu, Shinagawa, Tokyo, Akihabara, Ueno, Nippori and Ikebukuro, and back around. A full circuit takes about an hour and trains run every few minutes.

Think of it as the outer ring. To move between major neighbourhoods you generally go out to the loop, round it, and back in. Almost everything a visitor wants is inside it or on it.

Two lines cut across the middle and are worth knowing for that reason: the Chuo, which runs straight through from Tokyo to Shinjuku and out west, and the Ginza and Hanzomon metro lines, which cross the centre diagonally.

The numbering system means you never read kanji

Every line has a letter and a colour, and every station on it has a number: Ginza on the Ginza Line is G09, Shibuya on the same line is G01. The codes are printed on every platform sign, every map and every carriage display.

This means you can navigate entirely on letters and numbers. If you need to get from G09 to G01, you are on the right line and going the right way if the numbers are counting down.

Inside the carriages, digital displays cycle through the next stations in Japanese, English, Chinese and Korean, and show which side the doors will open and where the transfers are. Free multilingual maps are at every station office.

Exits are the actual difficulty

The trains are the easy part. The stations are where visitors lose time. Shinjuku has upwards of two hundred exits and Shibuya, Tokyo and Ikebukuro are not far behind, and coming out of the wrong one can leave you fifteen minutes and one large building away from where you meant to be.

Exits are numbered and lettered, and Google Maps will tell you which one you want. Note it before you go underground, because there is often no signal down there.

Follow the yellow signs for exits and the coloured line symbols for transfers. Transfers within a station can be genuine ten-minute walks, and the journey planner accounts for that; if it says the change takes eight minutes, it means eight minutes of walking.

IC card, and the passes that are not worth it

Buy an IC card at any ticket machine and load ¥2,000–3,000. Tap in, tap out, the fare is deducted, and you never calculate anything. A paper ticket requires selecting the correct fare from a map that is sometimes only in Japanese, and there is no situation where it is preferable.

Day passes exist — Tokyo Metro sells 24, 48 and 72-hour tourist tickets, and there is a combined Metro and Toei version. They only pay off if you are making a lot of separate journeys, since a typical fare is ¥180–320. Four or five rides a day is roughly the break-even.

For most itineraries the card is simpler and cheaper. Passes are worth it on a day of deliberate criss-crossing, and not much else.

Google Maps is the right tool

Transit directions for Tokyo are accurate and reliable: the correct line, the platform, the number of stops, the transfer, the fare and often the specific carriage to board for the fastest exit.

The specialist Japanese apps are more detailed for Shinkansen and long-distance planning and add nothing for basic subway navigation.

One habit worth adopting: screenshot the route before you go underground. Coverage in stations is generally good and it fails at the worst moments.

Rush hour and the last train

Peak crowding, roughly half seven to nine in the morning, is real. If you are carrying luggage, avoid it entirely; a suitcase in a rush-hour carriage is difficult for everyone including you. Coin lockers at major stations solve this, and they take IC cards.

Many lines run women-only carriages during the morning peak, marked in pink on the platform. Men wandering into one is a common visitor error.

Trains stop around midnight and do not run again until about five. This is not London or New York with a reduced overnight service — they simply stop. Taxis are expensive and scarce at that hour on a Friday. Check your last train before you settle in somewhere, and note that the last train on your line may be considerably earlier than the last train out of Shibuya.
