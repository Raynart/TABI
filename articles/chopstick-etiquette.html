How to Use Chopsticks in Japan — and What Never to Do

Using chopsticks correctly in Japan is less about technique and more about knowing the four things that are genuinely offensive. The technique is optional. The four things are not.

The two that are genuinely serious

Tatehashi: standing chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice. This is how rice is offered to the dead at a funeral and at a household altar. It is not a minor breach of form; it is placing a funeral object on a dinner table, and it will stop conversation.

Passing food directly from your chopsticks to someone else's is the other one, and it comes from the same place: at a Japanese cremation, the bones are passed between relatives chopstick to chopstick. If you want to share something, put it on the other person's plate.

Both are known to every Japanese person and explained to children. Seeing a foreign visitor do either is understood as unfamiliarity rather than disrespect, but they are the two worth actually remembering.

The everyday ones

Sashi-bashi: spearing food with a chopstick as if it were a fork. Mayoi-bashi: hovering over the dishes while deciding, which is read as indecisive and slightly greedy. Yose-bashi: dragging a dish towards you with your chopsticks instead of picking it up. Saguri-bashi: rummaging through a communal dish for the piece you want.

Neburi-bashi is licking the ends, and namida-bashi is letting food drip across the table on the way to your mouth — the name means 'crying chopsticks'. Watashi-bashi, laying them across the top of a bowl, is common enough in practice but the correct signal is the rest.

None of these will cause offence in the way the first two do. They are closer to eating with your elbows on the table: nobody says anything, everybody notices.

Where to put them down

The hashioki is the small ceramic rest beside your plate, and it is what it is for. Chopsticks go on it with the tips pointing left, parallel to the edge of the table, whenever you are not using them.

If there is no rest — common at casual places — the paper sleeve the disposable chopsticks came in can be folded into one in about five seconds, which is a small trick that Japanese diners do without thinking about it. Otherwise, resting the tips on the edge of a small dish is fine.

At the end of the meal, put them back in the paper sleeve if there was one, or lay them neatly on the rest. Leaving them crossed on the table is untidy rather than taboo.

How to actually hold them

The lower stick does not move. It sits in the crook between thumb and index finger and rests on the ring finger, and it stays exactly where it is for the whole meal. The upper stick is held like a pencil, between thumb, index and middle finger, and it is the only one that moves.

Almost everyone who struggles with chopsticks is moving both sticks. Fixing the bottom one solves most of it in about ten minutes of practice.

Grip near the top third rather than close to the tips. It feels less controlled and gives you far more reach and leverage — and holding chopsticks very low down is a childish grip that adults notice in the same way an adult holding a pen in a fist would be noticed.

Technique is not the point

Many restaurants that serve foreign visitors have forks available on request. If you cannot use chopsticks comfortably, ask for one. Nobody will be offended.

Struggling badly for an entire meal while declining a fork is not a gesture of cultural respect — it is inconvenient for you and creates mild concern for everyone eating with you. Use the fork. Practise the chopsticks when you have time and are not hungry.

And expect to be complimented on doing it badly. 'O-hashi ga jouzu desu ne' — you are good with chopsticks — is offered to foreigners with real warmth and very low standards. Take it as the kindness it is.

Shared dishes

At a shared meal — izakaya, shabu-shabu, hot pot — there may be serving chopsticks, toribashi, for moving food from the central dish to your plate. Use them if they are there.

If there are none, the convention in informal settings is to turn your own chopsticks around and use the clean ends. This is widely practised, though some etiquette purists dislike it on the grounds that the ends you are now handling have been in your hand. Either is acceptable; taking directly from a communal pot with the ends you have been eating from is not.

Take from the side of the dish nearest you rather than reaching into the middle for the best piece, and take one thing at a time.

Disposable chopsticks

Waribashi are the disposable wooden chopsticks served at casual restaurants and convenience stores. Split them apart holding them horizontally and pulling gently sideways — done vertically, they tend to split unevenly and take half the table with them.

Rubbing them together to remove splinters is poor form at a proper restaurant, because it implies the chopsticks are cheap, which implies the restaurant is cheap. At a convenience store on a park bench, nobody is watching and the rule is not in operation. Context applies.

Convenience stores will ask whether you want chopsticks and will give you exactly as many pairs as there are meals. Saying no if you do not need them is normal.

The bowl in your other hand

The rule that surprises Western diners most is that small bowls are lifted. Rice and miso soup are held up near the chest in the left hand and eaten from there, not left on the table and leaned over. Leaving a rice bowl on the table and bending down to it is the actual bad manners here.

Soup is drunk directly from the bowl; there is no spoon, and the chopsticks are for the solid pieces in it. Anything larger than about a hand's width — a donburi, a plate, a serving dish — stays on the table.

For noodles, the bowl stays down, the noodles come up, and slurping is normal and functional. It cools them on the way in.

The chopsticks themselves

Japanese chopsticks are shorter than Chinese ones and taper to a fine point, which is a design for picking bones out of fish rather than for reaching across a large round table. That difference is why they feel unfamiliar even to people confident with Chinese chopsticks.

Households here have personal chopsticks — each person's own pair, not interchangeable, in a way that plates and glasses are not. Lengths differ by hand size, and there are shorter pairs for children. That personal quality is exactly what makes a good pair a genuinely thoughtful souvenir.

A lacquered pair from a specialist shop costs a few thousand yen and will last decades if you keep it out of the dishwasher, which will strip the lacquer. Buy a rest to go with them; that is how a set is given.
