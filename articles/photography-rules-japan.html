What You Can and Cannot Photograph in Japan

Japan is exceptionally photogenic. It also has specific places and situations where photography is restricted, disrespectful, or now actively prohibited. Knowing the difference matters.

The general principle

In most outdoor public spaces in Japan, photography is unrestricted. Streets, parks, temple grounds, shrine pathways, market areas, train stations — all are freely photographable. The restrictions apply inside specific buildings, in areas where signage prohibits it, and in situations where the photography causes problems for other people or for the place itself. Read the signs, watch what other people are doing, and when in doubt, ask.

The prohibition sign is consistent and easy to learn: a camera icon inside a red circle with a diagonal bar, usually beside the characters 撮影禁止 (satsuei kinshi, photography forbidden). A second common sign shows a camera with a lightning bolt crossed out, which restricts flash only and leaves ordinary photography fine.

The most reliable instrument is the room itself. If nobody else has a phone out, there is a reason, and it is generally a better guide than any sign you did or did not spot on the way in.

Inside temples and shrines

Interior photography inside the main hall of a temple or shrine is usually prohibited. The sign will typically show a camera with a red line through it. Some places allow photography inside with a fee; some famous main halls are not accessible at all. Gardens attached to temples are generally freely photographable. Detailed garden photography is expected — it's one of the primary reasons people visit.

There are reasons behind the rule beyond crowd control. Many interiors hold designated cultural properties whose pigments and gold leaf are genuinely damaged by repeated flash. Others contain objects that are religious rather than exhibited — a statue in a working temple is an object of worship, not a display item, and photographing it is closer to photographing a person praying than photographing a museum piece.

Watch for funerals and memorial services, which happen in working temples constantly and are not always obvious to a visitor. Black formal clothing and a gathering near a side hall is your signal to put the camera away entirely.

At onsen and public baths

Photography is prohibited at onsen and sento (public baths), including in the changing rooms. This is absolute and enforced. Phones are not permitted in the bathing areas of most facilities, and taking your phone into a bathing area is both a rule violation and a privacy invasion of every other person present. Leave the phone in the locker.

This is one of the few photography rules in Japan with real legal weight behind it rather than social pressure. Prefectural nuisance ordinances cover surreptitious photography, and an onsen changing room is exactly the situation they exist for. Staff will remove you, and depending on circumstances they will call the police.

The exception people ask about is a private open-air bath attached to a room in a ryokan, where you are the only person present. That is your own space and there is nobody to photograph but yourself.

Photographing strangers

Japan doesn't have a specific law against street photography, but the social norms around photographing strangers are similar to those in most countries: pointing a camera directly at someone's face without consent, following someone for a photograph, or photographing children without parental consent all cross reasonable lines. Most Japanese people will not object verbally, but this reflects a cultural reluctance to confront rather than permission.

There is a recognised legal concept — shozoken, the right to one's own likeness — developed through court decisions rather than a single statute. In practice it is rarely invoked against tourists, but it means that publishing a recognisable photograph of someone who did not consent is not automatically safe just because you took it in the street.

The usable rule: a person incidental to a scene is fine, a person who is the subject of the scene needs consent. If you would feel obliged to explain yourself were they to notice, ask first. 'Shashin, ii desu ka?' — is a photo all right? — is enough, and the answer is yes more often than you would expect.

In Gion, Kyoto

Gion and the surrounding geisha districts of Kyoto are a specific and documented problem. Years of tourists surrounding and following maiko and geiko for photographs has led to complaints from residents, businesses, and the performers themselves. Some streets now carry photography restriction signs, and the private lanes off Hanamikoji have been closed to tourists altogether, with fines attached.

Note the distinction the signs are making: the public streets remain open, the private alleys are private property. The alleys are where people live, and the complaints that closed them were about tourists blocking doorways and pulling at sleeves, not about photography in the abstract.

More simply: if you see a maiko walking quickly with her head down, she is late for an appointment in a costume that costs more than a car and takes hours to put on. She is not a photo opportunity. She is a person doing her job. If you want the photograph, book a formal appointment — several teahouses and studios arrange them, and everyone involved has agreed to be there.

On trains

Photography on trains is not explicitly prohibited but is not commonly practiced for good reason: photographing other passengers without consent in an enclosed space they cannot leave is uncomfortable regardless of country. Photographing the view from the window is universally fine. Photographing your own group is fine. Pointing a camera at strangers in a carriage is not.

Platforms are a different matter and photographing trains is a substantial hobby in Japan, with its own etiquette. Stay behind the yellow tactile line, never use flash toward a cab, and do not set up where you block the flow of people. Station staff will move you promptly if you do.

Shops, restaurants and museums

Retail is the restriction visitors trip over most, because it is the least expected. A great many shops prohibit photography of their goods and displays, particularly department stores, confectioners, bookshops and anywhere selling designs that can be copied. There is often a small sign, and often there is not.

Restaurants vary. Photographing your own food is normal and nobody minds. Photographing the interior, the staff, or an open kitchen requires asking. Sushi counters and small chef-run places are the sensitive case: the chef is working a metre away, and a phone raised toward them without a word is intrusive in a way it is not at a chain restaurant.

Museums usually permit photography in permanent collections without flash and forbid it in special exhibitions, where the works are on loan under conditions the museum does not control. The signage for this is normally at the entrance to each room rather than the building.

The shutter sound you cannot switch off

Phones sold in Japan make an audible shutter noise that cannot be silenced, even on mute. This is not a legal requirement but an industry convention adopted decades ago in response to surreptitious photography, and every domestic manufacturer follows it.

A foreign phone on roaming will normally stay silent, which produces an odd asymmetry: you can take a quieter photograph than any local can. Treat that as a reason for more care rather than less. The noise exists so people know when they are being photographed, and its absence does not change what they are entitled to expect.

Drones and tripods

Drones are heavily restricted. Flying is prohibited over densely populated areas — which covers essentially all of Tokyo and Osaka — near airports, and in most national parks, and requires registration and permission that is not realistically obtainable for a short visit. Assume you cannot fly, and confirm the exception rather than the rule. Penalties are substantial.

Tripods are banned far more widely than people expect: most temple gardens, most museums, many station concourses, and effectively every crowded viewpoint during cherry blossom and autumn foliage season. The reasoning is congestion rather than image rights. A compact travel tripod used somewhere empty is rarely an issue; the same tripod at Kiyomizu-dera in November will get you moved on.
