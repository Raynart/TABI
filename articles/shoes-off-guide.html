When to Take Your Shoes Off in Japan: The Complete Guide

The shoe rule in Japan is not arbitrary. It reflects a clear line between outside space and inside space that structures daily life. Here is where that line is drawn.

  4. At temples, and everywhere else

The genkan

Traditional Japanese homes, ryokan and many older buildings have a genkan — an entrance area slightly lower than the rest of the floor, designed specifically for removing shoes. You step into the genkan, take off your shoes, and step up into the interior in socks or bare feet.

The step is the entire logic. The lower level is outside, conceptually, even though it is indoors; the raised level is inside. Once you understand that the floor height is doing the work, every situation in this article becomes readable without anyone explaining it.

The directional convention is that shoes are turned to point back toward the door once you have stepped up, ready to put on when you leave. This is not required of guests and it is what you will observe everyone doing. Doing it yourself takes two seconds and is noticed.

At ryokan

Shoes come off at the main entrance and you are given slippers. The slippers go everywhere inside the building except tatami rooms, where you go in socks or bare feet, and the toilet, which has its own dedicated pair.

The slippers-off-at-tatami rule catches most visitors at least once. Look at the floor: if it is woven straw matting, your slippers stay at the edge of it. The reason is practical as much as ceremonial — tatami marks and wears under hard soles.

Your outdoor shoes will usually be taken and stored for you, and produced again when you check out. You do not need to keep track of them, and you should not go and retrieve them yourself from the rack.

At restaurants and bars

Traditional restaurants — particularly those with tatami seating, private rooms or a raised platform — will have a designated shoe removal area near the entrance. The signal is usually a step, a row of slippers, or a member of staff making the gesture. Western-style restaurants do not require it.

There is often a wooden shoe locker with a wooden key, or a numbered cubby. Take the key with you. Some izakaya have a sunken floor under the table so you can sit at a low table with your legs down — shoes still come off.

If you are unsure, look at what the people ahead of you are doing, or simply pause at the threshold. Staff will tell you within about a second, because they watch for exactly this.

At temples, and everywhere else

Certain temple halls require shoes off before entering. There will be a step at the entrance and either a rack or plastic bags to carry your shoes with you, which is the better option in a busy place where racks overflow. This varies by temple — outdoor grounds and paths never require it, interiors often do.

The list of other places that follow the rule is longer than visitors expect: some clinics and hospitals, most schools, many small museums and galleries, traditional craft workshops, some public halls, fitting rooms in clothes shops when you step onto a raised platform, and any home you are invited into.

Modern hotels, convenience stores, department stores, offices and Western-style restaurants do not. If there is no step, there is no rule.

The toilet slipper trap

The most reliable mistake visitors make is walking out of the toilet still wearing the toilet slippers. At ryokan and traditional homes there is a dedicated pair inside the toilet room. You put them on to enter and take them off when you leave.

Walking back through the inn in them is the equivalent of carrying the toilet brush into the dining room. It happens to almost everyone once, and it is met with amusement rather than horror.

The correction is mechanical: look at your feet before you open the door. That is the whole technique.

What this means for what you pack

This is the single most consequential packing decision for a trip to Japan and most people get it wrong. Bring shoes you can remove and put back on while standing, without sitting down and without untying anything. Slip-ons, loafers, or laced shoes you can leave loose. Knee-high boots are a genuine mistake here.

Socks matter, because they will be seen constantly and often at close range. Bring more pairs than you think and check them for holes before you leave home. This sounds trivial and it is the thing people report being most embarrassed by.

Two more practical notes. Guest slippers are sized for Japanese feet and stop somewhere around a men's UK 9 or US 10; larger feet should expect to walk on their heels or go in socks, and nobody minds. And if you are visiting a home in winter, thick socks are welcome — floors are cold and central heating is rare.

Reading the signal

The consistent indicator is a change in floor level at the entrance. When you see a step, assume shoes off until there is evidence otherwise. A row of slippers waiting, a shoe rack, a wooden cubby, or a floor that changes from concrete to wood or tatami all point the same way.

When there is no step and no slippers, the rule does not apply. That covers the overwhelming majority of places you will go.

Getting it wrong is a small, common and forgivable error, and the correction is always a polite gesture rather than a rebuke. Watch the threshold, follow the person in front, and you will be right nearly every time.
