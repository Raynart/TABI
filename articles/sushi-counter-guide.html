Eating at a Sushi Counter: What to Expect and How to Not Ruin It

The sushi counter is not intimidating once you understand that there are three rules, they're easy, and the chef wants you to enjoy it.

Counter, conveyor, and standing

Counter sushi is different from kaiten in format, price and the nature of the relationship between you and the chef. At the counter there are usually eight to twelve seats and a glass case of today's fish. You either take a set omakase — chef's choice — or point at items and they are made in front of you. The fish was at the market this morning, the rice is slightly warm, and the whole operation is built around immediacy.

Kaiten, the conveyor belt, is not a lesser version so much as a different product: cheap, fast, family-friendly, and now mostly ordered from a touchscreen with the plates arriving on an express lane. It is genuinely fun and the good chains are far better than the format suggests.

The third category is tachigui, standing sushi bars, which serve counter-quality fish to people who eat six pieces in fifteen minutes and leave. They are the best value in the entire category and are worth seeking out.

The three rules

One: eat each piece as soon as it is placed in front of you. The rice is seasoned and tempered for eating now, not in three minutes. Sushi that sits changes, and at a counter the chef is timing each piece to the last.

Two: it is entirely acceptable to eat nigiri with your fingers, and this is the traditional method. Chopsticks work but risk the topping sliding off. Sashimi is always chopsticks.

Three: dip fish-side down, not rice-side down. Soy sauce in the rice makes the piece fall apart and oversalts the bite. Turn it over gently and touch the fish to the surface of the dish rather than soaking it.

How omakase actually works

Omakase means you have handed the decision to the chef, and the sequence is deliberate: lighter white fish first to calibrate the palate, then progressively richer, with the fatty tuna, the sea urchin and the eel later, and a rolled piece and tamago at the end.

You are not expected to say anything except at the start and the end. Tell them at the start if there is something you cannot eat — allergies, or you simply hate uni — and it will be quietly replaced. At a smaller counter, saying how hungry you are is useful information.

When the sequence has finished the chef will ask if you would like anything else. This is your moment to order a repeat of whatever you liked most, which is the correct and slightly flattering thing to do.

What to know about the fish

The tuna hierarchy is worth learning because it appears everywhere: akami is the lean red meat, chutoro the medium-fatty belly, otoro the fattiest. Otoro is the expensive one and it is overwhelming in quantity — chutoro is the piece most people actually enjoy more.

The words for the formats: nigiri is fish over a hand-pressed finger of rice, gunkan is the ones wrapped in a nori band to hold something loose like roe or urchin, maki is rolled, sashimi is fish alone with no rice at all.

Gunkan cannot be dipped without falling apart. The counter method is to use a slice of pickled ginger as a brush to paint soy sauce on top, which is what the ginger dish beside you is quietly for.

Fish is seasonal here in a way that is taken seriously. If the chef points at something and says it is in season now, order that.

Ginger, wasabi and tea

Ginger is a palate cleanser eaten between pieces, not a topping. Wasabi is already inside the piece at a proper counter, applied in the amount the chef judged right for that fish. Asking for extra is allowed and is a mild statement that you disagree with them; nobody minds.

Do not stir wasabi into your soy sauce at a counter. It is normal at a conveyor place and it is not done in front of a chef who put a measured amount into every piece.

The tea is called agari and it comes hot, strong and unlimited. It is deliberately astringent, to cut the fat between pieces.

Booking, cost and the lunch trick

The most common misconception is that counter sushi is prohibitively expensive. At a mid-range counter away from Ginza, ¥3,000–5,000 buys a satisfying meal of eight to ten pieces plus tea. The ¥20,000 experience exists and is extraordinary, but it is not the entry point.

Lunch is the trick. A great many serious sushi restaurants serve a lunch set using the same fish from the same case at a third to a half of the dinner price. Booking a famous counter for lunch instead of dinner is not a compromise.

The very top places are booked months ahead, often only through a hotel concierge or an existing customer, and some do not take foreign bookings directly. That is a real barrier and it is also irrelevant to having an excellent meal — the gap between a ¥5,000 counter and a ¥40,000 one is far smaller than the price difference implies.

The small courtesies

Do not wear strong perfume or aftershave to a sushi counter. You are sitting a metre from someone whose work depends on smelling fish, and this is a genuine request that good restaurants sometimes make explicitly.

Do not photograph the chef without asking; photographing your own plate is fine. Keep your phone off the counter — it is a work surface.

There is no tipping. Say gochisousama deshita on the way out, which at a counter reaches the person who made your food directly, and is worth more than money would be.
