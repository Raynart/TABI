10 Japanese Drugstore Products Worth Every Yen

Japanese pharmacies are overwhelming in the best way — walls of skincare, hair treatments, and stomach remedies at prices that make no sense until you try them. These are the ten products that actually earn their place in your luggage.

Why the drugstores are like this

Japanese pharmacy chains — Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Sundrug, Welcia and the rest — occupy a strange middle ground between a chemist and a small department store, and the skincare aisle in particular operates at a standard that is elsewhere reserved for premium brands. The reason is competitive: domestic consumers are unusually informed about ingredients and formulation, and the mass-market brands compete on substance rather than packaging. The practical consequence for a visitor is that the ¥800 product is frequently very good.

Sunscreen, which is the strongest category

If you buy one thing, buy sunscreen. Japanese formulations are widely regarded as the best mass-market sun protection available, largely because the regulatory environment permits UV filters that are elegant, stable and pleasant to wear — which is what actually determines whether people reapply. Expect fluid, weightless textures that sit under makeup without pilling. Look for PA++++ alongside the SPF number, which indicates UVA protection. The well-known gel and milk formats from the major cosmetics houses cost a fraction of what comparable products cost abroad.

Sheet masks and the hydration aisle

Sheet masks are sold in packs of thirty or more at a price that makes the boutique single-mask model look absurd. The large-volume packs from the mainstream brands are the ones locals buy in quantity — unfragranced, simple humectant formulas, meant to be used daily rather than as a treat. Also in this aisle: hatomugi and similar large-bottle lotions, sold in half-litre containers designed to be used generously on face and body. Neither category is glamorous and both are the reason people's luggage comes back heavier.

Cleansing oils and balms

Double cleansing — an oil to remove sunscreen and makeup, then a foaming wash — is standard practice in Japan, and the cleansing oil category reflects decades of refinement. The mass-market oils emulsify cleanly, rinse without residue, and cost very little for a large pump bottle. If you have only used a wipe or a micellar water, this is the single change most likely to be noticeable. Buy the large size; it is heavy but the price per use is the point.

Steam eye masks and the comfort aisle

Self-heating eye masks that warm gently for around ten minutes are a genuinely Japanese invention and difficult to find abroad at a sensible price. They come in boxes, in several scents, and they are the most reliably appreciated small gift on this list. Nearby you will find heat patches for shoulders and lower back, cooling sheets for fever and hot days, and a large selection of foot-care products aimed at people who walk a great deal, which after a week in Japan will include you.

The medicine cabinet items

Beyond cosmetics, a few pharmacy staples are worth knowing about. There are well-established stomach remedies formulated for overeating, sold in sachets and widely used. There are effective liquid bandage products. There are eye drops in a range of formulations that is bewildering by international standards. These are actual medicines: read the packaging, ask the pharmacist, and be aware that some products behind the counter require a brief conversation. If you take prescription medication, check the import rules before you travel, as Japan restricts several compounds that are unremarkable elsewhere.

Tax-free, and how to actually get it

Most large drugstores offer tax-free purchasing above a minimum spend for visitors, and consumables and general goods are counted in separate categories. Bring your physical passport — a photograph is not accepted. Consumable items are sealed in a bag that you are not supposed to open before leaving the country, so buy the things you want to use during the trip separately. The saving is meaningful on a large basket, and the queue at the tax-free counter is usually shorter in the early afternoon.

A note on how to choose

The aisle is overwhelming because almost everything in it is competent, which is a better problem than the alternative. Two shortcuts: the products with the plainest packaging and the largest volume are generally what locals restock rather than what is marketed to visitors, and staff will point you at the right shelf if you show them the back of your hand or say the category name. Buying three products you will actually finish beats buying twelve that look interesting in a photograph.
