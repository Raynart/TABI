10 Japanese Drugstore Products Worth Every Yen

Japanese pharmacies are overwhelming in the best way — walls of skincare, hair treatments, and stomach remedies at prices that make no sense until you try them. These are the ten products that actually earn their place in your luggage.

Why the drugstores are like this

Japanese pharmacy chains — Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Sundrug, Welcia, Tsuruha and the rest — occupy a strange middle ground between a chemist and a small department store, and the skincare aisle in particular operates at a standard that is elsewhere reserved for premium brands. The reason is competitive: domestic consumers are unusually informed about ingredients and formulation, and the mass-market brands compete on substance rather than packaging. The practical consequence for a visitor is that the ¥800 product is frequently very good.

The ten below are categories with a representative product named, rather than a ranking. Formulations get revised and packaging gets redesigned constantly in this market, so treat the names as a starting point to show a member of staff rather than a shopping list to match exactly.

1. Sunscreen

If you buy one thing, buy sunscreen. Japanese formulations are widely regarded as the best mass-market sun protection available, largely because the regulatory environment permits UV filters that are elegant, stable and pleasant to wear — which is what actually determines whether people reapply. Expect fluid, weightless textures that sit under makeup without pilling.

Look for PA++++ alongside the SPF number; the PA rating indicates UVA protection and is the half of the equation most foreign labels omit. The Biore UV range is the cheap and excellent option at a few hundred yen, and Shiseido's Anessa sits above it for a couple of thousand. Both cost a fraction of what comparable products cost abroad.

2. Sheet masks in bulk

Sheet masks are sold in packs of thirty or more at a price that makes the boutique single-mask model look absurd. The large-volume packs from the mainstream brands — Lululun is the one stacked by the doorway of every branch — are what locals buy in quantity: unfragranced, simple humectant formulas, meant to be used daily rather than as a treat.

A box of thirty runs around ¥1,500 and weighs very little. It is the highest value-per-suitcase-kilo item in the shop and the easiest thing to hand out to several people at home.

3. Hydrating lotions

The Japanese 'lotion' is not a moisturiser in the Western sense — it is a watery hydrating layer applied before one, and it is the step that makes the routine work. Hada Labo's Gokujyun hyaluronic lotion is the famous one, viscous and unscented, and Naturie's Hatomugi lotion comes in a half-litre bottle designed to be used generously on face and body.

Neither is glamorous, both cost under a thousand yen, and they are the reason people's luggage comes back heavier than it left.

4. Cleansing oils and balms

Double cleansing — an oil to remove sunscreen and makeup, then a foaming wash — is standard practice in Japan, and the cleansing oil category reflects decades of refinement. The mass-market oils emulsify cleanly, rinse without residue, and cost very little for a large pump bottle. Shu Uemura's is the department-store benchmark; the Biore and Kose Softymo bottles in the drugstore do the same job for a tenth of the price.

If you have only used a wipe or a micellar water, this is the single change most likely to be noticeable. Buy the large size; it is heavy but the price per use is the point.

5. Foaming face wash

The second half of the double cleanse, and the category where the whipped-foam texture Japan is known for actually originates. Shiseido's Senka Perfect Whip is the one every list mentions, costs a few hundred yen, and produces a dense foam from a very small amount.

Buy a foaming net while you are in the aisle. It costs a hundred yen, it is what the instructions on the tube assume you own, and it roughly doubles how far the tube goes.

6. Steam eye masks

Self-heating eye masks that warm gently for around ten minutes are a genuinely Japanese invention and difficult to find abroad at a sensible price. Kao's Megurhythm boxes come in several scents, cost around ¥1,000 for five, and are the most reliably appreciated small gift on this list.

They are also the correct thing to have in your bag on a long-haul flight home, which is a good argument for buying two boxes.

7. Heat patches and pain relief

Adhesive heat pads for shoulders and lower back, and the medicated compresses sold under names like Salonpas, are a staple of Japanese pharmacy shelves and genuinely effective. After a week of walking fifteen kilometres a day they stop being a curiosity and start being a purchase.

In the same aisle: disposable body warmers, sold in packs, which stick inside clothing and stay warm for hours. In winter they are the single cheapest comfort upgrade available in Japan.

8. Foot care

There is an entire wall of it, aimed at people who walk a great deal, which after a week in Japan will include you. Cooling gel sheets that you stick to the soles overnight, blister plasters that are better than anything sold at home, and gel cushions for specific parts of the foot.

The peeling foot masks — plastic booties containing an exfoliating solution, worn for an hour — are the famous novelty. They work, the results are alarming about a week later, and they are not something to do the night before more walking.

9. Stomach remedies

Japan has a long tradition of digestive medicine aimed specifically at having eaten too much, which is a useful thing to have on a food-focused trip. Ohta's Isan is the classic — a mildly minty powder in sachets, sold for over a century — and Kampo-based preparations sit alongside the modern tablets.

These are actual medicines rather than supplements. Read the packaging, and ask the pharmacist if anything is unclear; larger branches have one on duty and some products sit behind the counter and require a brief conversation.

10. Eye drops

The eye drop selection is bewildering by international standards — an entire bay, sorted by symptom, cooling intensity and whether you wear contact lenses. The strongly cooling ones are a specific Japanese taste and are startling the first time.

Worth knowing: some formulations contain vasoconstrictors that are restricted or unavailable in other countries. They are legal to buy and use here; check your own country's rules before bringing a large quantity home.

Tax-free, and how to actually get it

Most large drugstores offer tax-free purchasing to visitors above ¥5,000 in a day at the same store. Bring your physical passport; a photograph is not accepted. Until 31 October 2026 consumables and general goods are counted as separate categories, which occasionally means a basket that looks like it qualifies does not, and consumables leave the shop in a sealed bag. From 1 November 2026 both of those rules end: you pay the full price in store and claim the consumption tax back at the airport, where Customs has to see the goods, so keep them where you can reach them.

Consumable items are sealed in a transparent bag that you are not supposed to open before leaving the country. If you want to use the sunscreen during the trip, buy that bottle separately and pay the tax on it.

The saving is meaningful on a large basket, and the queue at the tax-free counter is usually shortest in the early afternoon.

A note on how to choose

The aisle is overwhelming because almost everything in it is competent, which is a better problem than the alternative. Two shortcuts: the products with the plainest packaging and the largest volume are generally what locals restock rather than what is marketed to visitors, and staff will point you at the right shelf if you show them the back of your hand or say the category name.

Buying three products you will actually finish beats buying twelve that look interesting in a photograph. And if you take prescription medication, check Japan's import rules before you travel — several compounds that are unremarkable elsewhere are restricted here, and that is a border problem rather than a pharmacy one.
