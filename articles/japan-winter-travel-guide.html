Why January Is the Best Month to Visit Japan (Yes, Really)

No cherry blossoms. No 38-degree humidity. No crowds. January is when Japan stops performing for tourists and just exists.

Why even consider January

Summer in Japan is spectacular and exhausting. Cherry blossom season is magical and so crowded that the famous spots feel like theme parks. Autumn foliage is genuinely among the most beautiful things in the natural world and is also, by November, completely overrun. January has none of these problems, because January has a reputation for cold and grey that is only partly deserved.

The reputation comes from the wrong side of the country. Japan's winter splits down the middle: the Sea of Japan coast gets enormous snowfall, and the Pacific side — where Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka are — gets cold, dry, sunny weather. Both are winter. Only one of them is what people picture.

The actual weather

Tokyo in January sits between about 4°C and 10°C during the day, with clear skies more often than you would expect, occasional rain and rare snow. It is not harsh. A good coat and a scarf are enough, and the sunshine hours are among the highest of any month.

Kyoto is a couple of degrees colder and gets occasional snow, which on temple grounds with grey stone and winter moss looks exactly like the photograph you have seen your whole life, except there are almost no other people in it. When Kinkaku-ji has snow on the roof, half of Japan tries to get there — but it lasts a day or two a year and the rest of January is simply quiet.

The genuine trade-offs: daylight is short, with dark falling around five, and some mountain roads and high routes are closed for the season. Dress for indoors as well as out, because trains, shops and restaurants are heated hard and you will be removing layers constantly.

Where the crowds went

The tourist surge disappears after New Year, when domestic travellers return home and the holiday-season international visitors leave. From around January 5th onwards you can visit the famous temples at human scale — the Arashiyama bamboo grove with nobody in your frame is not an exaggeration, it is a cold Tuesday.

The effect on the practical side of a trip is just as large. Restaurants with long waiting lists have tables. The ryokan in Hakone with no rooms in November has rooms, often at a meaningfully lower rate. Flights and hotels both cost less than in October or April.

It is the one month where you can plan a Japan trip loosely and have it work.

New Year, and the first week

The rituals of New Year give early January a purposefulness that ordinary months do not have. Hatsumode, the first shrine visit of the year, fills the major shrines from the 1st to the 3rd and keeps them festive through the first week. Senso-ji in Asakusa at six in the morning on January 2nd is one of the better crowds in the world to be part of.

The complication is closure. A great many restaurants, small shops and museums shut from around December 29 to January 3, and in smaller towns the shutdown is close to total. Convenience stores, department stores and chain restaurants stay open, and the shrine visit is worth doing, but this is not the week for a food-led itinerary.

If you can choose, arrive on about January 4th. You get the tail of the festivity and none of the closures.

Onsen towns are the point

Onsen towns are at their best in winter and this is not a small thing. The specific pleasure of sitting in an outdoor rotenburo with snow falling and steam rising is not available in July. The baths feel hotter, the air is colder, and the meal afterwards is twice as satisfying.

Hakone from Tokyo, Kinosaki from Kyoto, Beppu and Yufuin in Kyushu, Nyuto in Akita for the properly remote version. Kinosaki is the one to know: seven public bathhouses in a small canal town, and the local custom is to walk between them in the yukata and wooden geta your inn provides, which is an entire evening's entertainment.

Book the ryokan first and build the trip around it. This is the month when that is affordable.

Snow country

The Sea of Japan side gets some of the heaviest snowfall of any inhabited place on earth, and it is worth going to see. Shirakawa-go's thatched farmhouses under two metres of snow, lit for a handful of evenings each winter, is the image; the village is reachable by bus from Takayama or Kanazawa and the illumination nights are ticketed and book out months ahead.

The Ginzan Onsen street in Yamagata, gas-lit with snow on the wooden inn facades, is the other one people travel for. Both are extremely popular for a small number of days and empty either side of them.

For skiing, Hokkaido has some of the best powder in the world — Niseko, Furano, Rusutsu — and January is peak season. Hakuba and the Nagano resorts are closer to Tokyo and nearly as good. If skiing is relevant to your trip at all, this is not a consolation prize, it is the main event.

Winter food

Japanese cooking is more seasonal than most, and winter is when the best of it happens. Nabe — a communal hotpot cooked at the table — is the centre of it, in a dozen regional forms. Oden, the simmered pot of daikon, egg and fishcake sold everywhere including convenience stores, is a winter food and tastes like it.

This is also crab season on the Sea of Japan coast, and the entire Hokuriku region reorganises around it from November. A crab kaiseki at a ryokan in Kanazawa or the Tango peninsula is expensive and is one of the great meals available in Japan.

Smaller pleasures: hot canned coffee from a vending machine on a freezing platform, roasted sweet potato sold from a truck, amazake at a shrine, and the fact that every convenience store has a heated cabinet by the till from November.

What winter reveals

What Japan offers in winter that it cannot offer in spring or autumn is something closer to daily life. When a place is over-visited it optimises for visitors; when it is not being watched, it relaxes. January Japan is commuters and schoolchildren and the ramen shop that opens at half past six for the early shift.

February keeps most of the advantages and adds the Sapporo Snow Festival in the first week and, towards the end of the month, the plum blossom — which flowers on bare branches in the cold, gets a fraction of the attention cherry blossom does, and is arguably the more beautiful of the two.

That Japan is worth seeing, and it is more accessible than people think.
