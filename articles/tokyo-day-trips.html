Day Trips From Tokyo That Aren't Hakone or Nikko

Hakone is excellent. Nikko is impressive. Both are in every guidebook and overrun on weekends. There are better options if you're willing to look slightly further.

The problem with the usual recommendations

The standard day-trip recommendations from Tokyo are Hakone (hot springs, Fuji views), Nikko (elaborate Edo-period shrines in the mountains), and Kamakura (giant Buddha, seaside). All three are worth visiting. All three are also predictable, and in peak season they're handling tourist numbers that test their infrastructure. If you've already been to any of them, or if you're planning a second Tokyo visit and want to branch out, there are options that deliver comparable experiences with significantly smaller crowds.

The useful way to choose between what follows is by what you want the day to feel like, not by distance. Kawagoe is a half day of old streets and eating. Chichibu is mountains and a shrine town. Izu is coast and hot water. Mashiko and Sawara are craft and canal towns for people who like a slower pace. Ashikaga is a single spectacular sight and not much else. None of them needs an early start except Mashiko.

Kawagoe — the easy one

Kawagoe, 30 minutes from Ikebukuro on the Tobu Tojo Line (around ¥470). Called Little Edo, it has a preserved merchant district (Kurazukuri Street) with Edo-period clay-walled warehouses that have become antique shops, sweet shops, and cafés. The bell tower that rings every six hours has been doing so since 1624. The Kashiya Yokocho (Candy Lane) alley sells old-fashioned Japanese sweets from small family shops. Significantly fewer international visitors than Kamakura; similar historical atmosphere.

Do not skip Kita-in. The temple holds 540 stone rakan statues, each with a different face and posture — sleeping, laughing, arguing, holding a cat — carved over fifty years in the late 18th century. It also contains rooms moved here from Edo Castle, which makes them the only surviving interiors of a building otherwise lost entirely. The rakan garden is ten minutes' walk from the main street and most day-trippers never reach it.

Kawagoe is the right choice when you have half a day rather than a whole one, or when the weather is uncertain and you want to be back in Tokyo by mid-afternoon. It is also the most walkable of these — everything worth seeing sits within about twenty minutes on foot of everything else.

Chichibu — mountains and a festival

Chichibu, 90 minutes from Ikebukuro on the Seibu Chichibu Line. A mountain town with a famous Shinto shrine (Chichibu Shrine), a series of 34 Buddhist pilgrimage temples that serious walkers circuit over multiple days, and the only natural karst formation in the Kanto region at Ootaki. In December, the Chichibu Night Festival (December 2–3) is one of Japan's largest float festivals and draws 200,000 people — still far more manageable than any equivalent Tokyo-area event.

The other date worth planning around is mid-to-late April, when the shibazakura at Hitsujiyama Park flowers — an entire hillside of moss phlox in pink and white bands with the Chichibu mountains behind it. It is a genuinely photogenic hour and the park is otherwise unremarkable, so check the bloom before committing the trip to it.

Take the Limited Express Laview if the timing works. It is a striking piece of train design with enormous windows, it costs a modest supplement over the standard fare, and the last forty minutes into the mountains are the point of taking it.

Izu Peninsula — coast and hot water

Izu Peninsula, 90 minutes from Tokyo by Shinkansen to Atami or Mishima. A volcanic peninsula with hot springs, dramatic coastline, and fewer tourists per kilometer than anywhere in the Hakone area. Shuzenji in the central valley is a small onsen town with a classic feel — a bamboo grove along the river, wooden onsen hotels, a 9th-century Buddhist temple. Enough for a full day without a crowd problem.

Izu is the one on this list that rewards staying over rather than returning the same evening. The peninsula is large, the coast road south is slow, and the good parts — the Jogasaki cliff walk on the east side, the Kawazu waterfalls inland — are far enough apart that a day trip means picking one. If you can spare a night, an onsen ryokan in Shuzenji is a better version of what people go to Hakone for.

For a day trip specifically, the Odoriko limited express runs direct from Tokyo Station and removes the change at Atami, which is worth the small extra cost for the time it saves at both ends.

Mashiko — for the pottery

Mashiko, two hours from Ueno by train (JR Utsunomiya plus bus). A ceramics town that became famous when the potter Shoji Hamada established a studio there in the 1920s and invited other ceramicists. There are now around 300 kilns in and around the town. The spring and autumn pottery fairs are among the best craft markets in Japan. The Hamada Shoji Memorial Mashiko Sankokan museum is on the original estate and worth two hours. The journey requires planning but the experience is unique.

What makes Mashiko different from buying ceramics in a Tokyo department store is that you buy from the person who made the object, in the building where it was made, usually for less than the city price. Mashiko-yaki is heavy, earthy, thickly glazed stoneware — the deliberate opposite of fine porcelain — and it is the style that the mingei folk-craft movement built its case around. If it does not appeal to you in photographs it will not appeal to you in person.

Go for one of the fairs if you can. They run for about a week around Golden Week in spring and in early November, several hundred potters set up tents along the main road, and the town becomes the best place in the country to buy pots. Outside those weeks it is a quiet town with good shops and a strong museum, which is a different and slower proposition.

Sawara — the canal town

Sawara, in Chiba, roughly 90 minutes from Tokyo on the JR Narita Line. Another town that calls itself Little Edo, and the one that earns it most convincingly: a working canal runs through the old merchant district, willows lean over the water, and the buildings along it are still the original 18th- and 19th-century shops rather than reconstructions. Several are businesses that have been trading continuously for two hundred years.

Sawara was the home of Ino Tadataka, who surveyed the entire Japanese coastline on foot in the early 1800s and produced maps so accurate they were still in use a century later. His house and the museum opposite it are the anchor of a visit, and the story is remarkable even if maps do not usually interest you — he started the project at 55, after retiring from the family sake business.

It pairs naturally with Narita, which is on the same line: the temple complex at Naritasan is large, genuinely old, and almost entirely ignored by the millions of people who pass through the airport a few kilometres away. Two towns in one day is comfortable here.

Ashikaga — one sight, worth the trip

Ashikaga Flower Park, about 90 minutes north of Tokyo, is a single-purpose destination and unapologetic about it. Its great wisteria is over 150 years old, trained flat across a trellis the size of a tennis court, and when it flowers in late April and early May you walk underneath a ceiling of hanging purple. It is the sort of thing that looks manipulated in photographs and is not.

The bloom window is short — roughly the last week of April into the first week of May — and the park publishes its status, which you should check before booking anything. Go on a weekday if the dates allow; this is the one place on this list that gets genuinely crowded, because Japanese visitors come too.

Outside wisteria season the park runs a large winter illumination from October to February, which is good but not a reason to travel ninety minutes. In the months between, skip it.

Practical notes

Check the last train before you commit to a long dinner. These are all comfortable same-day returns, but the last useful service from a rural line can leave earlier than you expect, and the gap between the last convenient train and the actual last train is often three hours of waiting.

The private railways — Tobu to Kawagoe, Seibu to Chichibu — sell day passes bundling the return fare with local buses and small discounts, usually sold at the originating station and usually worth it if you plan to use a bus at the far end. Note that none of these are JR lines, so a JR Pass does not cover them; Izu, Mashiko and Sawara are the JR-served options on this list.

Finally, go on a weekday if you have the choice. Every crowd problem described above is a weekend and public-holiday problem. On a Tuesday in a shoulder month, most of these towns are close to empty.
