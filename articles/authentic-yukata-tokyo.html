Where to Buy Authentic Yukata in Tokyo Without the Markup

The tourist-district yukata sold near Asakusa are fine, but the ones sold at department store basements and specialist shops are extraordinary — and often cheaper. This guide takes you past the obvious options to the places worth visiting.

Yukata is not kimono

A yukata is unlined cotton, worn in summer, at festivals, at fireworks displays and around hot-spring towns, and it is the informal end of Japanese dress. A kimono is lined, usually silk, formal, structurally more complex and considerably more expensive. Conflating the two is the most common mistake visitors make, and it matters commercially: a shop quoting a very low price for a 'kimono' is almost certainly selling a yukata, and a shop quoting a very high price for a yukata is relying on you not knowing the difference.

The word itself is a contraction of yukatabira, a bathing robe, which is exactly what it was — something worn after the bath and, later, out to the summer evening. That history explains its rules: no lining, no under-kimono, bare feet in geta, and no occasion formal enough to require anything more.

What you actually need to buy

A wearable yukata is at minimum three items: the yukata itself, an obi sash, and something to hold the whole thing closed underneath — a koshihimo tie, and ideally a datejime belt. Many shops sell these as a set, which is the sensible way to buy your first one. A yukata bought alone, with no obi, is a garment you will not be able to wear.

Men and women wear different sashes and this is the detail most often got wrong. Men wear a kaku-obi, a narrow stiff belt tied low on the hips in a simple knot. Women wear a hanhaba-obi, considerably wider and tied at the back in a bow that takes practice — which is why pre-tied obi that clip on exist and why nobody will judge you for using one.

Add geta and a kinchaku drawstring bag for the complete look, though neither is essential. Geta are worn barefoot and will give you blisters between the toes on the first outing; the standard local solution is to accept this, and the sensible visitor solution is to walk less far than you planned.

The Asakusa question

The shops clustered around Asakusa and Nakamise are convenient and not a scam. What they are is optimised for a specific transaction: a tourist buying one yukata, quickly, in a place with high rent. The quality is generally acceptable, the printing is often digital rather than dyed, the sizes skew towards the middle, and the price reflects the location.

If you want a yukata this afternoon and are not going to think about it again, this is a perfectly reasonable place to buy one. If you have another day, you can do considerably better for the same money.

The rental shops in the same district are a separate proposition and a good one. For a few thousand yen they dress you properly, tie the obi correctly, do your hair if you want, and take it all back at the end of the day. If what you want is to spend an afternoon in Asakusa wearing one rather than to own one, rent.

Department store basements and upper floors

The major department stores — the ones with a kimono floor rather than a kimono corner — carry proper stock, with a summer yukata section from late spring onwards. The advantages are real: consistent sizing including longer lengths, staff who will fit you properly and show you how to tie the obi, dyed rather than printed fabric at the middle price points, and the ability to buy the components separately.

Prices start higher than Asakusa and the value is better. As a rough map: a printed polyester set runs a few thousand yen, a decent cotton yukata with a good obi sits somewhere around ¥10,000–20,000 as a set, and hand-dyed cotton from a named maker climbs from there.

Ask for the yukata section by name and be prepared to spend half an hour. The fitting is the service you are paying for and it is worth taking; being shown how the collar should sit, once, by someone who does it daily, is more useful than any video.

Fabric, and the polyester question

Traditional yukata is cotton, and the better ones use weaves designed for heat — ro and sha are open, gauzy constructions that move air, and chijimi is a crinkled cotton that holds itself off the skin. In a Japanese August this is not a refinement, it is the difference between comfortable and unbearable.

Polyester is common at the cheap end and it has one genuine advantage: it is machine washable and does not wrinkle, which matters if this is going to be a costume worn twice rather than a garment. It is also noticeably hotter. Choose deliberately rather than by price.

Dyed fabric and printed fabric look different at arm's length once you know: on a dyed yukata the pattern appears on both sides of the cloth, while a print sits on the face only and the reverse is pale. Turn a sleeve over in the shop. It is the single fastest quality check available.

The specialist and secondhand end

Specialist kimono shops, found in the older commercial districts rather than the tourist ones, sell to people who wear these clothes regularly and will happily explain the difference between a printed and a hand-dyed pattern. They can be intimidating to walk into and are almost always welcoming once you do.

Secondhand is where the genuinely interesting fabric is, frequently at a fraction of new prices — the recycled-kimono chains and the antique markets, Oedo in Tokyo and Toji in Kyoto, turn up vintage cotton with patterns nobody prints any more. Check the collar and cuffs for staining, check the underarms, and accept that sizing is whatever it is.

One seasonal warning: yukata is summer stock. Shops carry it from roughly April to August and clear it afterwards, so a visitor in November will find the selection thin everywhere except the secondhand shops, which have it year-round.

Fit, which is mostly about length

Yukata are not tailored to the body in the Western sense; the excess length is folded and held at the waist. What matters is that the garment is long enough — roughly your own height is the standard guide — and that the sleeves fall where you want them.

Visitors taller than about 175 centimetres should specify a long size explicitly, since standard stock often will not work and a too-short yukata is immediately obvious. The tell is the hem: it should fall to the ankle. Shoulder width matters second, and everything else is adjusted by the tying.

Men's yukata are worn at close to their full length with the fold taken up in the tying; women's are folded at the waist into the ohashori, the visible tuck below the obi. If you are buying a woman's yukata that is slightly too long, that is fine and expected. Too short cannot be fixed.

Wearing it without getting the important part wrong

Left side over right. Always. The reverse is how the dead are dressed, and it is the one error that Japanese people will notice instantly and find genuinely uncomfortable. The way to remember it: your right hand should be able to slip into the fold against your chest.

Beyond that: the obi sits at the natural waist for men and slightly higher for women, the collar should sit a little away from the back of the neck rather than tight against it, and small steps are simply how the garment moves. Wearing a yukata as a visitor is welcomed rather than resented, provided the left-over-right rule is observed.

Where to wear one: summer festivals and fireworks displays, which is what they are for and where you will be in good company; and hot-spring towns, where walking between baths in the yukata your ryokan provided is the entire local dress code. Wearing one to a restaurant in Ginza in December is a costume, and will read as one.

Washing and keeping it

Cotton yukata can be hand washed in cool water and should be dried flat or on a wide hanger, never wrung. Polyester goes in the machine on a gentle cycle in a mesh bag. Neither should go anywhere near a tumble dryer.

Store it folded rather than hung — there is a standard fold along the existing seams that keeps the lines crisp, and any shop will demonstrate it in thirty seconds if you ask. Hanging a yukata for a year distorts the shoulders.

Keep the obi rolled rather than folded, and if you have bought geta, expect the thongs to loosen with use; they can be re-tightened, and the shop that sold them will usually do it.
