Getting From the Airport Into the City: Narita, Haneda and Kansai

Three airports, three completely different problems. The wrong choice at Narita costs two hours and the price of a nice dinner, and the decision is easier to make before you land than after.

  3. Kansai, for Kyoto and Osaka

Narita is a long way out

Narita sits about sixty kilometres east of central Tokyo, which is the single fact that governs everything about arriving there. Nothing gets you into the city quickly and cheaply at the same time; you are choosing which of the two to give up.

The Narita Express is the JR option: reserved seats, luggage space, direct to Tokyo Station and then on to Shibuya, Shinjuku and Ikebukuro. It takes roughly an hour to Tokyo Station and is free with a JR Pass, which is the single best argument for activating a pass on arrival day.

The Keisei Skyliner is the fastest to the north-east of the city, reaching Nippori and Ueno in around forty minutes. If you are staying in Ueno, Asakusa or anywhere on the Yamanote's northern arc it is the better choice, and it is cheaper than the Narita Express.

Limousine buses run to most major hotels and take you door to door with your luggage in the hold, which is worth a great deal after a long flight. They are also at the mercy of traffic, so the journey can take anywhere from ninety minutes to considerably more.

The cheapest option is the Keisei Access Express or the ordinary commuter trains, which cost a fraction of the express fares and take somewhat longer. They are entirely usable, and they are standing-room-only trains with no luggage racks, which is a different proposition with two large suitcases.

Haneda is close, and that changes the calculation

Haneda is inside Tokyo, about fifteen kilometres from the centre. Everything from there is quick and inexpensive, and the decision hardly matters.

The Tokyo Monorail runs to Hamamatsucho and connects to the Yamanote line. The Keikyu line runs to Shinagawa and continues through to Asakusa and beyond without a change, which makes it the better option for a lot of destinations. Both take around twenty minutes to reach the edge of the centre.

If you have a choice of airports when booking, Haneda is worth paying a modest premium for. The difference in the arrival experience is substantial, and it is the difference between being in your hotel an hour after clearing customs and being in your hotel three hours after.

Kansai, for Kyoto and Osaka

Kansai International sits on an artificial island south of Osaka. For Osaka itself, the Nankai Rapi:t and the airport express trains reach Namba in around forty minutes.

For Kyoto, the Haruka limited express runs direct in roughly seventy-five minutes and is covered by the JR Pass. This is a genuinely useful connection, because it means a visitor can land at Kansai and be in Kyoto without crossing Osaka at all.

There are also buses to both cities, which are cheaper and slower, and the usual airport limousine services to the larger hotels.

The taxi question

A metered taxi from Narita into central Tokyo is extremely expensive — a five-figure fare — and it is not faster than the train except in the narrow case of arriving after the trains have stopped. Fixed-fare airport taxis exist and are cheaper than the meter, but still cost several times the train.

From Haneda a taxi is merely expensive rather than absurd, and can make sense late at night or with a family and a lot of luggage. From Kansai the same logic applies as at Narita: the distance makes it a poor choice.

The exception in every case is the last-train problem. Narita and Kansai both effectively close in the late evening, and an arrival delayed past that leaves a choice between an expensive taxi and an airport hotel. If your flight lands after about ten at night, work out in advance which one you are doing.

What to sort out before you leave the terminal

Buy an IC card, or set one up on your phone before you fly. It removes every subsequent ticket-buying decision for the rest of the trip.

Get cash. Airport ATMs accept foreign cards reliably, which is not true of every ATM in the country, and there are still places that take nothing else.

Sort out data. An eSIM activated before you fly is the least effort; the airport counters work but involve a queue at the exact moment you least want one.

And consider sending your luggage. The courier counters in the arrivals hall will forward a suitcase to your hotel, usually for next-day delivery, for a modest fee. Crossing Tokyo on a rush-hour train with two large cases is genuinely unpleasant, and this is the standard local solution to it.

A note on the return journey

Leaving is the direction where people misjudge the time. Narita in particular asks for a lot of buffer: the journey out, the check-in queues, and the security and immigration lines all take longer than the equivalent at a European or American airport of similar size.

The conventional advice is to be at Narita three hours before an international departure, and it is not padding. Work backwards from that to choose your train, and pick the service before the one that would just about do it.
