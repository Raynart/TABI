The Convenience Store Is Japan's Greatest Culinary Achievement

Seven-Eleven in Japan is not Seven-Eleven anywhere else. This distinction matters and deserves your full attention.

This is not convenience store food

The first thing to understand about konbini is that the food is genuinely good. Not good for a convenience store. Just good. The onigiri is made fresh and delivered several times a day. The sandwiches are produced to specifications refined over decades. The hot food meets a standard most fast-casual restaurants elsewhere do not.

The reason is competition and turnover. Three national chains fight over the same corners, products are dropped if they underperform for a few weeks, and stores take deliveries around the clock, so almost nothing sits. The result is a supply chain that behaves like a restaurant kitchen operating at national scale.

The three chains are not the same

7-Eleven is generally reckoned the strongest on food overall, and its egg sandwich and onigiri are the ones people write about. Its ATMs are also the most reliable for foreign cards.

Lawson runs a premium bakery line and the Natural Lawson variant aimed at healthier eating, and its fried chicken is a genuine rival to the others. Family Mart's fried chicken has been the subject of national argument, and its own-brand clothing — plain socks and t-shirts in a distinctive colourway — became an unexpected fashion item.

The differences are small and real, and the practical advice is to try the same category at all three in the first week and pick a side. Everyone in Japan has one.

What to eat

Onigiri: rice with a filling — tuna mayonnaise, pickled plum, salmon, kombu — wrapped in a three-panel system that keeps the seaweed crisp until you open it. The opening sequence is printed on the wrapper with numbered arrows. Follow them. Attempting it another way produces rice everywhere and no dignified explanation.

The egg salad sandwich has developed a following that crosses borders: thick-cut egg, slightly sweet, in the particular soft white bread that Japan does and nowhere else quite manages. Buy one on the first morning and the question of why people travel here for food starts to answer itself.

Hot food lives by the register. Oden in winter — a simmering pot of daikon, fishcake, egg and tofu, chosen with tongs and counted onto your bill. Steamed buns in the heated case. Fried chicken on a stick. In summer the same case sells cold noodles instead.

Also reliable: the pasta and bento in the chilled cabinet, the sweets — the chains take dessert seriously and the seasonal ones are genuinely good — and the fruit sandwiches, which sound wrong and are not.

Drinks

Canned coffee is a category of human achievement. Around ¥130 a can, cold or hot depending on the season, developed through research cycles most car companies would find excessive. The hot can on a November morning is one of those sensory memories travel writers try to describe and never manage.

The tea wall is the other thing to understand: unsweetened green tea, barley tea and oolong in every size, which is why nobody here carries a water bottle. If you want something sweet you have to look for it.

Alcohol is sold in every store, cold, at any hour the licence allows — beer, chuhai in a hundred fruit flavours, small bottles of sake and whisky. Drinking in public is legal, which makes the konbini beer on a park bench an entirely normal evening.

The services are the real point

Konbini are infrastructure. The 7-Bank ATMs take foreign cards, run around the clock and have English menus, and they are the single best place to withdraw cash in Japan.

The multifunction copier does more than it looks: it prints documents from a USB stick or your phone, scans, and sells tickets to concerts, football and some attractions. The counter ships luggage nationwide through the courier services, sells stamps, takes parcel returns and pays utility bills.

They also sell almost anything you have forgotten — chargers, umbrellas, shirts, ties, underwear, phone cables, plasters, batteries. At eleven at night this is the thing you will be most grateful for.

Larger branches offer tax-free above the usual threshold, and most have a lavatory that they will let you use without buying anything.

How to actually order

Budget ¥500–800 for a full meal. Pay at the register, where two questions will be asked. 'Atatamemasu ka' — shall I warm this up — and yes is nearly always the answer for anything from the chilled cabinet. And whether you want a bag, which now costs a few yen; 'fukuro wa irimasen' declines it.

Chopsticks, spoons and straws are handed over as needed and you will be asked how many. Wet wipes come with hot food.

Do not eat inside the store. There is usually a small counter with stools, or a space outside, and if there is not, eat by the entrance rather than walking away with it. The bins by the door are for things bought there, and they are one of the few public bins in the country.

The seasonal calendar

The shelves change with the year in a way that is worth noticing. Sakura-flavoured everything in March. Cold noodles and shaved ice in July and August. Chestnut and sweet potato from September. Oden and steamed buns from November. Christmas cake and fried chicken in late December, then New Year osechi boxes ordered in advance.

Regional exclusives exist and are worth checking wherever you are — chains stock local flavours that do not travel, and it is the cheapest way to eat something specific to a prefecture.

Limited-edition items are genuinely limited, often a few weeks. If you like something, buy it again the next day rather than assuming it will be there at the end of the trip.
