Buying Matcha: Ceremonial vs. Culinary Grade Explained

The matcha market is flooded with products that taste nothing like what you drink in a tea ceremony — and the price tag is not a reliable guide. Here's how to read labels, what to taste for, and where to buy the real thing.

What matcha actually is

Matcha is powdered green tea from leaves that were shaded for several weeks before harvest. The shading starves the plant of light, which raises the amino acid content — particularly theanine — and lowers bitterness, producing the sweetness and savoury depth that distinguishes good matcha. The leaves are then steamed, dried, destemmed and deveined into a form called tencha, and stone-ground into powder. That grinding is slow, and it is a large part of why the good stuff costs what it does.

Slow is not a figure of speech. A granite mill turns at a low speed and produces something in the region of forty grams of matcha an hour, because faster grinding generates heat and heat destroys the aroma. A single tin represents most of a day on one stone.

This also explains why matcha behaves unlike other tea. You are not brewing leaves and discarding them; you are drinking the whole leaf in suspension. That is why the caffeine hits differently, why the quality of the leaf is so exposed, and why it goes stale so fast.

Ceremonial and culinary are marketing terms

Neither phrase is a regulated grade. There is no certifying body and no legal definition, and a tin labelled ceremonial can be almost anything. What the words usually gesture at is real, though: 'ceremonial' generally means an earlier harvest intended to be whisked with water and drunk alone, and 'culinary' generally means a later harvest with more bitterness, meant to survive being mixed with milk and sugar.

Note that these are largely export-market terms. Japanese producers grade their own output with a house hierarchy and give blends poetic names rather than sorting them into two bins, and a tin bought in a Kyoto tea shop will frequently carry no grade word at all. That absence is not a bad sign.

Treat the label as a hint about intended use rather than a statement of quality, and buy for what you will actually do with it. Whisking an expensive tin into a latte with syrup wastes the thing you paid for; drinking a culinary grade straight will convince you that you dislike matcha.

What to look for instead

Four things carry more information than the grade name. Colour: vivid, almost fluorescent green means shaded leaf and careful handling; dull, yellowish or olive powder means age, oxidation or lower-grade leaf. Origin: Uji in Kyoto, Nishio in Aichi, and Yame in Fukuoka are the established regions and reputable producers say so on the tin. Harvest or milling date, because matcha degrades quickly once ground. And packaging: opaque, airtight and small. A large clear jar is a bad sign regardless of price.

Texture is the fifth, if you get the chance. Good matcha is talc-fine and clings to the side of a scoop; coarser powder feels gritty between the fingers and will not suspend properly no matter how hard you whisk.

Smell it if the shop allows. Fresh, high-grade matcha has a sweet, marine, almost nori-like aroma. Anything smelling flat, hay-like or faintly of dust has been on a shelf too long.

Price is a weak signal on its own

Cost tells you something but not enough. Very cheap matcha — the sort sold in large pouches at a suspiciously low price per gram — is almost always late-harvest and will taste flatly bitter no matter how you prepare it. But expensive matcha in an ornate presentation box is frequently priced for gift-giving rather than for what is inside.

The most reliable purchases sit in the middle: a modest 20 to 40 gram tin from a named producer, bought somewhere with turnover. As a rough anchor, a good drinking matcha runs somewhere around ¥1,500–3,000 for 20–40g, and the tins climbing past ¥5,000 are competition-grade blends whose distinctions are genuinely subtle.

Buying the smallest tin of the better grade beats buying the largest tin of the cheaper one, every time. You will not finish 200 grams before it fades.

Usucha and koicha

There are two preparations and shops assume you know which you want. Usucha, thin tea, is the everyday version: about two grams whisked with sixty or seventy millilitres of water into a foam. Koicha, thick tea, uses roughly double the powder in half the water and is whisked into something closer to warm paint — it is the centrepiece of a formal tea gathering and it is intense.

Koicha requires the top grades, because at that concentration any bitterness is unbearable. If a shop asks whether you want a koicha-grade tea and you are making lattes at home, say no; you are being offered the expensive thing.

A tin sold for usucha will make perfectly good koicha-adjacent tea if you want to experiment, but the reverse is the expensive mistake.

The equipment, and whether you need it

You need a bowl, a bamboo whisk and ideally a small sieve. The whisk — chasen — is the one item with no real substitute: it is carved from a single piece of bamboo into eighty or a hundred tines, and a milk frother produces a different, coarser foam. They are consumable, lasting perhaps a few months of daily use, and cost very little in Japan compared with abroad.

The bowl matters less than people think and is where the money goes if you let it. Any wide bowl works. A chashaku, the bamboo scoop, is charming and a teaspoon does the same job. The sieve is the item everyone skips and the one that most improves the result.

Starter sets containing bowl, whisk, scoop and a small tin are sold everywhere and make an excellent gift — the whole thing is light, packs flat with the bowl wrapped, and is a considerably better present than the tin alone. Buy a spare whisk while you are there.

Making it well enough to tell the difference

Sift about two grams to break the clumps, which is the step everyone skips and the one that causes most bad results. Add roughly 60 to 70 millilitres of water at around 80°C — not boiling, which scalds the powder and makes it harsh. Whisk briskly in a W motion, from the wrist rather than the arm, until a fine even foam forms across the surface.

Good matcha at this point is sweet and thick with a savoury finish. If it is simply bitter, the problem is usually the water temperature or the tea, in that order. If it is gritty at the bottom, you did not sift.

Drink it straight away. Matcha separates within a couple of minutes and the foam is part of the texture, which is why it is served immediately and drunk in three or four mouthfuls rather than sipped.

Where to buy

Established tea houses are the best option and many will let you taste before buying, which settles the question faster than any label. In Kyoto, the old houses around Uji are the obvious destination and worth the short train ride — several have tasting rooms and cafés attached, and Uji makes a good half-day out of the city.

Department store basement food halls carry good producers with reliable stock rotation. Specialist tea shops in any major city are generally trustworthy. Souvenir shops at stations and airports are where old stock goes to be sold in decorative boxes, and the difference in the cup is immediately obvious.

If matcha turns out not to be your thing, the same shops sell gyokuro, the shaded leaf tea matcha's leaf would otherwise have become, and hojicha, roasted and nearly caffeine-free. Both travel better and neither requires equipment.

Getting it home and keeping it

Matcha's enemies are air, light, heat and moisture, and it declines noticeably within a few weeks of opening. Buy small quantities rather than one large tin, keep it sealed, and store it in the refrigerator once opened — letting the tin come back to room temperature before opening, so condensation does not form inside.

Unopened tins travel fine in either checked or hand luggage and weigh almost nothing. If you are bringing back gifts, buy them last and keep them out of a hot car.

One thing to know before you buy for someone else: an unopened tin keeps for a year or so, but the clock on the good stuff started at milling, not at purchase. If there is a date on the tin, the gift is better given sooner.
