Japanese Toilets: The Control Panel, and Where to Find One

Heated seats, a panel of unlabelled buttons, and public facilities that are free, everywhere and clean. The toilets are one of the things visitors talk about most and prepare for least.

They are free, and they are everywhere

Public toilets in Japan are free, numerous and generally clean, which is not the case in most countries and is worth planning around in the opposite direction to usual: you do not need to find a café and buy something.

Train stations all have them. Convenience stores almost all have them and will let you use them without buying anything, though buying something is a decent gesture. Department stores have excellent ones, often on every floor. Parks, temples and shopping centres all have them.

This changes how a day of walking works. The constraint most travellers plan around simply is not a constraint here.

The control panel

The washlet — the electronic bidet seat — is standard in homes, hotels and most public buildings. The panel is either on the wall or on the arm of the seat, and on older units it is entirely in Japanese.

The two large buttons in the middle are the important ones: one is a rear wash, one is a front wash intended for women. The button that stops everything is usually the largest, often square, often red or orange, and marked with a symbol resembling a stop sign.

The rest adjust water pressure, water position, temperature and seat heating, and some have a dryer and a deodoriser.

The one button that catches people out is flush, which is frequently not on the panel at all. It may be a lever on the cistern, a separate wall panel, a sensor you wave a hand at, or a button marked with a large character. If nothing on the electronic panel flushes, look on the wall.

Squat toilets still exist

Older stations, parks, temples and rural facilities often have squat toilets, sometimes alongside Western ones. Cubicles are usually marked with a diagram so you can choose.

The orientation is the part visitors get wrong: you face the raised hood end, not away from it.

They are becoming less common but they have not disappeared, and a station with only squat toilets is entirely possible in the countryside.

The things that are missing

Paper towels and hand dryers are frequently absent. Japanese people carry a small handkerchief or hand towel for exactly this, and it is one of the more useful habits to adopt on arrival — they are sold everywhere and cost very little.

Bins are also often absent, in line with the general scarcity of public bins.

Toilet paper is provided in the cubicle and is designed to dissolve, so it goes in the bowl rather than a bin — the opposite of the convention in several other countries.

In some older or rural facilities there may be no soap. A small bottle of hand sanitiser covers it.

The sounds

Many toilets in women's facilities have a button or sensor that plays a flushing sound on a loop. This is the otohime, the sound princess, and it exists to mask the sounds of use — a practice that previously involved flushing repeatedly and wasting a great deal of water.

It is a button worth recognising so that pressing it is a choice rather than a surprise.

Finding one when you need one

Maps applications list public toilets and it is worth searching rather than guessing. Convenience stores are the reliable default in a city and are never far away.

On trains, the Shinkansen and most limited express services have toilets; ordinary commuter trains and subways do not, though the stations do. Plan around the station rather than the train.

In older stations the toilets are frequently inside the ticket gates rather than outside, which matters if you have not yet bought a ticket.

Department stores are the best facilities in any Japanese city — clean, spacious, well equipped and never busy on the upper floors.

Slippers, which is the one real trap

In any building where you have removed your shoes — a ryokan, a temple, a traditional restaurant, a private home — there will be a separate pair of slippers inside the toilet room.

Change into them on the way in and, crucially, change out of them on the way out. Walking back into a tatami room wearing the toilet slippers is the single most reliable mistake visitors make, and it is immediately visible to everyone.

It happens to almost everybody once. Nobody will say anything, and you will notice about four steps later.

Onsen, pools and the toilets attached to them

Bathing facilities have their own arrangement: the toilet is outside the changing room rather than inside the bathing area, and there are slippers for the trip.

Public baths and onsen frequently have older facilities than the rest of the building, and squat toilets are more common in them than elsewhere.

Take a small towel with you everywhere in a bathing complex; it does duty for washing, drying and covering, and there will be nothing provided at the basin.

Accessible and family facilities

Multipurpose cubicles are widespread and well equipped, with space for a wheelchair, a fold-down changing table, and often a small seat for a child.

Baby changing facilities are common in both men's and women's toilets in newer buildings, though not universally in older ones.

Department stores and larger stations are the reliable places to find a fully equipped facility.
