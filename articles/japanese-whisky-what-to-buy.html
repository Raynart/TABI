Japanese Whisky: What's Worth Buying and What's Just Marketing

The golden age is over but the whisky is still exceptional. You just need to know what you're actually looking at.

The golden age and the scarcity

Somewhere around 2014, the world decided Japanese whisky was the best in the world. A Suntory expression won a prominent international award, whisky writers made pilgrimages to Nikka's distilleries, and within two years you could not find a bottle of Yamazaki single malt at anything resembling its old shelf price. By 2020 you frequently could not find it at all.

What happened is straightforward supply arithmetic. Japanese distilleries had been producing at a domestic-consumption pace for decades, and whisky takes twelve or eighteen years to become a twelve or eighteen year old. Global demand arrived instantly; the stock to meet it had needed to be laid down in the 1990s, when nobody was buying. The aged expressions ran dry and the secondary market went strange.

The distilleries kept working throughout, and the whisky filled in the boom years is now coming of age. Availability is slowly improving, age statements are creeping back onto labels, and the frenzy has cooled enough that ordinary shops have stock again. It is a better time to buy than any point in the last decade.

What actually makes it Japanese

The style descends directly from Scotch — the founders trained in Scotland — but the industry evolved differently in one structural way. Scottish distilleries trade casks with each other constantly, so a blender can source a hundred component whiskies. Japanese houses historically did not trade, so each had to produce every character it needed in-house. That forced single distilleries to install multiple still shapes, multiple yeast strains and multiple cask types, and it is why one Japanese distillery can taste like several.

The cask that gets talked about is mizunara, Japanese oak. It is difficult wood — porous, prone to leaking, awkward to coop — and it gives long-aged whisky a distinctive sandalwood and incense character. It is used sparingly and it is genuinely different from anything in Scotland.

The house style overall runs cleaner and more precise than most Scotch, with less emphasis on peat and more on balance. Whether that is better is a matter of taste; that it is a coherent style is not.

The labelling rule, and why it matters

Until recently, 'Japanese whisky' meant nothing legally. Some producers were importing bulk Scottish or Canadian spirit, blending and bottling it in Japan, and selling it with Mt. Fuji on the label. It was not illegal and it was not disclosed.

A labelling standard introduced in 2021 fixed this for members of the industry association: to be called Japanese whisky, the spirit must be fermented, distilled, matured for at least three years and bottled in Japan, using water sourced in Japan. Producers were given a transition period to sell through old stock, which has now passed.

In practice this means you should read two phrases carefully. 'Japanese Whisky' now means what it says. 'World Blended Whisky', or a label that avoids the phrase entirely while using Japanese imagery, generally indicates imported spirit in the blend. Some of those are perfectly good drinks. They are just not what most people think they are buying, and they should not cost what Japanese whisky costs.

What is genuinely worth buying

Nikka From The Barrel is the standing recommendation and has been for years: a blend bottled at high strength in a squat little bottle, sold for a few thousand yen, and better than a great deal of whisky at three times the price. If you buy one bottle, buy this one.

Suntory Kakubin — the yellow-label blend in the hatch-patterned bottle — is what Japan actually drinks, costs very little, and exists to be made into highballs. Suntory Toki is its slightly more refined sibling aimed at the same job. Neither is a sipping whisky and neither is pretending to be.

In the middle, Nikka's Coffey Grain and Coffey Malt are unusual and worth the money: distilled in a continuous still of a type almost nobody else uses for malt, giving a soft, sweet, almost pastry-like whisky. Above that, any Yamazaki, Hakushu, Yoichi or Miyagikyo expression at close to list price is worth taking, and the no-age-statement bottlings are far better value than the aged ones.

The craft distilleries are the interesting part

The more rewarding shopping now is below the two big houses. A wave of small distilleries has come of age in the last few years, and their whisky is arriving at drinkable maturity just as the giants remain constrained.

Chichibu, in Saitama, is the one that made the category's reputation — small, meticulous, and released under the Ichiro's Malt name at prices that reflect how sought-after it has become. Mars Shinshu, high in the Nagano mountains, releases Komagatake expressions that are not as famous as Yamazaki and are much closer in quality than the price gap suggests. Kanosuke in Kagoshima, Akkeshi in Hokkaido and Nagahama in Shiga are all worth a punt if a shop is pointing at them.

These bottles are the ones you cannot easily buy at home, which is the actual test of a souvenir. A Yamazaki 12 is available in any good bottle shop in London or New York. A single-cask release from a distillery with two stills is not.

The highball is the format

Japanese whisky culture is built around the highball — whisky over ice with soda, in a tall glass — and it is not a compromise. It is the default way whisky is drunk here, it is what Kakubin exists for, and it is the correct thing to order with fried food.

The technique is taken seriously, which is why a bar highball tastes better than yours. The glass and the whisky are chilled first, the ice is a single dense block or large cubes rather than small ones, the soda is poured down a bar spoon to preserve carbonation, and it is stirred once. Never more than once.

Order one at a decent bar before you commit to a bottle. It calibrates your sense of what you are buying better than any tasting note, and it costs the price of a beer.

Where to buy, and visiting

Department store basement floors and specialist liquor shops have the best selection and the fairest prices. Large discount chains sometimes turn up bargains on the standard blends. Airport duty free is convenient but the range has narrowed since the shortage and the prices are no longer obviously better.

Avoid hotel gift shops entirely, and be sceptical of any shop in a tourist district displaying rare bottles in a lit cabinet — that is secondary-market pricing aimed at people who will not check.

The distilleries themselves welcome visitors, and Yamazaki, Hakushu, Yoichi and Miyagikyo all run tours with tastings. All of them require booking well in advance, often months, and the distillery-only bottlings in their shops are among the few genuinely scarce things you can buy at a fair price. If you know your dates, book before you book anything else.
