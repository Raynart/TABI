How to Watch a Sumo Tournament Without Watching It on TV

Six tournaments a year, three of them in Tokyo. Tickets go on sale months in advance and sell out. Here's how to actually get in.

Tournament schedule and cities

Sumo holds six official tournaments (basho) per year, each lasting 15 days. The January, May, and September basho are in Tokyo at Ryogoku Kokugikan. The March basho is in Osaka, July in Nagoya, and November in Fukuoka. If you're planning a trip around attending a tournament, knowing this schedule is step one. January and September in Tokyo are typically the most accessible for international visitors because they don't overlap with Golden Week or summer school holidays.

Tournaments always start on a Sunday and end on a Sunday. The two weekends and the final few days are the hardest tickets; a Tuesday or Wednesday in the first week is the easiest, and the sumo is no worse. If your dates are flexible at all, aim mid-week and early.

Outside the six tournaments there is nothing to watch competitively, which catches people out — there is no weekly fixture list. If your trip falls between basho, the regional exhibition tours (jungyo) travel the country in the gaps, and morning practice at a stable is the other option.

Getting tickets

The Japan Sumo Association sells official tickets at Ticket Pia starting roughly two months before the tournament. The best seats (masu-seki, the box seats on the ground floor) sell out in days. What's available to visitors with short notice: elevated arena seats (isu-seki), which start around ¥3,800 for the upper tiers, and same-day door tickets (¥2,200 for the cheap seats) that go on sale at the venue from 8am. The door ticket strategy works — arrive at Ryogoku at 7:30am and you will get in.

The same-day tickets are the answer for most visitors and they are worth understanding properly. A limited number go on sale each morning, one per person, cash, at the box office on the east side of the building. On a quiet weekday you can turn up at 8am and walk in. On a final weekend, or if a popular wrestler is having a good tournament, the queue starts before dawn.

Resale sites carry tickets at multiple times face value. This is legal for some and not for others, and the Association actively discourages it. Given that a same-day seat costs ¥2,200 and the sumo is identical from every seat in the building, paying ¥15,000 to a reseller is a poor trade.

Which seat to actually buy

Masu-seki are the square boxes on the floor, sold as a unit for four people, and they are the iconic sumo seat: you sit on flat cushions in a railed-off square, with food and drink brought to you. They are also the least comfortable seat in the building for anyone not used to sitting on the floor for several hours, and the four-person pricing means a couple pays for two seats they will not use.

Isu-seki are ordinary chairs on the raised tiers above. They have backs, they have legroom, and the view of the ring is unobstructed and often better than the floor because you are looking down into it rather than across it. For a first tournament this is the correct purchase.

The front-row floor seats — tamari-seki, the ones directly against the ring where the audience sits in formal dress — are not generally sold to the public and come with a real hazard: wrestlers land in them regularly. This happens several times per tournament and is one reason the seats are what they are.

What you are watching

Sumo matches are short. The actual bout can last anywhere from two seconds to two minutes. The bulk of the time in a sumo tournament is ritual — the ring-entering ceremony (dohyo-iri) for the highest-ranked wrestlers, salt throwing, the slow psychological confrontation before the wrestlers crouch and charge. Understanding that the ceremony is the event, not the prelude to it, changes everything about how to watch.

Sumo is a Shinto practice as much as a sport, and the ring is treated as sacred ground. The salt is purification, thrown by the handful by the upper divisions and in visible quantity. The stamping is to drive out evil from the ring. The suspended roof above the dohyo is a shrine roof, and the four coloured tassels hanging from it represent the seasons and the compass directions.

The staring is not theatre either. The two wrestlers crouch, touch the ground, rise, retreat to their corners, and repeat this several times. They are timing each other. The charge (tachiai) is only legitimate when both men commit simultaneously and unprompted — there is no starting signal, and matching that instant is a genuine skill. A bout is frequently decided in the first half second of contact.

How a bout is won

The rules are simple enough to learn in a sentence: you lose if any part of your body other than the soles of your feet touches the ground, or if you step or are pushed out of the straw ring. That is the whole of it.

What is not simple is the vocabulary. There are eighty-odd recognised winning techniques (kimarite), and the announcer names the one used after each bout. Four cover most of what you will see: yorikiri (a forward drive out while gripping the belt), oshidashi (a push out with no grip), hatakikomi (a slap-down as the opponent lunges), and uwatenage (an over-arm throw). Recognising those four is enough to follow the day.

The belt — the mawashi — is the strategic centre of the sport. A wrestler who gets both hands on his opponent's belt in a favoured grip has usually already won, and much of the apparent stalemate in a longer bout is two men fighting over grip rather than position.

The ranks, and why the last day matters

The top division is makuuchi. Within it, from the top: yokozuna (grand champion), ozeki, sekiwake, komusubi, and then the numbered maegashira ranks. Below makuuchi sits juryo, and below that the unsalaried divisions whose wrestlers also serve as attendants to the ranked men.

Each wrestler in the top divisions fights fifteen bouts, one per day. Eight wins is kachikoshi — a winning record, which means promotion or at least safety. Seven or fewer is makekoshi and means demotion. This is why the final days carry so much weight: a large share of the field arrives at day 13 or 14 sitting on seven wins, with a career-relevant result riding on a bout lasting four seconds.

The rank list itself, the banzuke, is a calligraphed document published before each tournament, with name size scaling to rank. It is sold at the venue for a few hundred yen and is the best souvenir in the building.

When to arrive

The day begins at 8am with the lowest-ranked wrestlers, and the audience trickles in as the day progresses. The top-division matches (makuuchi) begin around 3pm and end around 6pm. Most visitors arrive around 2pm, which is correct. The atmosphere builds through the afternoon. The final few bouts of the day — particularly if a yokozuna or ozeki is involved in a high-stakes match — generate a noise level that is genuinely startling.

There is an argument for arriving much earlier at least once. In the morning the hall is nearly empty, you can sit close, the young wrestlers in the lower divisions fight with obvious desperation because their careers depend on it, and nothing is ceremonial because nobody is watching. It is a completely different event from the same building at five in the afternoon.

The two moments not to miss in the afternoon: the makuuchi dohyo-iri around 3:40pm, when the whole top division enters in embroidered ceremonial aprons, and the yokozuna's own entrance immediately after, performed alone with two attendants.

Food, drink, and the building

The basement of Kokugikan has chankonabe, the stew traditionally eaten by sumo wrestlers. It is not a gimmick. It's good stew, with chicken and vegetables in a clear dashi broth, and it costs around ¥1,000. There are also yakitori stalls and beer vendors. Food at the masu boxes is served in bento-style sets and can be ordered in advance with tickets.

The yakitori is worth a note of its own: it is made in a kitchen in the basement of the stadium itself, and it is genuinely good. Eating and drinking through the afternoon is entirely normal — this is not an event where the audience sits in silence.

There is also a small sumo museum on the ground floor, free with a ticket, and the souvenir shops sell everything from banzuke to hand towels printed with individual wrestlers' names. Arrive an hour before you plan to sit down and there is plenty to occupy it.

If you can't get a ticket: morning practice

Some sumo stables (heya) in the Ryogoku area allow visitors to watch morning training, and it is arguably a better experience than a tournament day. Practice starts around 6am, you sit on the floor a few metres from wrestlers colliding at full speed in a small room, and there is no crowd and no ceremony.

The conditions are strict and non-negotiable: absolute silence, no photography unless expressly permitted, no leaving mid-session, formal-ish clothing, and no talking to the wrestlers. You are in someone's home and workplace. Most stables require arrangement in advance, often through a hotel concierge or a specialist tour, and several no longer accept visitors at all.

Between tournaments this is the only sumo available, and even during a basho the stables train in the mornings. If your dates miss the basho entirely, this is the thing to arrange instead of writing sumo off.

What surprises you in person

The wrestlers are larger than you expect. The ring is smaller than you expect. The speed of the bouts is faster than you expect. These three surprises reliably improve the live experience over whatever you were imagining from television coverage.

The fourth is the noise. Between bouts the hall is unusually quiet for a sports venue, and the sound of two very large men colliding carries to the back row without amplification. Television compresses it into nothing. In the building it is the thing you remember.
