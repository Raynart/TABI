The Yaeyama Islands: Closer to Taiwan Than to Tokyo

Four hundred kilometres beyond Okinawa's main island lies a group where the jungle reaches the water, the villages have coral walls and sand streets, and the night sky is the darkest in Japan.

Where this actually is

The Yaeyama Islands sit at the far south-western end of the Japanese archipelago, several hundred kilometres past Okinawa's main island and considerably closer to Taiwan than to any Japanese city. Yonaguni, the westernmost, is about a hundred kilometres from the Taiwanese coast.

Getting there means flying to Ishigaki, either direct from Tokyo and Osaka or connecting through Naha. From Ishigaki, a network of fast ferries reaches the other islands, most in under an hour.

This is a genuine journey rather than a side trip, and the reward for it is a part of Japan that looks and feels nothing like the rest of the country.

Ishigaki, the base

Ishigaki is the largest island and the transport hub, with the airport, the ferry terminal, the hotels and the restaurants. It has good beaches, a mountain in the north, and Kabira Bay — a bay of white sand and improbably coloured water where swimming is prohibited because of the currents and the pearl cultivation, so it is admired from the shore and by glass-bottomed boat.

The town has a proper night scene of izakaya serving Ishigaki beef, which is a wagyu of serious quality, and the local seafood. The public market is small and good.

Most visitors base here and day-trip outward by ferry, which is the sensible arrangement unless you want to stay on the quieter islands.

Taketomi, and the village that has not changed

Taketomi is a small flat island ten minutes by ferry from Ishigaki, and its single village is the reason to go: single-storey houses with red tiled roofs, low coral stone walls, and streets of white sand that residents rake every morning. Shisa lion figures sit on every roof.

It is a preserved district and the preservation is real rather than staged — people live there under a community agreement about how buildings are maintained. Water buffalo carts carry visitors through the lanes while a driver plays the sanshin, which sounds like a tourist confection and is oddly affecting.

The island can be walked or cycled in a couple of hours. The great majority of visitors come on a day trip and leave in the afternoon, so staying the night in one of the small guesthouses gives you an empty village, and a sky with no light pollution at all.

Iriomote, which is mostly jungle

Iriomote is the second largest island in Okinawa prefecture and roughly ninety per cent of it is subtropical rainforest and mangrove. There is one road, running partway round the coast, and no way across the interior except on foot or by river.

The activities are kayaking up the mangrove rivers, walking to the waterfalls at Pinaisara and Mariyudu, and boat trips up the Urauchi river. Guides are compulsory for some routes and strongly advisable for the rest; the interior is genuinely wild and people do get lost in it.

The island has its own wildcat, the Iriomote yamaneko, of which perhaps a hundred remain. You will not see one. Road signs everywhere ask drivers to slow down for them, and the whole island is a UNESCO natural World Heritage site.

Hoshizuna beach — star sand, made of the shells of tiny foraminifera shaped like stars — is on the northern coast.

The smaller ones

Kohama is small, hilly and has a famous view along a track through sugar cane fields. Kuroshima is flat, shaped like a heart, and has roughly ten times as many cattle as people. Hateruma is the southernmost inhabited point in Japan and has a monument saying so, along with some of the clearest night skies anywhere in the country.

Yonaguni, the westernmost, is known for hammerhead shark diving in winter and for an underwater rock formation that some believe is a man-made monument and geologists generally do not. It has its own small horses and a view of Taiwan on exceptionally clear days.

Ferries to the outer islands are weather-dependent and can be cancelled for days at a time, which is the risk of building a tight itinerary out here.

Diving, and the sea

The Yaeyamas have the best diving and snorkelling in Japan by a wide margin. Manta rays gather at Kabira Ishizaki off Ishigaki, most reliably in the warmer months. The coral around Iriomote and the Sekisei lagoon between the islands is the largest reef in the country.

The reef has suffered significant bleaching in recent years, which is worth knowing before you arrive with expectations set by older photographs.

Habu jellyfish are present in summer and beaches with nets mark the safe areas. Take the warnings seriously.

Practicalities

Book flights and accommodation well ahead for summer and for Japanese holiday periods; supply on the small islands is genuinely limited.

Rent a car on Ishigaki. Public transport exists but is thin, and on Iriomote it barely functions.

Typhoon season runs through late summer and early autumn and can close the airport and cancel every ferry, so leave slack at the end of a trip that has a connecting flight to catch.

And take cash. Card acceptance on the smaller islands is patchy and there are few ATMs.

How long it takes

Four nights is the realistic minimum for Ishigaki plus two other islands, and a week is better. Anyone treating this as a two-night add-on to a Tokyo trip will spend most of it travelling.

It is the furthest corner of the country, it takes effort to reach, and it is the part of Japan that least resembles the idea most visitors arrive with.
