Japan's Vending Machines Are a Serious Subject

There are 4 million vending machines in Japan. One for every 31 people. This is not an accident.

The density

Japan has more vending machines per capita than almost anywhere on earth. They appear outside train stations, on mountain trails, inside office buildings, at the end of rural roads where nothing else exists for kilometers. The density is a logistics achievement: the machines are serviced on fixed schedules, calibrated to local demand, and stocked with whatever the neighborhood apparently wants at the hour it apparently wants it.

The figure usually quoted is around four million machines, or roughly one for every thirty-odd people. What makes that number strange is not the count but the placement. A machine standing alone at a trailhead, unlit and unwatched, restocked by someone who drives up once a week, would not survive a month in most countries. Here it is simply part of the furniture of the country.

Why there are so many

Four conditions have to hold at once for unattended retail to work at this scale, and Japan is unusual in having all four.

The first is crime. Vandalism and theft of machines are rare enough that operators treat losses as negligible, so a machine can be sited anywhere there is power rather than only where it can be watched. The second is cash: Japan's ¥100 and ¥500 coins carry enough value that a drink can be bought with one or two of them, which is not true of coinage in most economies.

The third is land. A machine occupies well under a square metre, and in a country where urban land is expensive and awkward gaps between buildings are everywhere, that leftover strip becomes revenue. Property owners take a commission on sales for doing nothing but providing the space and the electricity.

The fourth is labour. A machine is a retail outlet that needs no staff, and in a country with a shrinking working-age population that arithmetic has only got more favourable. The drinks companies figured this out early and built their own machine networks as a distribution channel they control entirely — no supermarket buyer deciding which brands get shelf space, no competitor's product beside theirs.

What is for sale

The most common machines stock canned and bottled drinks — hot canned coffee, cold green tea, sports drinks, canned beer, occasionally alcohol. The hot/cold switching happens seasonally: a red label reading あたたか〜い means the product is heated, a blue つめた〜い means chilled, and the same machine will run both at once in the shoulder months.

Buying a hot Boss Coffee from a vending machine in February is one of those simple pleasures that people consistently describe as an unexpectedly significant memory from Japan. The can comes out at a temperature you can hold through gloves, and on a cold platform it functions as much as a hand warmer as a drink.

The tea deserves a note. Unsweetened green tea, barley tea and oolong are standard stock in every machine, sold cold in summer and hot in winter, and they are the reason nobody in Japan seems to carry a water bottle. If you are used to vending machines that sell only sugar, this is the single biggest practical difference.

How to use one

Insert coins or notes first, or tap your IC card, and the buttons light up for the products you can afford. Press once. The drink drops into the flap at the bottom, and change comes out of the lever or button marked 返却.

A red 売切 lamp under a product means sold out, not out of order. An 準備中 message means the machine is warming or cooling stock and that row will come back shortly.

Notes are accepted by most machines, but the reader is fussy about creased bills and many machines take only ¥1,000 notes — feeding a ¥5,000 or ¥10,000 note usually gets it pushed straight back out. Keep coins for machines in rural areas, where the older units are common.

There is almost always a recycling bin bolted to the side of the machine. It is intended for that machine's containers, and it exists because street bins in Japan largely do not. Drink the thing near the machine and put the can in the bin; walking off with an empty can in a country with no public bins is a mistake you make exactly once.

Specialty machines

Some train stations have instant ramen machines with a surprisingly wide variety. Umbrella machines appear outside train stations when rain is forecast and charge ¥500–800 for a basic transparent umbrella. There are machines selling fresh bananas, hot pizza by the slice, live stag beetles in summer (this is real), and surgical masks in quantities ranging from one to fifty.

A few are worth going out of your way for. The Ponshukan at Echigo-Yuzawa station is a wall of sake vending machines holding around a hundred local breweries — you buy a token, and each token pours a small measure into an ochoko cup, which makes it a tasting room disguised as a vending machine. Dashi machines dispense hot soup stock into a cup for a couple of hundred yen. Canned oden — a whole hot stew of daikon, egg and fishcake in a tin — is a genuine product and not only a novelty, though it is largely sold in Akihabara to people who want to say they have had it.

Frozen-food machines have spread quickly in the last few years, selling gyoza, ramen kits and single portions of curry from restaurants that put them outside their own front doors. It is the most recent example of the pattern: when a Japanese business wants to sell something outside opening hours, the default answer is a machine.

Pricing and payment

Drinks at vending machines are ¥130–200 for most items, approximately the same as a convenience store. The premium is zero or marginal, which is worth stating plainly because in most countries vending is the expensive option. Discount machines exist too, often near residential blocks, selling at ¥100 or below.

Most modern machines accept Suica, ICOCA and the other IC cards. Tap the reader, press the button, the drink drops, the fare is deducted. It is faster than coins and it is the main reason to keep a small balance on the card even if you are not using trains that day.

Alcohol machines are a shrinking category. Where they survive, age verification is nominal — a button confirming you are of age, occasionally a licence reader — and most now shut down overnight under a voluntary industry agreement.

The numbers are falling

The machine count peaked around the turn of the millennium and has been declining since. The convenience stores took the volume: there are now more than fifty thousand of them, open around the clock, selling the same drinks with a lavatory and a cash machine attached.

The other pressure is who owns them. A great many machines belong to small operators or to the elderly owners of the land they stand on, and as those owners retire the machines are not replaced. The decline is steepest in exactly the rural places where a machine is most useful.

It is still an enormous network and it is not going away, but the version of Japan where there is always a machine within sight is slowly becoming the version where there usually is.

The cultural point

Vending machines in Japan are clean, they work — machine failure is rare enough to be noteworthy — and they stock things people actually want. This seems basic until you've been somewhere where vending machines are a coin trap of expired merchandise and broken change returns.

There is a detail that makes the argument better than any of the statistics. A number of machines are designated disaster machines: in an earthquake or a major emergency, the operator can remotely unlock them and they dispense their stock free to whoever is standing there. Some carry digital displays that switch to emergency information.

That is the actual point. Japan's machines are infrastructure rather than retail furniture, and like most Japanese infrastructure they are maintained, taken seriously, and quietly designed for the day something goes wrong.
