Yakushima: Japan's Ancient Forest That Few Tourists Find

A UNESCO-listed island covered in cedar trees that are older than many civilizations, Yakushima sits off the southern tip of Kyushu and sees a fraction of the visitors it deserves. The hike through Shiratani Unsuikyo inspired Princess Mononoke — and it still feels that way.

An island that makes its own weather

Yakushima sits off the southern tip of Kyushu, is roughly circular, and rises to nearly two thousand metres almost immediately from the sea. That geometry produces one of the wettest climates in Japan — there is a local saying about it raining thirty-five days a month — and the rain is the entire point. It is why the island is covered in moss, why the cedars grow the way they do, and why the forest looks like something rendered rather than grown. Expect to be wet. Plan around it rather than against it.

Getting there, which is most of the filter

Access is by ferry or short flight from Kagoshima, and that single transfer is why the island stays quiet. The fast ferry takes a couple of hours, the slower car ferry longer, and flights are small and weather-dependent. Once on the island there is a coastal road, an infrequent bus service, and not much else — most visitors rent a car, and it is the right call. Nothing about Yakushima works well as a day trip. Give it three nights minimum.

Shiratani Unsuikyo, and the Mononoke question

Shiratani Unsuikyo is the accessible one: a network of trails through granite boulders, clear streams and cedar forest, with routes from about an hour to a full day. The upper section, sometimes called the Mononoke forest, is widely described as the inspiration for the landscape in Princess Mononoke — the connection is real in the sense that the film's art team visited, though the film itself is not set here. It doesn't matter. The moss genuinely does cover everything, the light genuinely is green, and photographs consistently fail to convey it.

Jomon Sugi is a full day and a commitment

The island's most famous tree is a yakusugi cedar estimated at anywhere from two to seven thousand years old, depending on which dating method you trust. Reaching it is a long day — commonly around ten hours round trip, much of it walking a disused logging railway line in the dark at the start. There is a bus system to the trailhead that replaces private access during the busy season, and it starts very early. It is a genuine hike rather than a walk, and it is not the best forest on the island. Do it if the tree matters to you; skip it without guilt if it doesn't.

The rest of the island

Yakushima is not only forest. There are hot springs on the shore at Hirauchi that are only accessible at low tide and have no walls. There are sea turtles nesting on the western beaches in early summer. The western coastal road runs through untouched forest where deer and macaques sit in the road and regard your car with contempt. Ohko-no-taki and Senpiro-no-taki are both roadside waterfalls of a scale that would be a national park centrepiece anywhere else.

What to bring, given the rain

Proper waterproofs rather than a folding umbrella, because the trails are steep and you will need both hands. Boots with grip — wet granite and wet cedar roots are the main hazard on the island and account for most injuries. A dry bag for a camera and phone. More socks than seems reasonable. Cash, because the island's shops and guesthouses are not uniformly card-friendly. Almost none of this is exotic; it is just that people arrive with city clothing and regret it within an hour.

The practical rules

Yakushima is a national park and a World Heritage site, and access to the main routes is managed. The Jomon Sugi trailhead is served by a shuttle bus during the busy months, with private vehicles restricted, and there is a conservation charge for the main trails. Overnight walkers are asked to submit a plan, and portable toilet kits are sold and expected on the longer routes because there are no facilities.

The specifics change between seasons and are posted at the ferry terminal and the visitor centre. Check on arrival rather than relying on anything written in advance, including this.

Guides are not compulsory and are genuinely useful on the longer routes, both for the navigation in the dark at the start and because the forest makes much more sense with someone explaining what you are looking at.

What a yakusugi actually is

The word means a cedar on this island over a thousand years old, and the reason they exist is poor soil. The granite bedrock holds few nutrients, so the trees grow extremely slowly, produce dense resinous wood that resists rot, and consequently live for millennia rather than centuries.

It is also why the stumps are everywhere. The island was logged heavily from the Edo period, when the wood was cut for shingles, and many of the most striking shapes on the trails are ancient stumps with new trees growing out of them — a second generation rooted on top of the first.

If a full day to Jomon Sugi is too much, Yakusugi Land is the compromise: a set of looped trails from thirty minutes to two and a half hours through the same forest, reached by road, with several named ancient cedars along the way.

Why it stays empty

Yakushima is a UNESCO World Heritage site, an hour from a major airport, and objectively one of the most extraordinary landscapes in the country — and it receives a small fraction of the visitors that a comparable place in Honshu would. The reasons are mundane: the transfer, the weather, and the fact that it requires a car and a few days rather than a bullet-train afternoon. Those same reasons are why the forest is still quiet. It is the rare case where the inconvenience is doing something useful.
