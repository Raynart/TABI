The JR Pass: Who It's For, Who It Isn't, and How to Not Overpay

The JR Pass is one of Japan's most famous travel products. Since the 2023 price rise it is also, for most itineraries, a waste of money.

The cost, and why old advice is wrong

The 7-day JR Pass costs ¥50,000 (around $330 USD at current rates). For that price you get unlimited travel on most JR-operated trains, including the Shinkansen, for one week. The 14-day version is ¥80,000 and the 21-day is ¥100,000. If your itinerary involves Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and Hiroshima in that sequence, the math works. If your itinerary is mostly in one city with a few day trips, it almost certainly doesn't.

That qualifier matters more than it used to. In October 2023 the 7-day pass went from ¥29,650 to ¥50,000 — a 69% increase overnight. At the old price the pass was close to an automatic purchase for any first-time visitor; at the new one it is a calculation. Almost every blog post, forum answer and guidebook paragraph written before that date is describing a product that no longer exists, and a lot of it is still the first thing you find when you search. Check the date on any JR Pass advice before you trust it, including this page.

There is also a Green Car version, roughly 30–40% more, which buys you the first-class carriage: wider seats in a 2+2 layout instead of 2+3, more legroom, and a quieter car. On a two-hour ride in a full train it is pleasant. It is not a different train and it does not arrive earlier. Buy it if the comfort is worth the money to you, not because it sounds like the serious option.

Break-even: when the pass wins

The break-even calculation for the 7-day pass: a single Tokyo–Kyoto Shinkansen (Hikari) ticket is ¥13,870 each way. Two of those is ¥27,740. Add Kyoto–Osaka (¥1,420), Osaka–Hiroshima (¥5,720 each way), a couple of JR local trips, and you're somewhere near ¥60,000. The pass wins.

Now the itinerary most people actually have. Tokyo for four days, Kyoto for three, back to Tokyo to fly home. That is one round trip: ¥27,740 against a pass price of ¥50,000. You lose ¥22,260 — more than the round trip itself cost. Adding a day trip to Nara or Osaka moves the needle by a couple of thousand yen and changes nothing about the conclusion.

The version that flips it is the long west-bound run. Tokyo → Kyoto → Hiroshima → Osaka → Tokyo inside seven days clears ¥50,000 comfortably, and every JR local ride, airport express and side trip after that point is free. The general rule: the pass wins when you are covering long distances in one direction and coming back, and loses when you are basing yourself in one or two cities. Work out your actual route on a fare calculator before you buy. It takes ten minutes and the answer is frequently the opposite of what you assumed.

The Nozomi supplement changed the calculation

The JR Pass does not cover the Nozomi or Mizuho, the fastest services on the Tokaido–Sanyo line — only the Hikari and Kodama. Tokyo to Kyoto on a Hikari takes about 2h45m versus 2h15m on a Nozomi. This is not a big difference and most people don't notice it.

What did change in 2023: you can now pay a supplement to ride the Nozomi with a JR Pass, rather than being barred from it outright. This is less about the half hour and more about frequency. Nozomi services are the backbone of the timetable; Hikari runs perhaps two an hour, Kodama stops everywhere and takes close to four hours to Kyoto. If you arrive at the platform at an awkward time, the supplement buys you the next departure instead of a forty-minute wait for a train that is itself slower.

The trap is the Kodama. It appears on the same board, it is fully covered, and it is a genuinely slow train that stops at every station on the line. Read the service name, not just the destination.

What the pass covers that people miss

What the pass does cover that people underestimate: the Narita Express (N'EX) from the airport, the local JR lines within Tokyo (which serve some neighborhoods that subway doesn't), the JR Haruka from Kansai Airport into Kyoto, and most Shinkansen within Kyushu (on the Kyushu JR Pass). If you're starting from Narita, the N'EX alone is ¥3,070 to Shinjuku. Over seven days that's two trips or ¥6,140 saved before you board a Shinkansen.

Two more that regularly go unused. The JR ferry to Miyajima — the one that puts you in front of the floating torii — is a JR service and is included. And the Yamanote line, the loop that connects most of the places you will go in Tokyo, is JR. If you are holding an active pass, you do not need to touch your IC card inside Tokyo at all. People carry an activated pass around for a week and pay cash for the Yamanote out of habit.

Regional passes are usually the better buy

This is the part the national pass's fame obscures. JR is six separate regional companies, and each sells its own passes covering its own territory at a fraction of the national price. If your trip stays in one region — which most trips do — one of these is almost always the right answer.

The Kansai Area Pass covers Osaka, Kyoto, Nara, Kobe and Himeji for one to four days and costs a small fraction of the national pass. JR East sells area passes for the Tohoku and Nagano/Niigata directions, useful if you are going north rather than west. The Hokuriku Arch Pass covers Tokyo to Osaka via Kanazawa, which is a genuinely good route and a poor fit for the national pass. JR Kyushu and JR Hokkaido each sell their own.

Two practical notes. These passes are sold to foreign passport holders on a tourist entry, same as the national pass, and several of them can only be bought before you arrive or at specific station offices. And you can stack them across a trip — a regional pass for the week you are moving around and plain IC card travel for the week you are not is usually cheaper than one long national pass covering both.

Reserve your seats — it is free and unlimited

With a JR Pass, seat reservations cost nothing and you can make as many as you like. A surprising number of pass holders never do it, ride in the non-reserved cars, and stand from Tokyo to Nagoya during a busy period.

Reserve at any JR ticket office (Midori no Madoguchi) or a reserved-seat machine, in a few minutes, with the pass and your passport. Do it the evening before rather than on the platform. During Golden Week, Obon in mid-August and New Year, the reserved cars fill days ahead and the non-reserved cars become standing room from the moment the doors open — these are the three weeks of the year when the difference is not comfort but whether you sit down at all.

If you are travelling with luggage, ask for a seat in the last row of the carriage. The space behind it is the only place on a Shinkansen where a large suitcase fits without blocking an aisle.

The luggage rule that catches people

On the Tokaido, Sanyo and Kyushu Shinkansen — the Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka–Hiroshima line, which is to say the line most visitors use — a bag whose combined length, width and height exceeds 160cm requires an oversized baggage seat reservation. That reservation is free if you make it in advance. If you turn up with the bag and no reservation, you pay a ¥1,000 penalty and the staff will find you a place for it.

A large checked-size suitcase is often exactly at this threshold. Measure yours before you travel rather than discovering the rule at the gate, and make the reservation at the same time as your seat — it is the same counter and the same conversation.

The alternative most residents use is to not carry the bag at all. Takkyubin, the overnight luggage courier, moves a suitcase between hotels for roughly ¥2,000–3,000 and it is the single best-value service in Japanese travel. Hand it to your hotel's front desk before noon, collect it at the next hotel the following afternoon, and cross the country with a day bag.

Alternatives to the JR Pass

Alternatives to consider: IC cards (Suica) cover everything within cities. Individual Shinkansen tickets bought in advance can be slightly discounted through JR's Ekinet system. The Osaka Amazing Pass and the Kyoto Sightseeing Card cover local transit within those cities and don't require a JR Pass to function alongside them.

Two more worth knowing. Platt Kodama sells discounted Tokyo–Osaka seats on the slow Kodama service — if you have the time, it is materially cheaper than the standard fare. And the overnight highway bus, at roughly a quarter of the Shinkansen fare, is a real option that Japanese students and budget travellers use constantly; the modern three-across coaches are more comfortable than the idea sounds, and you save a night of accommodation.

Domestic budget airlines (Peach, Jetstar Japan, Skymark) beat the Shinkansen on price for the long hops — Tokyo to Fukuoka or Sapporo — where the train stops being competitive on time as well.

If you have decided to buy one

If you've already decided to buy the pass: buy it before leaving your home country. It used to be only available abroad, and while you can now buy it in Japan, the domestic price is roughly 10–20% higher. Get it from JR's official website or an authorized travel agent in your country. Activate it at a JR office in any major station, not at the airport machines.

Bring your passport to the exchange counter — the pass is sold only to visitors on a temporary visitor entry, and the staff will check the stamp. When you activate it you choose the start date, and it does not have to be the day you collect it. If you are spending your first three days in Tokyo without leaving the city, start the pass on day four. People routinely burn two days of a seven-day pass riding the Yamanote line.

The pass is now issued digitally through the official site, which lets you make seat reservations online instead of queueing. The queues at the exchange counters in Tokyo Station and the airports are long, and they are longest at exactly the hour your flight lands.

The short version

Add up the long-distance legs you will actually take, at the real fares, and compare that to ¥50,000. If you are doing one city with day trips, skip it and use an IC card. If you are staying in one region, buy that region's pass. If you are crossing the country and coming back inside a week, buy the national pass and then use it for everything, including the airport train and the Yamanote line, because you have already paid for it.
