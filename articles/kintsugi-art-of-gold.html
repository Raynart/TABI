Kintsugi: The Art of Repairing Broken Things with Gold

A 500-year-old technique for mending ceramics with lacquer mixed with gold, silver, or platinum has become a global metaphor for resilience. We visited a master craftsman in Kyoto to understand why kintsugi is about far more than broken bowls.

  3. Three recognised styles
  4. Where to see it and where to try it

What it actually is

Kintsugi — golden joinery — repairs broken ceramics with urushi lacquer, then dusts the seams with powdered gold, silver or platinum. The result is a bowl whose fracture lines are the most conspicuous thing about it. The technique is usually dated to the fifteenth or sixteenth century, and the frequently repeated origin story involves a shogun sending a damaged tea bowl to China for repair and being dissatisfied with the metal staples that came back. Whether or not that particular account is reliable, the practice grew up alongside the tea ceremony and its taste for objects with history.

Lacquer is the real material

The gold gets the attention; the lacquer does the work. Urushi is sap from the lacquer tree, and it is both the adhesive and the structure of the repair. It cures through humidity rather than drying, so pieces spend days or weeks in a damp cabinet between stages, and each layer must harden before the next goes on. Raw urushi is also a skin irritant closely related to poison ivy, which is one reason the craft has always been specialist work. A genuine repair takes weeks to months, not an afternoon.

Three recognised styles

Practitioners distinguish between roughly three approaches. Crack kintsugi joins clean breaks with a thin gold seam. Piece-method kintsugi replaces a missing fragment with a solid area of lacquer and gold. Joint-call kintsugi — the most striking — fills a gap with a shard from a completely different vessel, so the finished bowl visibly contains two histories. The last of these makes the philosophy hardest to ignore: the repair is not attempting to return the object to a previous state at all.

Where to see it and where to try it

Kyoto is the centre for both. Museum collections hold repaired tea ware, and a number of workshops in the city and in Tokyo run short courses for visitors — typically half a day, usually using a synthetic substitute for urushi to avoid the curing time and the skin reaction. That substitution is worth understanding before you book: a two-hour class produces something real and satisfying, but it is a demonstration of the aesthetic rather than the traditional technique. Ask which material a workshop uses if it matters to you.

Buying repaired pieces

Antique shops and flea markets — Toji temple market in Kyoto, Oedo in Tokyo — turn up kintsugi pieces regularly, at prices ranging from modest to serious. Two things to check. Real gold seams are matte and slightly uneven under the light rather than uniformly bright, and genuine urushi repairs have a slight softness at the edge of the seam. Also ask whether a piece is food-safe; older repairs and some modern synthetic ones are not, and a bowl for display is a different purchase from a bowl for tea.

The metaphor, and its limits

Kintsugi has become globally popular as a metaphor for resilience: the break is part of the object, the repair is not concealed, the thing is more valuable for having been damaged. That reading is not a Western invention — it is close to what the tea masters were getting at. It is worth adding the part that usually falls off in translation: kintsugi is slow, expensive, requires a specialist, and cannot be done to every break. The metaphor is more honest when it keeps the labour in.
