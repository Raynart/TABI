Bowing: What It Actually Means and What Visitors Should Do

You will be bowed to several hundred times a day and you are not expected to return most of them. The rules are simpler than they look, and getting them slightly wrong is entirely fine.

It is punctuation, not ceremony

Bowing in Japan is closer to a spoken particle than to a ritual. It marks greeting, thanks, apology, request, acknowledgement and departure, and it happens constantly and mostly without thought.

Shop staff bow to customers. Drivers bow from inside the cab. People bow while on the telephone to someone who cannot see them. Train conductors bow to the carriage on entering and leaving it.

Because it is that routine, the anxiety visitors bring to it is misplaced. Nobody is assessing your form.

The three depths, roughly

A slight nod of about fifteen degrees is the everyday one: acknowledgement, thanks for something small, greeting a colleague. This covers the overwhelming majority of situations a visitor is in.

Around thirty degrees is a proper greeting or a sincere thank you — meeting someone, thanking a host, entering a formal situation.

Around forty-five degrees, held slightly longer, is deep respect or serious apology. A visitor will almost never need this, and using it casually looks odd rather than polite.

The form: back straight rather than curved, bend from the waist, hands at the sides for men and folded in front for women, eyes down rather than making eye contact. Do not bow and shake hands simultaneously, which is a common and slightly comic hybrid.

When you do not need to respond

This is the useful part. Service bows are not invitations to reciprocate. Staff greeting you as you enter a shop, the person handing you a tray, the conductor at the door — these are professional courtesies directed at customers in general, and no response is required beyond perhaps a nod.

Returning every one of them is exhausting and marks you out more than not returning them.

The situations that do call for a bow from you: being introduced to someone, thanking a person who has genuinely helped you, apologising, leaving a restaurant or ryokan where you were looked after, and receiving something with both hands.

Bowing at shrines and temples, which is different

Religious bowing follows a set form rather than a social one. At a shrine the sequence at the main hall is two bows, two claps, a pause, then one more bow. At Izumo Taisha the convention is four claps rather than two.

At Buddhist temples there is no clapping. A single bow with palms together is the form.

A shallow bow at the torii on entering and leaving a shrine is customary and is a nice habit to pick up, as is stepping to the side of the path rather than walking down its centre.

Business, briefly

In a business setting the bow is calibrated to relative seniority: the junior person bows slightly lower and rises slightly later. Visitors are not expected to navigate this and will be given latitude.

The exchange of business cards has its own choreography — both hands, card facing the recipient, read it before putting it away, never write on it or put it in a back pocket — and it matters considerably more than the bow accompanying it.

A moderate bow, a card offered properly, and attention to the person in front of you covers everything a visitor needs.

The apology bow, and why it is everywhere

A great deal of Japanese bowing is apology, and the threshold for it is far lower than in most cultures. Squeezing past someone, arriving slightly late, asking a question that takes up time — all of these are routinely marked with sumimasen and a small bow.

Sumimasen does the work of excuse me, sorry and thank you depending on context, and pairing it with a slight bow is the single most useful piece of physical vocabulary a visitor can acquire.

Using it too often is not a mistake. Using it too rarely reads as brusque.

Things that are not bowing but do the same job

A slight nod while saying sumimasen covers most of what a visitor needs in a day. It is the compressed everyday form and it is what people actually use in shops and on trains.

Holding a hand vertically in front of the face, like a small karate chop, while moving through a crowd or past someone is a real and widely used gesture meaning excuse me. It looks odd the first time and is entirely normal.

Waving a hand in front of the face means no, and is the polite refusal — of an offer, a compliment or a mistaken assumption.

None of these are formal etiquette. They are the physical shorthand of ordinary interaction, and picking them up happens by imitation within a few days.

What actually matters

Attentiveness. A bow that is slightly too shallow but clearly directed at the person is read as polite; a technically correct bow performed while looking at a phone is not.

Receiving things with two hands, particularly money, cards and gifts.

And not overthinking it. Japanese people do not expect foreign visitors to bow correctly, are pleased when the attempt is made, and are entirely untroubled when it is not. A nod and a sumimasen will carry you through a month here.
