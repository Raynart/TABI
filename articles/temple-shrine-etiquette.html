How to Visit a Japanese Temple or Shrine Without Being Accidentally Rude

Temples and shrines have different rules, different purposes, and different gods. Getting them confused is the starting mistake. Everything else follows from there.

Temple or shrine, and why it matters

Temples are Buddhist. Shrines are Shinto. The buildings differ, the rituals differ, and the correct behaviour differs slightly at each.

The fastest test: if there is a torii — the distinctive gate, usually vermilion or stone — you are at a shrine. If there is a large incense burner and a hall with Buddhist statuary, you are at a temple. Shrine names often end in -jinja, -jingu or -taisha; temple names in -ji, -dera or -in.

Many famous sites have both on the same grounds, particularly in Kyoto and Nara, because the two coexisted and intermingled for over a thousand years before being forcibly separated by the Meiji government. Where you find both, nobody minds which set of manners you use.

The torii and the approach

Walking through a torii marks the transition from the ordinary world into sacred space. Walk through it rather than around it, and a small bow before passing under is the conventional gesture — you will see people do it without breaking stride.

The centre of the approach path is considered the route of the deity, so many people walk slightly to one side. This is not required of visitors and is the kind of thing worth knowing exists.

At temples, the equivalent is the main gate, often with guardian figures in alcoves on either side. Same principle: pass through it rather than around, and look up at the statues, which are frequently the best thing on the site.

The purification fountain

Near the entrance there is a temizuya — a stone basin with ladles. The sequence: take a ladle in your right hand and pour water over your left; swap hands and pour over your right; pour a little into your left palm, rinse your mouth and spit to the side onto the ground, never into the basin; then tip the ladle upright so the remaining water runs down the handle, and replace it face down.

One ladleful covers all of it, which is the part people get wrong. You are not meant to refill.

Since the pandemic many basins have had their ladles removed and run as a simple flowing spout instead, in which case rinsing your hands is the whole ritual. Most foreign visitors skip this entirely and it is not obligatory, but doing it correctly is a straightforward gesture of respect.

How to pray at a shrine

Approach the main hall. Toss a coin into the offering box — a five-yen coin is traditional because go-en also means a good connection or good fortune. Ring the bell if there is a rope. Bow deeply twice, clap twice, hold the moment, then bow once more.

Two bows, two claps, one bow is the standard sequence at most shrines, though a few famous ones use a different count and will have a sign saying so.

You do not need to remember all of it. Two deep bows and two claps gets you most of the way and nobody will correct you. Coins are thrown gently, not lobbed, and the box is not a wishing well.

How to pray at a temple

Buddhist temples do not involve clapping, and this is the single most common visible error visitors make. Place a coin in the box, put your hands together, bow, hold the moment. That is all of it.

The large incense burner in front of the main hall is for purification: light a stick, place it in the sand, and waft the smoke towards yourself — commonly towards a part of the body that hurts. Buy the incense at the counter rather than taking from someone else's bundle, and light it from the burner rather than from another person's stick.

Many halls require shoes off at the threshold. There will be a step and either a rack or plastic bags. Photography inside is often prohibited and there will usually be a sign.

Omamori, ema and omikuji

Omamori are cloth amulets sold for specific purposes — safe travel, health, study, childbirth, road safety. They are not souvenirs exactly; they are meant to be carried, replaced annually, and returned to a shrine to be burned rather than thrown away. Do not open them to see what is inside.

Ema are the small wooden plaques you see hung in racks by the hundred. Buy one, write your wish on the back, hang it. English is fine. The wish is between you and whatever you have chosen to address.

Omikuji are paper fortunes, drawn at random for a hundred yen or so, ranked from great blessing down to great curse. The convention is to keep a good one and tie a bad one to the rack or the tree provided, leaving the misfortune behind at the shrine rather than carrying it home.

The stamp book

Goshuin are hand-brushed calligraphic stamps, written for you in ink on the spot by a priest or attendant, recording your visit. They are collected in a dedicated concertina book — a goshuincho — sold at most shrines and temples for a couple of thousand yen, and each stamp costs a few hundred.

This is the best souvenir in Japan and very few visitors know about it. Each one is drawn while you wait, and a full book from a two-week trip is an object nobody else has.

The etiquette: use a proper goshuincho rather than a notebook, hand it over open at the page you want, do not talk while the calligrapher is working, and do not treat it as an autograph collection to be rushed around for. Many places will not stamp a book that already contains stamps from the other religion, so some collectors keep two.

General conduct

These are working religious sites, not museums. Services happen, funerals happen, and people are praying beside you. Keep your voice down, do not photograph anyone mid-prayer, and step aside rather than through if someone is at the offering box.

Dress is not formally regulated and nobody will refuse you entry over shorts, but the general standard of dress in Japan is neat and matching it is easy. Remove hats inside halls.

Most sites are free; some charge a few hundred yen for a garden or a treasure hall, which is nearly always worth paying. Opening hours are shorter than you expect — many close by four or five, and the last admission can be half an hour before that.

If you want to go further, some temples run shukubo, temple lodging, where you stay overnight, eat the vegetarian temple cuisine and can join the morning service. Koyasan is the best-known place to do it and it is a genuinely different experience from any hotel.
