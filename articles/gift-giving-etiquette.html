The Rules of Gift Giving in Japan: What to Give, How to Give It, When Not To

Gift giving in Japan is governed by a set of conventions that are invisible until you get them wrong. Here is what they are.

The presentation matters as much as the gift

In Japan the wrapping of a gift is not separate from the gift — it is part of it. A well-chosen item presented unwrapped or in a plastic bag conveys carelessness. A modest item in beautiful wrapping conveys consideration. Department stores wrap purchases without being asked, with a precision that takes real skill.

If you are bringing something from abroad, find a way to present it wrapped. The intent is not to disguise value; it is to show that the presentation was thought about. Any Japanese department store will wrap a gift you bought elsewhere if you ask at the service counter.

For formal occasions there is a layer above wrapping: noshi, a printed paper band placed over the parcel carrying the occasion and the giver's name. Shops sell and apply it, and asking for it signals that the gift is a proper one rather than a casual one.

Numbers and colours to avoid

Avoid sets of four, since shi sounds like the word for death, and nine, since ku sounds like suffering. Three, five and seven are the auspicious counts and are what shops package in anyway.

White wrapping alone, and black-and-white ribbon combinations, belong to funerals and should not appear on a celebratory gift. Red and gold are strongly positive. Red-and-white cord is for happy occasions; black-and-white or silver for condolences, and the two are not interchangeable.

One more that catches people: giving a clock or a knife carries unfortunate connotations, and potted plants are traditionally avoided as a get-well gift because the roots suggest an illness taking hold. None of these will cause genuine offence from a foreign visitor, and all are easy to sidestep.

Gifts are not opened in front of the giver

The convention is to receive a gift graciously, set it aside, and open it privately later. Opening it immediately, examining it, exclaiming over it, asking what it cost — none of that is standard.

If a Japanese host opens your gift on the spot, that is a gesture of warmth towards you specifically. If they set it aside, do not read it as indifference. The gift has been received and will be appreciated; the appreciation is simply expressed later, and often in writing.

The mirror image applies when you are the recipient. Take it with both hands, thank the person, and put it down. You can open it after they leave.

Giving with two hands, and what to say

Gifts, business cards, money envelopes and anything given as a gesture of significance are presented with both hands and a slight bow, and received the same way. Handing something over one-handed while doing something else with the other reads as inattentive in a formal setting.

There is a set phrase that accompanies it and it is worth understanding even if you never use it: 'tsumaranai mono desu ga' — this is a boring, trifling thing, but. It is not false modesty about the object so much as a way of removing any suggestion that the recipient now owes you something. The modern, softer version is 'kokoro bakari desu', just a small token.

Whatever you say, keep it brief and do not talk up the gift. Explaining that it was expensive or hard to find inverts the whole exchange.

The occasions that have rules

Temiyage is the gift you bring when visiting someone's home — food or drink, bought that day, handed over at the door after you have removed your shoes and been shown in, not thrust forward on the doorstep. Never arrive empty-handed.

Ochugen in midsummer and oseibo in December are the formal seasonal gift exchanges between businesses, clients and families. Department stores build entire floors around them for a few weeks a year. As a visitor you will not be expected to participate, but it explains why the gift halls are suddenly enormous in July and December.

Weddings and funerals run on money in specially printed envelopes, with the design, the cord and the amount all governed by convention — and with real rules about which notes to use. If you are ever invited to either, ask a Japanese friend rather than improvising; this is the one area where getting it wrong is genuinely awkward.

Reciprocation is expected

Japanese gift culture is reciprocal by default. A substantial gift creates an expectation of okaeshi, a return gift, conventionally at around half the value of the original. This is why calibration matters so much.

The practical consequence for a visitor: an expensive present to a casual acquaintance is not generous, it is a burden. You have obliged someone to spend money and time working out how to respond. A modest, consumable, clearly-from-abroad gift lands better and closes the loop cleanly.

Consumables are the elegant solution precisely because they are used up. Food, drink and good-quality cosmetics discharge the obligation by being consumed, which is why they dominate the category.

What makes a good gift from abroad

Regional food from where you actually live, something associated with your country that is not a cliché, or anything that reflects genuine thought about the person. Packaged sweets, good tea or coffee, decent whisky, and local specialities all work.

Individually wrapped portions are better than one large item, for the same reason omiyage is: it may be shared around an office. Something with English packaging is a feature rather than a problem — it is visibly from elsewhere.

What to avoid: luxury branded goods, which create the reciprocation problem; anything requiring refrigeration; and anything that needs explaining at length. If the gift needs a paragraph of context to make sense, it is the wrong gift.

When not to give a gift

Do not tip in lieu of a gift, and do not give cash to anyone outside the formal envelope contexts. Do not give a gift to someone in a customer service role unless you have a genuine personal relationship with them — it puts them in an awkward position, and many workplaces do not allow it.

Do not give a gift that clearly exceeds the social context. The gift economy here runs on calibration: the value reflects the relationship, and mismatching in either direction creates discomfort.

And do not press a refused gift. If someone declines twice, the answer is no, and continuing is the one move that turns a small awkwardness into a real one.
