The Perfect Route up Mt. Fuji: What Nobody Tells You

Climbing Fuji is one of those experiences that sounds straightforward until you're doing it at 3am in a headlamp queue. The mountain rewards preparation and punishes assumptions — here's what we learned from five ascents across different seasons.

The season is shorter than you think

Fuji has an official climbing season, roughly early July to early September, and it exists for good reasons. Outside it the mountain huts are shut, the first-aid stations are unstaffed, the trails ice over above the treeline, and buses to the trailheads stop running. Climbing outside the season is not a scenic variation; it is genuine alpinism on a volcano with notoriously bad weather. Within the season, the mountain is busy, well-marked, and manageable for anyone reasonably fit. Plan for the season.

Four routes, and why most people take Yoshida

There are four trails to the summit. Yoshida on the north side is the busiest by a wide margin: most huts, most first-aid, separate ascent and descent paths, and the easiest access from Tokyo. Subashiri is quieter and joins Yoshida near the top. Gotemba is the longest and lowest-starting, with a famous scree run on the descent that is either the best or worst part of the trip depending on your knees. Fujinomiya is the shortest and steepest, and the closest to the true summit. A first ascent should almost certainly be Yoshida.

The overnight split is the whole strategy

The single decision that determines how the climb goes is whether you sleep. The common pattern is to start in the afternoon, climb to a hut somewhere around the seventh or eighth station, sleep a few hours, and continue before dawn. The alternative — climbing straight through the night without stopping, sometimes called a bullet climb — is what produces most of the altitude sickness and most of the miserable summit photographs. Book a hut. They are basic, communal and not cheap, but they are the difference between an experience and an ordeal.

Altitude is the actual difficulty

Fuji is not technical. There is no scrambling that requires skill and no exposure that requires nerve. What it has is a summit approaching 3,776 metres and a trailhead that buses drop you at around 2,300, which is a fast gain for a body that woke up at sea level. Headache and nausea above the eighth station are common. The countermeasures are dull and effective: spend an hour at the fifth station before starting, walk slower than feels natural, drink far more water than you want to, and if symptoms get worse rather than better, go down. Descending is the only reliable cure.

What to actually carry

Layers, because the summit can be near freezing while the trailhead is warm, and wind is the real problem rather than temperature. A headlamp, not a phone torch, because you will be walking in the dark and you need your hands. Cash in small coins for the toilets, which are paid and often the only ones for an hour. Two litres of water minimum, or money to buy more at hut prices, which rise with altitude. Gloves, mainly for the rocky sections near the top. Rain gear regardless of the forecast.

Sunrise, and the queue that comes with it

Goraiko — sunrise seen from the summit — is the reason most people climb, and in the peak weeks the final stretch of the Yoshida trail becomes a slow-moving line of headlamps that can add an hour or more. Two ways around it. Watch the sunrise from the eighth station instead, which is nearly as good and far calmer, then continue afterwards. Or climb on a weekday, ideally in early July or the last week of the season, when the volume drops noticeably. The mountain is the same either way.

The descent nobody prepares for

Going down takes about half the time and does most of the damage. The Yoshida descent is a long series of switchbacks in loose volcanic gravel with no shade and very little to look at, and it is where sore knees and blisters actually happen. Trekking poles help more here than on the way up. So do boots that fit properly with enough room at the toe. Budget three to four hours, keep drinking, and be aware that the last bus from the fifth station leaves earlier than you expect.

The old saying about climbing twice

There is a Japanese proverb to the effect that a wise person climbs Fuji once and a fool climbs it twice. It is affectionate rather than dismissive. Fuji is not a beautiful mountain to be on — it is a bare grey cone with a vending machine every few hundred metres — but it is a remarkable mountain to be on top of, and the view of it from the ground afterwards is permanently different. Go once, do it properly, and enjoy looking at it from a train window for the rest of your life.
