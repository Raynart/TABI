Taking a Taxi in Japan: What to Know Before You Get In

Japanese taxis are expensive, exceptionally clean, and almost entirely driven by people who know exactly where they are going. The main things to know are not about navigation.

The door opens automatically

Japanese taxi doors open and close automatically, controlled by the driver. Do not touch the door. Attempting to open or close it manually will work but is unnecessary and slightly startles the driver. This catches almost every first-time visitor. Stand near the door, wait for it to open, get in. When you arrive, the driver will open it for you. Get out, step away, the door closes.

Only the rear left door is automatic — the one on the kerb side. If you get in on the road side, which you generally should not, you are opening that one yourself.

Show the destination in writing

Most taxi drivers in Japan speak limited English. The reliable approach: have your destination written in Japanese characters, either on your phone (from Google Maps, copy the Japanese name) or on a piece of paper from your hotel. Pronunciation of Western place names in Japanese is often different enough from English pronunciation to cause confusion. A screen showing the map with the destination pinned is universally understood. Your hotel's front desk should always be able to provide you with a card showing the hotel address in Japanese — ask for one at check-in.

The method that works even better: give the driver a phone number. Japanese taxi navigation systems search by telephone number, and every restaurant, hotel and shop has one listed. Reading out ten digits gets you to the door with no ambiguity about which of the four branches with the same name you meant. This is what locals do, and drivers visibly relax when you do it.

Addresses themselves are the weak option. Japanese addresses describe nested blocks rather than positions along a street, and a written address alone can leave a driver hunting the last hundred metres. Name plus phone number, or a dropped map pin, beats an address every time.

Fares and metering

Fares are metered. In Tokyo the base fare is ¥500, which since the April 2026 revision covers the first kilometre rather than the 1.096 km it used to, and every further ¥100 buys about 232 metres. A short hop across a couple of neighbourhoods lands around ¥1,000; a five-kilometre ride across the city is more like ¥2,500. Between 10pm and 5am everything is 20% higher, so that ¥500 flagfall starts at ¥600. Fares are set regionally, so other cities differ. There is no tip. Pay exactly what the meter says.

Two things get added to the meter legitimately. Expressway tolls are passed through at cost, which matters on airport runs and is worth agreeing to before the driver takes the ramp. And the meter also ticks over slowly while stationary, so heavy traffic costs you even when you are not moving.

Fares vary by region. Tokyo is the expensive end; a ride in a smaller city can be noticeably cheaper for the same distance. What does not vary is the honesty of the meter. Deliberate long-hauling of tourists is close to unheard of, and if a driver takes a route that looks odd it is almost always a one-way system or a traffic avoidance, not a scam.

Paying, and the small tray

Credit cards are accepted in most Tokyo taxis now; IC cards (Suica) are accepted in many. Check the payment stickers on the rear side window before you get in if you're unsure. Outside the big cities, assume cash and carry it — a rural taxi that cannot take your card at midnight is a genuinely bad situation.

Money is exchanged on a small tray, not hand to hand. Put your notes in the tray; change and receipt come back the same way. This is standard everywhere in Japan, not a taxi quirk, and it is not rudeness — it is simply how the transaction is done.

There is no tipping. Not a small one, not rounding up, not leaving the coins. Attempting it produces a driver chasing you down the pavement to return your money, which is a worse outcome for everyone than simply taking the change. If you want a receipt, ask for a ryoshusho.

When to use a taxi

Taxis are worth the cost in specific situations: after the last train, with heavy luggage between hotel and station, when traveling with elderly or young children, in cities with infrequent bus service, or when the destination is not near a train line. In Tokyo during normal hours, taxis are significantly more expensive than the train and not faster — traffic density means trains win on time and price for most inner-city routes. Outside Tokyo, in smaller cities, taxis become more valuable as transit alternatives are thinner.

The case that justifies the fare most often is the last train. Tokyo's network stops around midnight and does not resume until roughly 5am, and there is no night service to fall back on. A taxi home at 1am is expensive; the alternative is a capsule hotel, an all-night cafe, or four hours in a bar you have stopped enjoying. Budget for one or two of these rides on a trip rather than treating each as a failure of planning.

The other strong case is rural. In the countryside, buses can run four times a day and stop in the early evening, and a taxi from the station to a remote ryokan is not a luxury but the only way to arrive. Ask the ryokan to book it — many will send one, and some will collect you free.

Hailing and apps

Taxis in Japan are hailed from the street — stand at the curb, extend your arm, and a cab with a lit red indicator will stop. The red character in the windscreen, 空車, means vacant; a green or orange display generally means occupied or on call, which is the opposite of what visitors from most countries expect. Do not try to hail a cab at a zebra crossing or intersection.

At any significant station there is a taxi rank, and the rank is the better option. Queue at the head of it and take the front car; do not walk down the line picking one. In the rain, at a busy station, the rank will still be moving when the street has nothing free on it at all.

Taxi apps: GO (Japan-specific, major cities, English available), Uber (operates in some Japanese cities but uses licensed taxis rather than private drivers), DiDi, and S.RIDE in Tokyo. GO is the most widely used and works in Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, and most other large cities. Having it installed before you need it at midnight is a better plan than finding it at midnight — and note that most of these want a Japanese phone number or a working data connection at sign-up, which is an argument for sorting your eSIM at the airport rather than later.

The etiquette that is not obvious

Sit in the back. The front passenger seat is not off limits but is unusual for a lone visitor, and the rear left seat is the conventional one. Seatbelts are required in the back as well as the front.

Do not eat in the taxi, and keep drinks closed. The interiors are immaculate — white lace seat covers, spotless floor mats, a driver in a suit and often white gloves — and that standard is maintained deliberately. Treat the car the way it is being presented to you.

Large luggage goes in the boot; the driver will handle it and does not expect help or payment for doing so. If you need space, look for a JPN Taxi, the tall boxy hybrid now common in cities. It has a sliding door, a wheelchair ramp, and considerably more room for suitcases than the older sedans.

Where taxis let you down

Airports are the main one. A metered taxi from Narita into central Tokyo runs well over ¥20,000 and can take ninety minutes in traffic; the Narita Express or the Keisei Skyliner does it for a tenth of that. Some companies sell fixed-price airport transfers, which at least remove the uncertainty, but the train is nearly always the right call unless you are arriving at 2am with three suitcases and a toddler.

The second is Kyoto in peak season. The city's traffic during autumn foliage and cherry blossom is genuinely bad, taxis sit in it with the meter running, and a bicycle or a bus lane beats a car by a wide margin. The third is anywhere a Shinkansen goes: at intercity distances the fare becomes absurd long before the journey becomes convenient.
