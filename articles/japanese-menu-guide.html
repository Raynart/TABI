How to Read a Japanese Menu Without Speaking Japanese

Most Japanese restaurants don't have English menus, and that's not a problem once you know what to look for. A handful of kanji, some pattern recognition, and the willingness to point gets you through almost any restaurant in the country.

The three kinds of menu you will meet

Japanese menus fall into roughly three types, and identifying which one you are looking at solves most of the problem. The photographic menu, common in chains and family restaurants, where you can simply point. The printed Japanese-only menu, usually at specialist shops that do one thing. And the handwritten wall menu, which changes daily, is the hardest to read, and almost always signals a place worth eating at.

The wall menu is a good sign, not a warning. It means the kitchen buys what is good that morning rather than printing a fixed list.

Twenty or so characters carry most of the meaning

You do not need to read Japanese. You need to recognise a small set of repeated characters. The proteins: 肉 meat, 牛 beef, 豚 pork, 鶏 chicken, 魚 fish, 海老 shrimp, 卵 egg. The staples: ご飯 rice, 麺 noodles, 蕎麦 soba, うどん udon, パン bread. The methods: 焼 grilled, 揚 fried, 煮 simmered, 蒸 steamed, 生 raw.

A few more do a lot of work at the margins: 大 large and 小 small, 大盛 an extra-large portion, 辛 spicy, 冷 cold and 温 hot, 本日 today's. And 円 is yen, which matters because prices are frequently written in kanji numerals on handwritten menus.

Learn to spot those thirty-odd shapes and a menu stops being opaque and becomes a puzzle with most of the pieces already filled in.

Set meals are the shortcut

Look for 定食 (teishoku) and セット (setto). A teishoku is a set meal — a main dish with rice, miso soup and pickles — and it is almost always the best value on the menu and the most complete meal.

At lunch, ランチ usually indicates a discounted midday version of the same food served at dinner, frequently at a third to a half the price. This is the single largest saving available to a visitor eating well in Japan.

If you can find nothing else on a menu, finding the teishoku section is enough to order properly. 定食 is the most useful pair of characters in this article.

The ticket machine, which is easier than it looks

Many ramen, curry and gyudon shops have a vending machine by the door. You pay first, it prints a ticket, you hand the ticket to the counter and sit down.

The convention is that the top-left button is the shop's flagship — the thing they want you to order and the thing they are best at. If you are unsure, press the top-left button. Machines increasingly have English toggles and most have photographs.

Insert money before pressing anything, since the buttons only light for what you can afford. Take your change from the lever at the bottom, which people forget constantly. The awkwardness is entirely in the first ten seconds.

Plastic food, and reading the shopfront

The replica food in restaurant windows is not a tourist gimmick; it predates mass tourism and exists because it works. It is an accurate guide to portion size and composition in a way photographs often are not. If a case shows what you want, walking in and then pointing at the case is an established move rather than an embarrassment.

The shopfront tells you other things. A queue of local office workers at half past twelve is the strongest possible signal. A noren curtain hanging means open; taken in means closed. 準備中 on a sign means preparing and 営業中 means open for business.

Small specialist shops are usually better than places with long menus. A restaurant that makes one thing has been making it for years.

Six phrases that cover almost everything

Sumimasen — excuse me, and the way you get attention. Kore o kudasai — this one, please, while pointing. Osusume wa nan desu ka — what do you recommend. Onaji mono o — the same again. O-kaikei onegaishimasu — the bill, please. Gochisousama deshita — thank you for the meal, said on the way out.

That is a complete restaurant vocabulary. Pronunciation matters less than you fear, because Japanese vowels are consistent and forgiving.

Two more that save trouble: 'kaado tsukaemasu ka' — can I use a card — asked before you order rather than after, and 'omizu onegaishimasu' for water, though water and tea arrive free and unasked almost everywhere.

Allergies and restrictions need more than pointing

This is the one place where improvisation is a bad idea. Dashi, the stock made from dried bonito, is in a very large proportion of savoury food including things that look entirely vegetarian — miso soup, simmered vegetables, most noodle broths, many dipping sauces. Saying 'no meat' does not communicate this.

If you have a genuine allergy or a strict restriction, carry a written card in Japanese explaining it precisely. Several organisations publish printable versions, and a hotel front desk will write one for you. Show it when you sit down, not after ordering.

Small specialist restaurants often cannot modify a dish at all, and a clear written note lets them tell you honestly rather than guess. Being turned away is a better outcome than being served something you cannot eat, and it is meant kindly.

A few habits that make it easier

Photograph anything you liked, including the sign outside, so you can find it again or show someone. A photograph of a dish is also the most reliable way to order the same thing somewhere else.

Download the Japanese language pack for your translation app before you arrive, so the camera translation works without a connection. It handles printed menus well and handwriting badly, which is worth knowing before you rely on it in front of a wall menu.

And order the thing the shop is named after. A shop with soba in its name makes soba, and the specialisation of Japanese restaurants means the obvious order is nearly always the right one.
