Hiroshima Is More Than the Peace Park — And the Peace Park Is More Than You Think

People visit Hiroshima for one reason. Most leave having found three or four others.

The Peace Memorial Museum

The Peace Memorial Park and Museum is why most people come to Hiroshima, and it should be. The museum documents what happened on August 6, 1945 with a directness and specificity that other memorial museums often avoid — personal objects, shadows burned into stone, recorded testimony. Budget two to three hours, not one.

The main building was rebuilt and rehung in 2019, and the sequence now leads with the human consequences before the historical and political context rather than after it. The effect is deliberate and it works. The objects carry it: a tricycle, a lunchbox with its contents carbonised, a school uniform, each with the name and age of the child it belonged to and a short account of where they were found.

It is a difficult two hours and you should plan the day around that rather than scheduling something cheerful immediately afterwards. Go in the morning while you have the energy for it. There are benches outside along the river, and taking half an hour there before doing anything else is the normal response, not an overreaction.

The rest of the park

The park is not only the museum, and the individual monuments repay knowing about in advance. The Genbaku Dome — the former Industrial Promotion Hall, left in exactly the state the blast left it — is the one image everyone knows. It stands almost directly under the detonation point, which is why any of it survived: the force came near-vertically down onto it rather than sideways.

The Children's Peace Monument commemorates Sadako Sasaki, who developed leukaemia at eleven and folded paper cranes toward a thousand in the belief that it would make her well. The glass cases around the monument hold cranes sent by schools from all over the world, and they are replaced constantly because the volume never stops.

The Cenotaph is worth standing in front of properly. It is a saddle-shaped arch holding the register of every named victim, and it is aligned so that looking through it frames the Flame of Peace and the Genbaku Dome behind it in a single line. The flame has burned since 1964 and is intended to be extinguished when the last nuclear weapon on earth is gone.

The hypocentre itself is not in the park. It is a small marker on an ordinary side street about 300 metres east, outside a clinic, with traffic going past. Finding it is a stranger experience than the park is.

Hiroshima beyond the park

Hiroshima has one of Japan's most interesting covered shotengai (shopping arcade) districts, centered around Hondori and its side streets. It's genuinely local — not a tourist market, but the arcade where people in Hiroshima buy clothes and eat lunch. The okonomiyaki restaurants here are a reason to visit Hiroshima independent of the memorial.

The thing that takes visitors a moment to absorb is that Hiroshima is a completely normal, busy, cheerful Japanese city of over a million people. It is not a memorial with a town attached. Spending the afternoon shopping and eating after spending the morning in the museum is not disrespectful; it is more or less the point the city has spent eighty years making.

Hiroshima okonomiyaki

Hiroshima okonomiyaki is different from Osaka okonomiyaki. The Osaka version mixes everything together; the Hiroshima version layers ingredients — batter, bean sprouts, cabbage, noodles (yes, noodles), pork, egg — in sequence on a flat griddle. The result is denser and more substantial than the Osaka style. Budget ¥1,000–1,400.

The specific place for it is Okonomimura, a three-floor building near the Hondori arcade with around 25 individual shops each making their version. Pick one that looks busy. The shops are nearly identical in menu and differ in the cook, so the crowd is the only signal you have and it is a good one.

Sit at the counter, not a table. The dish is built on the griddle in front of you over about ten minutes, served onto the hot metal directly rather than a plate, and eaten from there with a small metal spatula. Choose soba or udon for the noodle layer; soba is the default. Adding oysters is worth it between November and February, when Hiroshima Bay is producing most of Japan's supply.

Miyajima Island

Miyajima Island: a 10-minute ferry from Miyajimaguchi (covered by the JR Pass, as it is a JR service). The vermilion torii gate of Itsukushima Shrine, standing in the sea at high tide, is one of those images that is on every Japan poster and still manages to be more striking in person than in photographs.

Check the tide table before you go, because the island is effectively two different destinations. At high tide the gate and the shrine stand in water and the shrine's boardwalks appear to float. At low tide the water withdraws entirely and you can walk out across the sand to stand underneath the gate. Both are worth seeing and neither is a substitute for the other — the tide tables are published online and by the ferry operators.

Walk past the shrine to the hills. Daishoin Temple, on the slope above, is a working Shingon temple with a covered stairway lined with metal prayer wheels and a cave hall lit by hundreds of lanterns, and it is largely empty because most day-trippers stop at the shrine. Beyond it, Mt. Misen can be climbed in about two hours or reached by ropeway to a point twenty minutes' walk below the summit. The view from the top takes in the whole Seto Inland Sea.

The island also has deer, which are smaller and less aggressive than Nara's but will absolutely eat your map. There are no crackers sold here and feeding them is discouraged.

The castle, the garden, and the trams

Hiroshima Castle was flattened in 1945 and rebuilt in 1958. The keep is a concrete reconstruction housing a decent museum on the city's history as a castle town, and the grounds are pleasant. What is genuinely affecting is a eucalyptus and a willow inside the grounds that survived the blast and are still growing, marked as such.

Shukkeien is a formal garden dating from 1620, laid out around a central pond with a compressed miniature landscape of bridges, islands and tea houses. It was destroyed and became a refuge for the wounded in 1945; many died in it, and there is a memorial. It has been fully restored and is now simply a beautiful garden, half an hour's slow walk from end to end.

Get around by streetcar. Hiroshima kept its tram network when most Japanese cities scrapped theirs, and it is the easiest way to cross the city at a flat fare. Several cars still in service are pre-war, and a few of those were running on the morning of August 6 and were repaired and put back to work within days. They are marked, and riding one is a curious way to spend fifteen minutes.

How long to stay

Hiroshima is a half-day to full-day stop on the standard Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka route (Shinkansen from Shin-Osaka takes about 85 minutes). A full day allows the museum, Hondori, lunch, and a Miyajima trip, though it will be a compressed day and Miyajima will get the short end of it.

Staying overnight is the better call if you can. It gives Miyajima a proper half day rather than a rushed afternoon, and it gives you the city in the evening, when the riverbanks around the Motoyasu-gawa fill up and the Dome is lit. A night here also breaks up what is otherwise a long stretch of Kansai for most itineraries.

If you have two nights, stay one of them on Miyajima itself. The last ferry takes the day-trippers off around dusk, the island empties, and the shrine at night with nobody on the boardwalk is the version of the place that people who have been twice will tell you about.
