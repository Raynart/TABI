Hakodate: The Port Town at the Bottom of Hokkaido

A morning market selling squid you catch yourself, a hillside of nineteenth-century foreign consulates, and a night view that Japan has been ranking against Hong Kong and Naples for a century.

The first port to open

Hakodate was one of the first Japanese ports opened to foreign trade in the 1850s, after more than two centuries of national seclusion. Consulates, churches and merchant houses went up on the slope above the harbour, and the result is a townscape unlike anywhere else in Hokkaido: Russian Orthodox onion domes, a British consulate, a Chinese hall and Japanese wooden houses within a few streets of each other.

The Motomachi district on that slope is the part to walk. It is steep, the streets run straight down to the water, and the buildings are preserved rather than reconstructed. The old public hall, painted pale blue and yellow, is the landmark.

Hakodate is also the first stop in Hokkaido for anyone arriving by land. The Shinkansen runs under the strait from Aomori, which makes this the natural bridge between Tohoku and the north island rather than a place you fly to separately.

The morning market

The Hakodate morning market runs beside the station from early morning and is the most enjoyable market of its kind in Japan, partly because it is unashamedly a spectacle. Crab, sea urchin, salmon roe and scallops are stacked in tanks and on ice, and stallholders will happily explain what you are looking at.

The signature is the squid fishing: tanks where you catch a live squid with a small rod, which is then prepared for you on the spot and served within a minute or two. It is theatrical and the freshness is not in question.

The other thing to eat here is a kaisendon — a bowl of rice covered with whatever you choose from the counter. Ordering three toppings from the case is the standard move, and the sea urchin in season is the reason people come.

Go early. Most of it winds down well before lunch.

The night view

Mount Hakodate rises at the end of the peninsula, and the town below it is pinched to a narrow waist between two bays. Seen from above at night, the lit streets form a distinctive hourglass shape with black water on both sides.

The ropeway runs to the summit and the view is genuinely spectacular. It is also extremely popular: the queue for the last ropeway down is long, and the observation deck at sunset is crowded. Going up slightly before dark and staying past the initial rush is the way to do it.

In winter the road is closed and the ropeway is the only way up. In summer a bus runs. Clear nights are not guaranteed — sea fog is common — so treat it as an opportunity rather than a fixed item.

Goryokaku

Goryokaku is a star-shaped fort built in the 1850s on a European model, and it was the site of the last battle of the Boshin War, when the short-lived Republic of Ezo made its final stand here in 1869.

The walls and moats survive as a park. The shape is the point, and it is only legible from above — the adjacent tower exists for exactly that reason, and it is one of the few observation towers in Japan that genuinely earns its ticket.

In late April and early May the moat is lined with more than a thousand cherry trees, which flower weeks after the main islands. In winter the outline is picked out in lights.

The food beyond the market

Hakodate shio ramen is the local style: a clear, pale, salt-based broth rather than the miso of Sapporo or the soy of Tokyo. It is delicate in a way that ramen usually is not, and it is the third of Hokkaido's three famous ramen styles.

The town also has a genuine burger claim. A local chain born here serves a Chinese chicken burger that has a following far beyond Hokkaido, which is the sort of thing that sounds like a joke until you join the queue.

And the dairy. Hokkaido milk, soft serve and cheese are as good as advertised, and the soft serve is eaten year-round including in February, which visitors find harder to understand than residents do.

Onuma, and further out

Onuma Quasi-National Park is about half an hour north by train: a lake scattered with small islands, linked by footbridges, under a volcano. It is an easy half day of flat walking or cycling and it is beautiful in autumn.

The Yunokawa hot spring district is within the city, on the coast, and has the unusual feature of a monkey park where macaques bathe in an outdoor hot spring through the winter.

Further afield, the Shimokita and Tsugaru peninsulas are across the water, and the ferry remains a pleasant alternative to the tunnel.

When to go

Summer is mild and the whole of Hokkaido is at its best. Autumn colour arrives early — late September into October — well before the main islands.

Winter is cold, snowy and atmospheric, with the illuminated Goryokaku and steam rising off the harbour. Spring is late: the cherry blossom here is a May event.

Two nights is enough for the town itself. Three if you want Onuma and a slow morning at the market.
