SIM Card vs Pocket Wi-Fi in Japan: What Nobody Tells You Before You Choose

Both work. One is significantly more convenient. The answer has changed in the last three years and most travel articles haven’t caught up.

Why you need internet

Navigation in Japan without internet access is not impossible — the signage in major train stations is multilingual and the numbered exit system on the Tokyo Metro is excellent — but it is significantly harder. Google Maps with Japanese transit data is the most useful single tool available to a visitor, and it needs a live connection to be worth much.

It is not only maps. Restaurant booking, train times, translating a sign, the taxi apps, contacting your accommodation, and increasingly the ticket for whatever you have booked all assume you are online. Sort this before you arrive rather than at the airport.

eSIM is now the default answer

If your phone supports eSIM — most released after 2020 do — a Japanese eSIM is the cleanest solution and the reason most older advice on this question is out of date.

You buy it online before you travel, install the profile over your home wifi, and switch it on when you land. Your original SIM stays active for calls and texts from home while data runs over a Japanese network. No physical swap, no spare device, no counter to queue at, and nothing to return. Providers including Ubigi, Airalo and several Japanese operators sell Japan plans in the region of $15–25 for a week to a month.

Two things to check before you buy. That your phone is carrier-unlocked, which is the usual reason an eSIM fails to activate. And which network the plan actually uses — Japan has three, and coverage in the mountains and on rural rail lines differs between them, though all are fine in cities.

Pocket Wi-Fi: the case for

A pocket Wi-Fi is a small device that creates a personal hotspot on a Japanese network. It connects several devices at once, which is the real argument for it: a family or a group of four sharing one rental is cheaper per head than four eSIMs, and a tablet or a laptop gets online without tethering.

It also requires no changes to your phone at all, which matters if your handset is locked, old, or issued by an employer. Airport rental desks are efficient and the return is a prepaid envelope you drop in a postbox.

Pocket Wi-Fi: the case against

It is one more thing to carry, charge and remember. The battery typically lasts eight to ten hours under real use, which is not a full travel day, so you are also carrying a power bank for it.

If you leave it at the hotel, the whole group has no internet until someone goes back. If you split up for the afternoon, only one of you is online. This is the failure mode that gets people, and it happens on nearly every trip where a couple shares one device.

Data may be throttled after a few gigabytes a day depending on the plan, and the cheapest rentals are the most aggressive about it. And there is a deposit and a return obligation — losing the unit is expensive.

Physical SIM cards

A Japanese tourist SIM replaces your existing SIM and gives you a local data connection. Data-only cards run roughly ¥3,000–5,000 for fifteen to thirty days with five to ten gigabytes; voice-capable cards cost more and most visitors do not need one.

The advantages are the same as an eSIM: nothing extra to carry, your phone's own battery life, always connected. The disadvantages are physical. You have to swap the card, which means storing your original somewhere it will not be lost — the ejector-pin trick of taping it inside your passport cover works. And you may need to set the APN manually, which takes five minutes and a look at the provider's instructions.

This is the right answer for a phone that does not do eSIM, and it is easy to buy on arrival.

What about free wifi and roaming

Free wifi exists and is not a plan. Convenience stores, major stations, most cafés and many trains have it, but the coverage is patchy, several networks require a registration form and an email confirmation, and sessions time out. As a fallback when your data runs out it is fine; as the primary way of navigating a country you do not know, it is not.

Roaming from your home carrier is the zero-effort option and it has got much cheaper. Some plans include Japan at no extra cost, and a daily-rate roaming pass at a few pounds or dollars a day is competitive for a short trip. For a week or less, check what your own carrier charges before buying anything — it may already be sorted.

The one thing not to do is arrive with nothing and decide at the airport. That is how people end up on the most expensive counter rental available at eleven at night.

How much data you actually need

Less than the plans imply. Maps, messaging, translation, searching and email run at well under a gigabyte a day for most travellers — call it 500MB to 1GB. A 10GB plan comfortably covers two weeks of that.

What consumes data is video: streaming on the Shinkansen, uploading photos and clips at full resolution, and video calls home. If you intend to do those, buy a larger allowance or set your phone to upload only on wifi, which is the simpler fix.

Unlimited plans are usually not worth the premium and frequently are not unlimited — read the fair-use clause, which typically throttles after a daily threshold anyway.

Where to get them, and the short answer

eSIMs are bought online before departure and activated on landing. Pocket Wi-Fi rental counters are at Narita, Haneda, Kansai and most other major airports, and booking online in advance is meaningfully cheaper than walking up. Physical SIMs are sold at airport shops and at the big electronics retailers, Bic Camera and Yodobashi, in every city.

The short answer for most people: one or two travellers on a phone from the last five years should buy an eSIM before leaving home. A group of three or more, or anyone with a locked or older handset, should rent a pocket Wi-Fi and book it in advance. Anyone staying under a week should check their home roaming rate first, because it may already be the cheapest option.
