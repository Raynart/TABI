How to Actually Eat Wagyu Beef in Japan

Wagyu is expensive. This is not a misunderstanding. The question is what you're getting for the expense, and whether a ¥5,000 lunch version is the same as a ¥30,000 dinner.

What wagyu actually is

Wagyu refers to four specific Japanese beef cattle breeds — Black, Brown, Shorthorn, and Polled — of which Japanese Black (Kuroge Washu) is the one you'll encounter at restaurants. The defining characteristic is intramuscular fat (shimofuri), distributed through the muscle in a marbling pattern that creates the butter-soft texture wagyu is known for.

The word itself just means Japanese cattle, which is why it is legally meaningless in most countries and precise inside Japan. Domestic beef is tracked individually: every animal has a ten-digit identification number, and in a good restaurant or a department store counter you can look up the animal your meat came from, including where it was raised.

The fat is the whole proposition, and it behaves differently from beef fat elsewhere. It has a lower melting point — low enough that a slice softens against a warm hand — which is why very thin cuts and very short cooking are the norm here, and why a thick wagyu steak cooked like a ribeye is a waste of both the meat and the money.

What the grade actually tells you

The grading has two parts and visitors usually only hear about half of it. The letter — A, B or C — is the yield grade: how much usable meat came off the carcass. It is a number for farmers and it says nothing about how the beef tastes. The digit, 1 to 5, is the quality grade, and it is the one that matters.

That digit is itself an aggregate of four assessments — marbling, colour and brightness, firmness and texture, and the quality of the fat — with the final score being the lowest of the four. Marbling is scored separately on a twelve-point scale, so two pieces of A5 can differ considerably.

The useful thing to know is that A5 is not simply the best. It is the most marbled, and past a certain point marbling stops being a pleasure and becomes a limit on how much you can eat. A4 has noticeably more beef flavour and less fat, and a great many Japanese diners prefer it. Ordering A4 is not settling for less; it is frequently the more enjoyable meal.

Kobe, Matsusaka, and Omi

The three regions you'll encounter most often. Kobe beef (from Tajima-strain cattle in Hyogo Prefecture): the most famous internationally, and genuinely excellent, though supply is far smaller than the global market for 'Kobe beef' implies — only a few thousand animals a year qualify, and most 'Kobe beef' outside Japan is not from Kobe.

Matsusaka beef from Mie Prefecture is considered by many Japanese food professionals to be superior in fat quality; it's less famous internationally because nobody started a marketing campaign for it in the 1990s. Top-grade Matsusaka comes only from virgin female cattle and is raised for considerably longer than usual. Omi beef from Shiga is one of the oldest beef brands in Japan, with a documented history back to the Edo period, when it was sent to the shogun as a medicinal preparation while meat-eating was still officially prohibited.

There are more than two hundred regional brands in total, and outside the famous three they are frequently better value: Yonezawa, Hida, Miyazaki and Kagoshima all produce beef that wins the national judging and costs less because the name is not on posters abroad. If a restaurant offers a local brand you have not heard of, that is usually the intelligent order.

Formats and prices

Yakiniku — grilling your own at a table-top charcoal or gas grill — is the most common restaurant format and the most flexible, at ¥3,000–8,000 for a plate of premium cuts, best shared. You order cut by cut, which lets you compare a fatty cut against a lean one directly. Cook each slice for well under a minute a side; the staff will tell you if you are overdoing it, and they are right.

Sukiyaki simmers thin slices briefly in a sweet soy broth with tofu and vegetables, then dips them in raw beaten egg before eating. It is the winter version and slightly more forgiving for first-timers because the broth and the egg both moderate the richness. Shabu-shabu is the lighter relative, with the slice swished through hot dashi for a few seconds.

Wagyu as a Western-style steak is available in department store restaurants and high-end steakhouses — expect ¥15,000–40,000 per person. Teppanyaki, where a chef cooks it on a flat plate in front of you and portions it into cubes, sits at the same level. Both are genuine experiences and neither is the most efficient way to find out what wagyu tastes like.

The accessible option

Lunch is the single best piece of advice on this page. Restaurants that charge ¥20,000 at dinner routinely serve a wagyu lunch set at a quarter of that, using the same beef and the same kitchen, because the midday trade is a different market. Booking a famous steakhouse for lunch instead of dinner is not a compromise.

Department store food halls (depachika) in Tokyo, Osaka and Kyoto sell wagyu by the slice for home cooking and often have prepared wagyu bento and small yakiniku sets. A wagyu beef bowl (gyudon) at a specialist counter can be had for ¥1,500–3,000 and gives a genuine sense of what A4–A5 tastes like without the restaurant commitment.

Two more cheap entries: wagyu croquettes and menchi-katsu, sold hot for a few hundred yen at butchers and basement counters, and the wagyu skewers and grilled nigiri sold at markets such as Nishiki in Kyoto and Kuromon in Osaka. None of these is the full experience, and all of them are more informative than a photograph.

Ordering without reading the menu

A handful of words covers most yakiniku menus. Karubi is short rib, fatty and the default. Harami is skirt, leaner and beefier, and the cut regulars order most. Rosu is loin. Tan is tongue, usually served thin with lemon and salt, and it is the conventional first order because it is cooked hot and fast before the grill gets greasy.

The convention is to work from lean to fatty and light to strong — tongue, then lean cuts, then the marbled ones — because the order does not reverse well. Order in small rounds rather than all at once, so nothing sits waiting while the grill is full.

Two tools, two jobs: use the tongs for raw meat and your chopsticks only for cooked. The tongs are provided for exactly this reason and mixing them is the one thing that will get you a polite correction.

The one thing worth knowing

Wagyu at A5 is rich. A small amount satisfies in a way that lean beef doesn't. At a proper yakiniku restaurant, a 100g portion is enough for one person. The instinct to order more than you can eat is strong and almost always incorrect. Order one plate, taste it properly, and then decide if you want another.

The corollary is that the expensive all-you-can-eat wagyu deal is a poor bet. Nobody eats 400 grams of A5 with any pleasure, and the format is built on the fact that you will stop long before you have got your money's worth.

Eat it with plain rice and something sharp — pickles, a salad with vinegar dressing, or the raw egg in sukiyaki. Every traditional wagyu format includes a counterweight to the fat, and skipping it is how people end up saying that wagyu was too much for them.
