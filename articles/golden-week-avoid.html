Golden Week: What It Is, Why It Will Destroy Your Trip, and When to Go Instead

Golden Week runs from late April to early May. If your trip overlaps it — or Obon, or New Year — you have two options: embrace the chaos or reschedule.

What Golden Week is

Golden Week is Japan's largest consecutive holiday period — a cluster of four national holidays between April 29 and May 5 that most companies treat as a full week off. Around 20 million Japanese people travel domestically during this window. International visitors add to this. The result is that every popular destination, every Shinkansen, every tourist attraction, and every hotel in Japan is at or near maximum capacity simultaneously.

The four are Showa Day on April 29, Constitution Memorial Day on May 3, Greenery Day on May 4 and Children's Day on May 5. How long the break actually runs depends on where those dates fall in the week: when they line up with a weekend the country gets nine or ten consecutive days off, and when they scatter across a Tuesday and a Thursday many people take the gaps as leave anyway.

Check the calendar for your specific year rather than assuming the dates. A Golden Week that produces a ten-day block is a materially different proposition from one that produces two separate four-day weekends, and hotels price accordingly.

The other two weeks nobody warns you about

Golden Week gets the attention because it has a name in English, but there are three periods of mass domestic travel in Japan and the other two catch visitors just as hard.

Obon, in mid-August, is when people return to their home towns to visit family graves. It is not a run of national holidays — only Mountain Day on August 11 is official — but the entire country moves at once for roughly a week around August 13 to 16. Trains and flights fill, rural accommodation books out, and city businesses close for several days. It is also the hottest and most humid part of the Japanese year, which makes it the worst of the three to be caught in.

New Year is the biggest of all, running roughly December 29 to January 3. This one is different in kind: the problem is not crowds at attractions but closure. Restaurants, small shops, museums and many businesses simply shut, sometimes for a week. Shrines, meanwhile, are extraordinarily busy with hatsumode, the first visit of the year, and the major ones handle millions of people over three days.

If your dates are fixed and they land on New Year, plan around what is open rather than what is crowded — department stores, chain restaurants and convenience stores keep running, and the shrine visit is genuinely worth doing once.

What actually happens

The Tokaido Shinkansen from Tokyo to Osaka sells out weeks in advance and runs extra services that also sell out. Popular ryokan are fully booked four to six months ahead. The queue for Fushimi Inari in Kyoto, normally manageable at 7am, is significant by 8am. Hakone accommodation triples in price. Narita arrivals and departures are maximally busy.

The traffic is the part people underestimate. Expressway congestion during Golden Week is reported in tens of kilometres, and a drive that takes three hours in October takes seven. If any part of your plan involves a rental car or a highway bus, assume it will not run to schedule.

None of this is impossible to navigate; it's just not the Japan trip most people imagine. The photographs that made you want to come were taken on a Tuesday in February.

If you are already booked

Lean into it. Go to places that are interesting specifically when full of Japanese people rather than tourists. City parks during Golden Week — Yoyogi in Tokyo, Maruyama in Kyoto — are excellent: families with picnic setups, food stalls, outdoor performances, a festive atmosphere that you don't get in October. The experience is genuinely different rather than categorically worse.

This is also the best week of the year for festivals, since they are scheduled to coincide with the holiday. Local matsuri run in most towns, and they are the version of Japan that is impossible to see on a quiet weekend in autumn.

The practical adjustments: stay in one city rather than moving between three, book restaurants ahead rather than walking in, start each day at opening time, and accept that the famous sites are a write-off between ten and four. Base yourself somewhere with good local transit and treat long-distance travel as the thing to avoid.

The booking timeline

Work backwards from the constraint that binds first. Ryokan and anything in Hakone, Kyoto or a hot-spring town: six months out, and the good ones go earlier. Domestic flights: as soon as the schedule opens, typically around two months. Shinkansen reserved seats: exactly one month before departure, which is when JR opens sales, and the popular departures go in the first hours.

If you have a JR Pass, reserved seats cost nothing and you can hold as many as you like, so reserve every leg the moment you activate. Non-reserved cars during Golden Week are standing room from Tokyo to Kyoto, and standing for two and a half hours with luggage is a specific kind of misery.

If you do not have a pass, buy through JR's Ekinet system or at any ticket office as far ahead as the rules allow. Same-day travel during these weeks is not a plan.

When to go instead

The weeks immediately after Golden Week ends — roughly May 10 to 25 — are among the best of the year. The weather is mild, the crowds have dispersed, hotel rates drop back to normal, and the new green on the maples is a genuinely underrated sight that gets no attention because everyone is looking at cherry blossom two months earlier.

Late October and early November give autumn colour with manageable crowds, though Kyoto in the third week of November is its own crush. Late September, after the summer humidity breaks and before the foliage crowds arrive, is quiet and pleasant. And January, once New Year is over, has the fewest tourists and the best value on onsen towns of any month.

One more to know about: the September holidays around Respect for the Aged Day and the autumn equinox occasionally align into a long run that the press calls Silver Week. It is nothing like Golden Week in scale, but in the years it lines up, domestic travel spikes for four or five days.
