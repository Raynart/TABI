Izumo and Matsue: The Oldest Shrine and the Quietest Coast

On the far side of the mountains from everything else, a shrine that predates written Japanese history, a castle that was never rebuilt because it never burned, and a garden ranked first in the country for two decades.

The San'in coast

Japan's main island has a busy side and a quiet side. The Sanyo coast on the Pacific carries the Shinkansen, the industry and the population; the San'in coast on the Sea of Japan has none of those things, and Shimane prefecture is among the least visited in the country.

That is the appeal. This is a region of small cities, dark tiled roofs, grey winter seas and an unusually deep well of mythology.

Getting here takes effort — a limited express across the mountains from Okayama, a flight, or a long drive — which is precisely why it stays quiet.

Izumo Taisha

Izumo Taisha is one of the oldest and most important shrines in Japan, appearing in the earliest chronicles and predating reliable dating entirely. It is dedicated to Okuninushi, a deity associated with nation-building and, more usefully for modern visitors, with the forming of relationships.

That last association makes it the enmusubi shrine — the place people come to pray for a good match, romantic or otherwise. It is busy with couples and with people hoping to become couples.

The prayer form is different here: four claps instead of the usual two, on the basis that you are praying for yourself and for the other person. The enormous shimenawa rope hanging at the Kagura hall is one of the largest in Japan and is startling in person.

In the tenth lunar month, when the rest of Japan calls it the month without gods, Izumo calls it the month with gods, because the entire pantheon is held to gather here. The corresponding festival is the major event of the shrine's year.

Matsue and its castle

Matsue is a small city built between a lake and a lagoon, threaded with canals. Its castle is one of only twelve in Japan with an original keep — built in 1611, never destroyed, never reconstructed — and it is a national treasure.

The difference between an original keep and a concrete reconstruction is immediately obvious inside: steep wooden stairs, dark timber, arrow slits, and the smell of old wood. It is a military building rather than a decorative one.

A small boat tour runs the moat and canals around the castle, passing under bridges low enough that the roof of the boat is lowered and everyone ducks. It is the best way to understand how the town is put together.

The Shiomi Nawate street below the castle preserves samurai residences, one of which is open.

Lafcadio Hearn

Matsue was home to Lafcadio Hearn, the Greek-Irish writer who arrived as a schoolteacher in 1890, married into a samurai family, took Japanese citizenship as Koizumi Yakumo, and wrote the books that shaped Western understanding of Japan for a generation — particularly his collections of ghost stories.

His residence and an adjacent memorial museum are preserved near the castle. The house is small and the garden is the thing he wrote about most affectionately.

For anyone who has read Kwaidan, this is a genuine pilgrimage; for anyone who has not, it is a well-presented window on how a foreigner saw Meiji Japan.

The Adachi Museum garden

The Adachi Museum of Art, in Yasugi about half an hour from Matsue, has been ranked the finest garden in Japan by a specialist American journal every year for two decades.

The unusual thing about it is that you cannot walk in it. The garden is composed to be viewed through the windows of the building, framed deliberately as living paintings, with the borrowed scenery of the hills behind incorporated into the composition. Staff maintain it obsessively.

The museum also holds a substantial collection of modern Japanese painting, particularly Yokoyama Taikan, which is what it was built for. The garden has rather overtaken it.

It is a strange and very beautiful place, and it is the single strongest reason to come to this corner of the country.

The rest of it

Matsue's lagoon, Lake Shinji, is famous for its sunsets, which are a local institution with designated viewing spots along the shore.

Iwami Ginzan, further west, is a UNESCO-listed silver mine that at its peak produced a significant share of the world's silver, with a preserved mining town and walkable tunnels.

Tottori, along the coast to the east, has Japan's largest sand dunes — a genuinely surprising landscape of wind-sculpted sand running down to the sea, with camel rides and sandboarding attached, and a sand sculpture museum next to it.

The Oki Islands, offshore, are a UNESCO geopark with sea cliffs and almost no visitors.

Practicalities

The usual approach is the limited express from Okayama, which takes around two and a half hours and is a scenic run across the mountains. There are also flights to Izumo and Yonago from Tokyo.

A local train links Matsue and Izumo, and the Ichibata private line runs closer to the shrine.

Two nights covers Izumo Taisha, Matsue and the Adachi garden without hurrying, which is the right pace for a region whose main quality is that nothing is hurried.
