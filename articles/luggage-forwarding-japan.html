Send Your Suitcase Ahead: How Takkyubin Changes a Japan Trip

Japan has a next-day courier network that will move your suitcase between hotels for the price of a decent lunch. Almost no visitor uses it, and almost every visitor should.

The thing nobody tells you about

Takkyubin — the word is Yamato Transport's brand name and has become the generic term — is a door-to-door courier service that moves parcels and suitcases anywhere in the country, usually overnight. It is used constantly by Japanese travellers and almost never by foreign ones, which is odd, because it solves the single most tiresome part of moving around Japan.

The proposition is simple. Instead of dragging a suitcase onto a Shinkansen, through two station changes and up the stairs at the other end, you hand it over at your hotel's front desk in the morning and collect it at the next hotel the following afternoon. You travel with a day bag.

What it costs and how long it takes

Moving a normal suitcase between two cities costs on the order of a few thousand yen — comparable to a couple of coffees and a sandwich at station prices, and considerably less than most people assume. Size and weight bands determine the exact figure, and the courier's own site has the current table.

Next-day delivery is standard for most of the country. Longer hauls — to or from Hokkaido, Okinawa or remote areas — take an extra day, and this is the detail that catches people out. Confirm the delivery date when you hand the bag over rather than assuming.

You can usually choose a delivery time window, which is worth doing so the bag does not arrive before you can check in.

How to actually do it

The easiest route is your accommodation. Almost every hotel, and most ryokan and guesthouses, will handle this at the front desk: they have the forms, they speak enough of the process to guide you, and the courier collects daily. Ask the evening before if you can, because collection times are often mid-morning.

Convenience stores also accept parcels — the chains have counters for it — as do the courier companies' own depots and the service counters at major airports.

You will fill in a form with the destination address and a phone number. The hotel you are sending to is the address; write the guest name and the check-in date on it, and tell that hotel to expect it. Hotels do this constantly and think nothing of it.

The itineraries it transforms

Hotel to hotel across cities is the obvious one. Tokyo to Kyoto with the bags going separately turns a logistical exercise into a train ride.

Airport to hotel, and hotel to airport, is the one first-time visitors benefit from most. Send the suitcase from the arrivals hall and travel into the city unencumbered; send it back to the departure airport the day before you fly and spend your last day without checking out into a station locker.

A ryokan stay in the middle of a trip. Ryokan rooms are not designed around large suitcases, and an overnight stay needs a change of clothes rather than everything you own. Send the main bag ahead to the next city hotel.

And any leg with a mountain, an island or a lot of stairs in it. Yakushima, the Kiso valley, a night on Koyasan — all much better with a rucksack.

The rules and the limits

Size and weight are capped, and oversized items cost more or need a different service. A standard large suitcase is well within the ordinary bands.

Do not send anything you cannot replace or cannot be without. Passports, medication, valuables and electronics stay with you — not because the service is unreliable, which it emphatically is not, but because the bag is somewhere else for a day.

Perishables need the cold service, which exists and is widely used for food. It is not for a suitcase.

And build in the timing. If the bag has to be somewhere on a specific morning, send it two days ahead rather than one.

Same-day, and the airport counters

There is a same-day service on some city routes — a bag handed over early in the morning can reach a hotel in the same city that evening — but it is limited, more expensive, and not something to build a plan around. Treat next-day as the rule and same-day as a bonus if a counter offers it.

The airport counters are worth knowing about specifically. Both major Tokyo airports and Kansai have courier desks in arrivals that will send a bag to a city hotel, and desks in departures that receive bags sent to the airport. Sending a suitcase to the airport the day before a flight is the move that most improves a last day: you check out with a day bag, spend the day properly, and collect the case after check-in rather than dragging it around.

The reverse — sending from the airport on arrival — is the one that most improves a first day, for the same reason in the opposite direction.

What it does to how you pack

Most Japan packing advice is built around the assumption that you will be carrying everything you own up and down station stairs. Once the courier network is part of the plan, that assumption weakens considerably, and the calculus changes.

What matters instead is the split: a main case that moves independently of you, and a day bag with the things you cannot be without for twenty-four hours. Passport, medication, valuables, chargers, and a change of clothes go in the day bag as a matter of routine, because the case will occasionally be somewhere else overnight by design rather than by accident.

Pack the case so it can be handed over without repacking — that is the only real constraint the system imposes, and it is a mild one.

Coin lockers, for the same problem at a smaller scale

Within a single day the equivalent tool is the coin locker. Every major station has banks of them in several sizes, and the larger ones take a full suitcase. Most now take IC cards as well as coins, which removes the hunt for hundred-yen pieces.

They fill up. At major stations on a weekend the large lockers can all be occupied by mid-morning, and the fallback is the staffed left-luggage counter, which most big stations have and which costs a little more.

The combination — lockers for a day, the courier for a night — covers essentially every luggage problem a trip presents, and between them they remove the argument for travelling light that dominates most Japan packing advice.
