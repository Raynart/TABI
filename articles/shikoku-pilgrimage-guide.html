The 88 Temples: Walking the Shikoku Pilgrimage

Twelve hundred kilometres, eighty-eight temples and roughly six weeks on foot around an entire island. Most people do a fraction of it, and the fraction still works.

What it is

The Shikoku henro is a circular pilgrimage visiting eighty-eight temples associated with Kukai — known posthumously as Kobo Daishi — the monk who founded the Shingon school of Buddhism in the ninth century and who was born on this island.

The full circuit runs roughly twelve hundred kilometres around the coast and through the mountains of Shikoku, and walking it takes something in the region of six weeks. It is one of very few long circular pilgrimage routes anywhere in the world, and unlike a linear route it ends where it began.

It is also a living practice rather than a heritage attraction. People walk it after a bereavement, after retirement, at a turning point, or as devotion. Others drive it, cycle it, or take organised bus tours, all of which are considered entirely legitimate.

You do not have to do all of it

The most useful thing to know is that partial pilgrimage is normal and accepted. Walking a single prefecture's worth of temples, or a week of the route, or even a handful of temples in a day, is a recognised way of doing it. There is no requirement to complete the circuit, and no shame attached to not doing so.

The island's four prefectures are traditionally associated with stages of the journey — awakening, discipline, enlightenment and nirvana — which gives a natural way to divide it. Tokushima, where the route begins, has the first twenty-three temples and the densest, easiest walking.

For a visitor with a week, the opening stretch through Tokushima is the obvious choice: temples close together, flat terrain, good infrastructure, and the sense of a beginning.

The clothing and the equipment

Pilgrims are recognisable by a white cotton vest, a conical sedge hat, and a wooden staff. The white is the colour of death — historically the pilgrimage was dangerous enough that walkers dressed to be buried in — and the staff represents Kobo Daishi himself, who is understood to be walking with you.

That belief has practical consequences. The staff is treated with respect: washed before you at the end of a day, never used to lean on when crossing a bridge, and given the better place in a room.

The other item is the nokyocho, a book stamped and calligraphed at each temple for a small fee. Filled, it becomes the record of the journey and is a genuinely beautiful object.

Full kit is not required and plenty of people walk in ordinary hiking clothes. The vest and hat do identify you as a pilgrim to everyone you pass, which changes how you are treated.

Osettai

Osettai is the custom of giving to pilgrims — food, drink, money, a lift, a bed. It is offered constantly and by all kinds of people, and it is not charity in the usual sense: the giver is understood to be acquiring merit by supporting the pilgrimage, so the gift is made to Kobo Daishi through you.

The correct response is to accept graciously and offer a name slip in return. Refusing is discouraged, because it denies the giver the merit.

For visitors this is often the most affecting part of the whole thing. Being handed a mandarin by an elderly farmer who has walked out to the road specifically to wait for a pilgrim is a form of encounter that ordinary travel does not produce.

Practicalities of walking it

The route is waymarked, though not uniformly — red arrows and small stickers, easy to follow in most places and thin in others. English-language guidebooks and maps exist and are worth carrying, along with an offline map.

Accommodation is a mixture of temple lodgings, small family-run inns used to pilgrims, business hotels in the towns, and free huts along the route. Booking a day ahead is the usual rhythm; some sections have very little and need planning.

The terrain varies from coastal road walking to steep mountain temples that involve serious climbing. Kochi prefecture in particular has long empty stretches.

Spring and autumn are the sensible seasons. Summer is dangerously hot for long road walking and winter brings snow to the mountain temples.

Doing it without walking

Bus tours run the full circuit in around ten days and are used heavily by older Japanese pilgrims. Driving takes about a week and is entirely accepted.

Cycling is increasingly popular and covers the ground in roughly two to three weeks.

None of these are regarded as inferior. The distinction the tradition draws is between doing it and not doing it, not between methods.

What it is actually like

It is not a scenic highlight route. Long sections are along ordinary roads through ordinary towns, past car parks and convenience stores and factories. The temples vary from spectacular mountain complexes to modest suburban compounds.

That is precisely what people who have walked it describe as the point. The pilgrimage is a discipline of continuing rather than a sequence of views, and the ordinariness is the substance of it.

Shikoku is the least visited of Japan's four main islands, and walking any part of this route is a way of seeing a version of the country that itineraries built around Kyoto and Tokyo do not contain at all.
