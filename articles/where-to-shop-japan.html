Where to Actually Shop: Department Stores, 100-Yen Shops and the Rest

Japanese retail is organised by type of shop rather than by price, and knowing which kind of building to walk into saves more money and time than any list of individual stores.

The types, and what each is for

Japanese retail sorts itself into recognisable categories, and each has a predictable range, price level and standard of service. Learning the six or seven types is more useful than any list of specific shops, because every city has all of them.

The depatchika, the electronics chain, the hundred-yen shop, the lifestyle store, the drugstore, the discount store and the shotengai each solve a different problem, and the mistake most visitors make is buying something in the wrong one.

Department stores, and their basements

The depato is the top of the retail hierarchy: multiple floors, immaculate service, staff who bow as the doors open, and a craft floor that is frequently the best curated selection of Japanese-made goods in the city.

The basement, the depachika, is the reason to go. It is a food hall of extraordinary density — prepared dishes, sweets, bento, tea, sake, fruit sold as gifts — and it is where to buy anything intended as a present. The wrapping alone is worth the visit.

Two practical notes. Prices are highest here, and the quality justifies it for gifts rather than for daily shopping. And in the last hour before closing, the prepared food counters discount heavily, which is a genuine and widely used strategy rather than a hardship.

100-yen shops

Daiso, Seria and Can Do sell almost everything at a flat low price, and the quality is startling relative to what an equivalent shop sells elsewhere. This is where Japanese households buy storage, stationery, kitchen tools and cleaning supplies.

For a visitor they are the best value souvenir source in the country: chopsticks, small ceramics, tenugui, stationery, kitchen gadgets, packaging materials. A bag of small gifts from a hundred-yen shop is not an insult here in the way it might be elsewhere.

The larger branches are the ones worth finding, and Seria skews towards better-designed goods than the others.

Lifestyle and variety stores

Loft and Tokyu Hands occupy a category that barely exists outside Japan: several floors of well-designed practical objects, from stationery and travel goods to kitchenware and gadgets nobody knew they wanted.

They are the best place to browse without a specific purpose and the best source of the small clever objects that Japan is known for. Stationery in particular is a floor of its own.

Muji is the minimalist end of the same idea, and it is meaningfully cheaper in Japan than abroad.

Discount stores and electronics

Don Quijote is the chaotic discount chain, open late or around the clock, stocking everything from snacks to cosmetics to luggage in narrow aisles under fluorescent light. It is an experience as much as a shop.

The electronics chains — Yodobashi, Bic Camera — are far more than electronics: they carry watches, toys, cosmetics, alcohol and household goods over many floors. Their point cards give meaningful discounts, and the duty-free counters are efficient.

For anything with a plug, check the voltage and the plug type before buying. Japan runs at a hundred volts, which is lower than most of the world, and appliances bought here may work poorly or not at all elsewhere.

Shotengai, and the shops that are not chains

A shotengai is a covered shopping street, usually near a station, lined with small independent shops: a tofu maker, a fishmonger, a sweet shop, a stationer, a knife sharpener. Many are decades old and some are struggling.

These are the most interesting places to shop and the least convenient. There is little English, opening hours are short, and payment is often cash. What you get is goods made by the person selling them.

Every city has several. Ask where the local one is rather than looking for a famous one.

Markets, second-hand and antiques

Temple and shrine markets — Toji and Kitano Tenmangu in Kyoto, Oedo in Tokyo — run monthly and sell antiques, old textiles, ceramics and general junk. They are the best source of genuinely old objects at reasonable prices.

The second-hand chains are a distinct Japanese phenomenon. Book Off and its siblings sell used books, clothes, instruments and electronics in a condition that would be sold as new elsewhere, because the domestic tolerance for wear is low. Prices are correspondingly good.

Recycle shops and vintage clothing districts, particularly in Koenji and Shimokitazawa in Tokyo, are where the interesting clothing is.

Opening hours, which are not what you expect

Department stores open late by international standards — commonly around ten — and close early, often by eight. Planning a morning of shopping before eleven does not work.

Small independent shops keep shorter and less predictable hours, and many close one fixed day a week. Craft shops and galleries in particular often shut on Mondays or Wednesdays.

Convenience stores, discount chains and some electronics stores run late or around the clock, which inverts the usual pattern: the cheap shops are open when the expensive ones are shut.

New Year is the one period when a great deal simply closes, for several days. Golden Week and Obon are busy rather than closed.

Practical matters

Tax-free purchasing requires your physical passport and a minimum spend at the same store on the same day. The system changes substantially at the start of November 2026, moving from a deduction at the till to a refund claimed at the airport, so check which applies to your dates.

Many shops still prefer cash, particularly the small independent ones and the markets.

Ask for a bag if you want one; they are charged for and no longer given automatically. A furoshiki solves this permanently.

And most shops will gift wrap on request, to a standard that makes the wrapping part of the present.
