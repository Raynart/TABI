Tsukiji Outer Market: Where to Actually Eat (and What to Skip)

The inner market moved to Toyosu in 2018. The outer market never moved and never needed to.

What stayed and what moved

In 2018 the wholesale fish market at Tsukiji relocated to Toyosu, a reclaimed island further east, and travel writers immediately began writing elegies for Tsukiji. What they missed is that the part most visitors cared about — the outer market, several hundred shops selling seafood, street food, knives and tamagoyaki — never moved and shows no sign of moving.

What went to Toyosu was the business end: the auction floor, the wholesalers, the pre-dawn tuna trade. What stayed was the retail and restaurant street that grew up around it over eighty years.

If you want the auction, Toyosu has a viewing deck above the tuna floor, which requires being there before about half past five in the morning and watching through glass. It is interesting and it is not the same as walking through a market. Most people want the outer market, and the outer market is still here.

When to arrive

The outer market opens early. Most stalls are running by six, and the target window is between seven and ten, when the selection is widest and the food is freshest. By noon the best places have sold out of the things worth eating and many are closing.

Come for breakfast. This is the single piece of advice on this page that is not negotiable.

Check the day before you go. The market largely closes on Sundays and public holidays, and many shops also shut on Wednesdays — the schedule follows the old wholesale market's calendar and it is published online. Turning up on a closed day is the most common way to waste the trip.

Sushi first

Sushi from one of the small counters in the side streets off the main lane. Eight seats, a glass case in front, no ceremony. Point, sit, eat immediately. The fish came from Toyosu this morning.

A ¥1,500–3,000 set here will be among the best sushi you have eaten, and the gap between it and a ¥20,000 counter in Ginza is far narrower than the price gap. Some of these places have queues by eight; the ones down the narrower lanes usually do not.

The alternative is kaisendon — a bowl of rice under an arrangement of raw fish, roe and urchin — which is the market's other signature and is easier to eat standing up.

Tamagoyaki, ikura and uni

Tamagoyaki, the thick rolled sweet omelette, is a Tsukiji speciality and several stalls sell slices on wooden skewers for a couple of hundred yen. The outside is slightly caramelised, the inside is custard-soft, and you will want two.

Ikura — salmon roe — on rice, from any stall making it fresh. The roe should be bright and translucent, and the texture is unlike anything from a jar. A small bowl runs ¥500–1,000.

Uni, sea urchin, is the market's luxury item and the place to find out whether you like it, since it is served here fresh enough to taste sweet rather than metallic. Buy one tray from a stall with turnover rather than a cheap one that has been sitting.

Also worth stopping for: grilled scallops and oysters in season, dashi-maki, and the dried goods shops selling katsuobushi and kombu, which pack flat and make far better souvenirs than they sound.

What to skip, and how to behave

Skip the photogenic seafood-on-a-stick vendors near the main entrance that have been optimised for social media. The scallops are fine and the prices are for tourists, and the queue is people taking the same photograph. Walk past, turn into a side street, and find the stalls where nobody has a phone out.

Eating while walking is discouraged here specifically, and several shops have signs asking you not to. Most have a small standing counter or a designated area beside the stall — eat there, then move on.

This is a working market and the narrow lanes carry delivery carts and staff who are in a hurry. Do not stop in the middle of a lane to photograph, do not touch produce, and do not block a shopfront. Most stalls are cash only.

The knife shops

Tsukiji has one of the highest concentrations of professional knife dealers outside the forging towns, and it is a genuinely good place to buy. Prices run from a few thousand yen for a competent home santoku to well over ¥50,000 for a hand-forged single-bevel blade.

Buy in person, after handling several. Ask whether the blade is single or double bevel, what steel it is, and whether they will sharpen it before you leave. Most will engrave a name in Japanese characters at no charge, which takes a few minutes and turns it into a gift.

Pack it in checked luggage, never in hand baggage. This catches somebody at Haneda security every single day.

Getting there, and the alternatives

Subway to Tsukijishijo on the Oedo Line or Tsukiji on the Hibiya Line. Allow two hours, three if the knives are part of the plan.

While you are there, the Namiyoke Shrine at the market's edge has been the fish traders' shrine for three hundred years and has monuments donated by the sushi and egg trades. It takes five minutes and it explains the neighbourhood.

If you are travelling beyond Tokyo, the same morning is available elsewhere: Nishiki in Kyoto for prepared foods and pickles, Kuromon in Osaka for grilled seafood eaten standing, and Omicho in Kanazawa, which is the best of the three and the least visited.
