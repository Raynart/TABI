Hokkaido in July: Japan's Best-Kept Summer Secret

Tokyo in July is 35 degrees and 90% humidity. Hokkaido in July is 22 degrees and clear. These two facts explain a lot.

The climate case

Hokkaido is Japan's northernmost island and its least densely populated. The summer climate is a direct consequence of this geography: while central Honshu is enduring a summer that most temperate-climate visitors find difficult, Hokkaido is experiencing something close to a Northern European July. Average temperatures in Sapporo in July run 22–25°C, with lower humidity. It's possible to walk outside without immediately sweating through your clothes. For visitors arriving from humid climates, this discovery is approximately life-changing.

The other half of the case is the rainy season. Honshu, Kyushu and Shikoku spend roughly six weeks from early June under tsuyu, a near-continuous wet grey that ruins a lot of first trips to Japan. Hokkaido is the one region the front does not reach. June, which is a poor month almost everywhere else in the country, is one of the best months here.

Pack for it properly. Mornings and evenings drop into the low teens, and the high country is colder again — a summer day at Asahidake can sit around 10°C with wind. A light insulating layer and a waterproof shell are not optional, and visitors who packed for a Japanese summer are visibly cold by seven in the evening.

Lavender fields in Furano

The lavender fields of Furano are the most photographed image of Hokkaido summer: long rows of purple lavender against a backdrop of hills and clouds, peaking in early to mid-July. Farm Tomita in Furano is the most famous, and is legitimately beautiful despite being a tourist operation. Come early morning. The crowds arrive by 10am. The lavender at 7am with mist still on the hills is the real version of the photograph you've seen.

The bloom window is narrow — broadly the first three weeks of July, with the peak usually in the middle. Arrive in late June and you get green rows; arrive in August and the harvest has been cut. The farms publish bloom status, and building a trip around lavender without checking it is the most common way to be disappointed by Hokkaido.

Farm Tomita is free to enter and makes its money on lavender ice cream, soft drinks and cosmetics, which is a reasonable trade. If the crowds are too much, the smaller Choei Lavender Farm on the hillside above Nakafurano charges nothing, has a chairlift up through the rows, and is materially quieter for a nearly identical view.

Biei and the Panorama Road

Biei: a small town 20 kilometers north of Furano with rolling farmland — patchwork fields of potato, wheat, sunflower, and canola — that attracts photographers specifically for the quality of the light in summer. Renting a bicycle and spending the morning on the Panorama Road is the correct approach. No specific sight to reach; the road and the fields are the destination.

Take an electric bicycle rather than a standard one. The landscape photographs as gentle and rides as a continuous series of long climbs; rental shops by the station stock e-bikes for a modest premium and it is the difference between a pleasant morning and an ordeal.

There are named trees out here — the Ken and Mary Tree, the Seven Stars Tree, the Philosophy Tree — which became landmarks after appearing in cigarette and car advertisements decades ago. They are single trees in fields. Some visitors find the pilgrimage charming and others find it baffling; both reactions are correct. The Blue Pond a few kilometres away is the genuinely unusual sight: an artificial reservoir whose mineral content turns it an opaque cobalt with dead birch trunks standing in it.

One rule that matters here: the fields are working farms and not scenery. People walk into them for photographs every season, compact the soil, and destroy crops. Stay on the roads and the marked verges.

Sapporo

Sapporo: the capital and the city that functions as the base. The Odori Park beer garden runs all summer (July–August) — rows of tables under the elm trees with brewery stalls from Sapporo, Asahi, Kirin, and Suntory running simultaneously. This is worth a single evening on its own terms.

Soup curry, a Sapporo invention, is served in most restaurants here and is one of those regional dishes that makes no sense until you've eaten it: a thin spiced broth rather than a thick sauce, served with rice on the side and large pieces of vegetable and a chicken leg sitting in it. You choose a spice level and a rice size. It is the city's best cheap meal.

The other two things to eat are the seafood and the ramen. Nijo Market in the centre and the larger Curb Market near Nijujo Station do rice bowls piled with crab, sea urchin and salmon roe from around ¥2,000 — Hokkaido is where most of Japan's supply comes from, and the difference in a bowl eaten here is not subtle. For ramen, the miso version is the local style, and Susukino has the highest concentration of shops worth trying.

Sapporo is also unusually easy to navigate for a Japanese city. It was laid out on a grid in the 1870s with numbered blocks running north–south and east–west from a central point, so an address tells you exactly where a place is — which is not true anywhere else in the country.

Daisetsuzan National Park

The Daisetsuzan National Park: the largest national park in Japan, an alpine region accessible from multiple trailheads. The ropeway at Asahidake gives access to volcanic terrain above 1,500 meters where the hiking is excellent and the views extend across the island. A serious day-hike into the high terrain will not intersect with many other people and looks nothing like the rest of Japan.

The short version from the top of the ropeway is a marked loop of about an hour across a plateau of steaming vents, sulphur, and small alpine ponds. It requires no equipment beyond decent shoes and it is worth the trip on its own. The climb to the summit of Asahidake, Hokkaido's highest peak, is roughly two and a half hours up from the ropeway station and is a real mountain walk on loose volcanic scree.

Treat it as a mountain, not an attraction. Weather here changes fast and visibility can close in within twenty minutes; the ropeway suspends service in high wind, which can leave you walking down. Check the forecast, carry layers and water, and note that this is brown bear country — the population in Daisetsuzan is substantial. Carry a bell, make noise, and read the trailhead notices, which post recent sightings.

Snow lingers at altitude into July, and the alpine flowers come out immediately behind it. Mid-July to mid-August is the window where the high routes are reliably clear.

Getting there and getting around

Sapporo (New Chitose Airport) is 1h40m from Tokyo by air. Domestic flights are cheap — book at least three weeks in advance for ¥8,000–15,000 return. The route is one of the busiest air corridors in the world, which is why the fares stay low.

The Shinkansen does not reach Sapporo. It currently terminates at Shin-Hakodate-Hokuto in the south of the island, and the remaining leg is a further three and a half hours by limited express. Total journey time from Tokyo is eight hours or more. Unless you specifically want the ride, fly.

Once you're there, a rental car is the right choice for Furano and Biei; trains exist but the timing is inconvenient for covering multiple areas in a day. Distances are the thing visitors misjudge — Hokkaido is roughly the size of Austria with a fraction of the rail density, and two hours between places is normal. An international driving permit obtained before you leave home is required, and roads here are wide, well-surfaced and empty by Japanese standards.

If you would rather not drive, the Furano–Biei Norokko sightseeing train runs in summer between Asahikawa and Furano and stops at a temporary station beside the lavender farm, which covers the two headline sights without a car.
