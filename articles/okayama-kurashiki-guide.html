Okayama and Kurashiki: A Great Garden and a Canal District

One of Japan's three great landscape gardens, and half an hour away a merchant quarter of white-walled warehouses along a willow-lined canal that survived the war entirely intact.

Where this is and why you pass through it

Okayama sits on the Sanyo Shinkansen between Osaka and Hiroshima, which means a very large number of visitors travel through it without stopping. It is also the junction for the line across the bridge to Shikoku and the ferries to the art islands, so it is a place many people change trains in.

Stopping for a day is worth it, and stopping for two lets you add Kurashiki, which is the better of the two.

The region is called Setouchi, the Inland Sea, and it has the mildest and driest weather in Japan. Okayama markets itself as the land of sunshine, which by Japanese standards it is.

Korakuen

Korakuen is counted as one of the three great landscape gardens of Japan, with Kenrokuen in Kanazawa and Kairakuen in Mito. It was completed in 1700 for the local lord and it is unusual among Japanese gardens for its openness: broad lawns, wide views, and a scale that lets you see across it rather than around a corner at a time.

There are tea houses, a small rice field and tea plantation kept in working order, streams threaded through the whole thing, and a hill to climb for the overview. The black keep of Okayama Castle sits immediately beyond the river as borrowed scenery.

It rewards a slow visit of an hour or two, and it is at its best early in the morning before the tour groups arrive. Seasonal night illuminations run several times a year.

Okayama Castle, and being honest about it

The castle is called the crow castle for its black boards, and it is a striking object across the water from the garden. The current keep is a post-war reconstruction — the original burned in 1945 — and was renovated in recent years with a modernised interior of displays.

It is worth crossing the bridge for the exterior and the view back at the garden. Whether the inside justifies the ticket depends on how much reconstructed castle interior you have already seen on the trip.

Kurashiki, which is the real reason to come

Kurashiki is fifteen minutes from Okayama by local train, and the Bikan historical quarter is the best-preserved merchant district of its kind in Japan. Warehouses with white plaster walls and black tiled trim line a willow-shaded canal, with arched stone bridges and low wooden shopfronts.

The town was a rice-collection centre in the Edo period, which is what the warehouses were for, and it escaped bombing in the war. What survives is therefore genuine fabric rather than reconstruction, and it has been protected as a preservation district since the 1960s.

It is small enough to walk in an afternoon and pleasant enough to spend a whole day in. Punts run on the canal. Most of the warehouses now hold cafés, craft shops and small museums, which is the usual fate of preserved districts but has been done here with more restraint than most.

The Ohara Museum

The Ohara Museum of Art, in a neoclassical building at the head of the canal, was Japan's first museum of Western art, founded in 1930 by a local textile magnate on the advice of a painter he sponsored.

The collection is remarkable for a provincial city: El Greco, Monet, Gauguin, Matisse, Picasso, alongside a strong holding of Japanese folk craft and modern Japanese painting. The El Greco Annunciation is the piece the museum is known for.

It is the kind of collection that exists because one wealthy person in the 1920s had specific taste and a great deal of money, and it is worth an hour and a half.

Denim, and the other local industry

The Kojima district of Kurashiki is the centre of Japanese denim production, and Japanese selvedge denim has a global following among people who care about such things. Kojima Jeans Street is a row of workshops and brand stores where the jeans are made a short distance from where they are sold.

This grew directly out of the region's cotton and textile history, which is the same industry that funded the Ohara collection.

It is a bus or short train ride from central Kurashiki and is a genuine speciality rather than a manufactured attraction.

Eating around here

Okayama is fruit country, and it is serious about it. The white peaches and the Muscat of Alexandria grapes grown in the prefecture sell at prices that make sense only once you have eaten one, and the fruit parlours in the city build desserts around them.

Barazushi is the local dish: a lacquered box of vinegared rice scattered with an extravagant quantity of seafood and vegetables. It reputedly began as a way of circumventing a feudal edict restricting meals to one soup and one dish, by hiding everything in the rice.

Kurashiki's canal district has a strong cafe culture, and the Inland Sea supplies conger eel, octopus and small oily fish that appear across both towns.

Kibi dango, a small soft rice cake, is the standard souvenir and is tied to the Momotaro legend, which Okayama claims as local.

Fitting it in

Okayama is an easy Shinkansen stop between Osaka and Hiroshima, so the natural itinerary is to break the journey here for a night rather than treat it as a destination in itself.

It is also the mainland end of the Seto-Ohashi bridge to Shikoku and the usual jumping-off point for Naoshima and the other art islands, which makes it a genuinely useful hub for the whole Inland Sea.

One night, with the garden in the morning and Kurashiki in the afternoon, works well. Two if the art islands are next.
