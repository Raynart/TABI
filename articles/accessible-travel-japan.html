Accessible Travel in Japan: What Works Well and What Still Doesn't

Japan's rail network is one of the most accessible in the world, and its older streets and buildings are among the least. Both things are true at once, and planning around the gap is the whole task.

The two Japans

Japan presents an unusually sharp contrast. The rail system, the newer buildings and the public infrastructure built or renovated in recent decades are exceptionally accessible — lifts everywhere, tactile paving on nearly every pavement and platform, audible signals, staff trained and willing.

The older layer is the opposite. Historic temples with gravel approaches and high thresholds, narrow streets without pavements, small restaurants up a flight of stairs, traditional buildings where the whole point is a raised tatami floor. Very little of this can be retrofitted and much of it will not change.

Planning around that contrast — using the excellent infrastructure to reach places, then researching the last hundred metres individually — is what a trip here requires.

Trains are the strong point

Station staff will arrange assistance, and the system for it is well established. Tell the staff at the gate where you are going; they will deploy a ramp to bridge the platform gap, radio ahead, and someone will be waiting with a ramp at the other end. This works across operators and it works reliably.

Give notice where you can. Turning up unannounced is usually fine at large stations, but for Shinkansen travel, for smaller stations and at busy times, contacting the operator in advance makes everything smoother.

Shinkansen trains have designated wheelchair spaces, and the number of them was increased on newer sets. These need to be booked, and the booking is not always possible through the ordinary online channels — the station ticket office is the reliable route.

Lifts exist at the great majority of stations, though at the larger interchanges the accessible route can be considerably longer than the direct one, and the signage for it is not always as prominent as the stairs.

Buses, taxis and the road

City buses are largely low-floor with ramps. The driver deploys the ramp; this is routine.

Taxis are the quiet success story. The newer standard taxi, which has steadily replaced the old sedans, has a high roof, a sliding door and a wheelchair ramp, and it is now the common vehicle rather than a specialist one. Fully accessible vehicles can also be booked in advance through the dispatch apps and by phone.

Pavements are generally good in cities and frequently absent in older residential streets, where pedestrians, cyclists and cars share the road surface. This is manageable but requires attention.

Accommodation needs verifying, not assuming

This is where the most planning is required. Newer hotels and the international chains have accessible rooms built to a recognisable standard. Business hotel chains vary: some have accessible rooms, many have unit bathrooms moulded in one piece with a step into them.

The word 'accessible' is used loosely in listings. Ask specific questions rather than accepting the label: door widths, whether the bathroom has a step, whether there is a roll-in shower or only a bath, bed height, and whether the accessible room is on a floor served by the lift.

Traditional accommodation is a particular case. Ryokan involve floor-level living, futons on tatami and often a step into the bathing area, and the bath itself may have no accessible entry. Some ryokan have adapted rooms and a private bath that can be booked; many cannot adapt at all. This needs asking about directly rather than hoping.

Temples, castles and the historic sites

Major sites have often done what they can: ramps at the main approach, an accessible route around the gravel, a lift in a reconstructed castle keep. Reconstructed castles — those rebuilt in concrete after the war — frequently have lifts. Original wooden keeps almost never do, because the interior is a national treasure with staircases closer to ladders.

Temple grounds are variable and the individual buildings more so. The larger and more visited a site is, the more likely it has been adapted.

The practical approach is to check each site's own access page, which many now publish in detail, rather than relying on a general assumption either way.

Other access needs

For visual impairment, tactile paving is close to universal, station announcements are thorough, and lifts and crossings have audio. Braille appears on handrails, ticket machines and canned drinks.

For hearing impairment, visual information is excellent — train displays, station signage and route information are comprehensive and increasingly multilingual.

For anyone who finds crowds and noise difficult, the rush hour on the major Tokyo and Osaka lines is genuinely overwhelming and entirely avoidable by travelling outside roughly eight to nine-thirty in the morning and six to seven-thirty in the evening.

Assistance dogs are legally permitted in public facilities, though awareness among individual businesses varies and documentation is worth carrying.

Practical preparation

Bring documentation of your equipment and any medication, and check the import rules for medication well ahead — Japan restricts some compounds that are unremarkable elsewhere, and the permission process is not quick.

Rental of mobility equipment is available in the major cities and can be delivered to a hotel, which avoids flying with a heavy chair.

Research the last hundred metres of each destination rather than the journey, because the journey is the part Japan does well.

And ask. The gap between what is formally provided and what staff will do to help is wide in Japan, and it is wide in the helpful direction.
