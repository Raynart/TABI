The Onsen Rules Nobody Bothers to Tell You

The water is shared. The silence is expected. The tattoo policy is more complicated than you've been told.

The rule everyone needs to know

The fundamental rule of a Japanese onsen or public bath is that you wash before you enter the communal water. This is not optional and not a suggestion. There are shower stations around the perimeter — a low stool, a handheld showerhead, a bucket, shampoo and body wash in dispensers.

You sit on the stool, rinse thoroughly, wash properly, and rinse off every trace of soap before you get anywhere near the bath. The water in the tub is for soaking in, not for cleaning in, and it is shared by everyone in the room.

Sitting rather than standing matters, because a standing shower sprays the person on the next stool. Rinse the stool and the space when you finish, which is what the bucket beside you is for.

Naked is the format

There are no swimming costumes in an onsen. Bathing is done naked, in gender-separated rooms, and this is universal rather than a regional variation. A facility that permits swimwear is a water park, not an onsen.

This is the part that stops many first-time visitors, and it is worth saying plainly that it is far less uncomfortable than it sounds. Nobody looks at anybody. The social convention of not looking is absolute, and after about ninety seconds you stop thinking about it entirely.

The small towel you are given is the only concession. It is not for covering yourself in the water — it does not go in the water at all — but people carry it in front of them while walking between the changing room and the bath, and that is what it is doing.

What you bring in

Essentially nothing. The large towel stays in the changing room. The small one comes in, and is folded on your head or set on the rim of the tub while you soak. Soap stays at the shower station.

Your phone does not come into the bathing area at all, and this is enforced seriously. Photography here is a privacy violation of every other person present, and it is one of the few rules in Japan that will get you removed rather than politely corrected.

Long hair goes up. It must not touch the water. Hair ties are usually available at the changing-room counter if you have forgotten one.

Tattoos, briefly

The official policy at most traditional onsen is no tattoos, a rule that dates to historical associations with organised crime. Enforcement varies enormously: large resort facilities tend to be stricter, small neighbourhood sento are often entirely pragmatic.

Private family baths, bookable by the hour at many ryokan and onsen hotels, have no policy because there are no other guests. Tattoo-friendly facilities exist and increase in number every year.

The workable approach is to ask before you book rather than discover the policy at the door. This is a large enough subject to have its own guide, and if you have tattoos it is worth reading before you plan an onsen trip.

Temperature, and how long

Onsen run hot. 40–42°C is standard and 44°C exists. The first immersion will feel excessive; give yourself thirty seconds to acclimatise before concluding you cannot manage it. Enter slowly, sit still, and breathe steadily, and you will be fine within a minute.

Ten to fifteen minutes at a time is plenty. If you feel dizzy, lightheaded or your heart is racing, get out and sit down — that is the heat, not a weakness, and fainting in a bathroom full of hot water and hard surfaces is the actual risk here.

Drink water before and after. Do not go in drunk, do not go in immediately after a large meal, and if you are pregnant or have heart or blood-pressure conditions, check before you soak — most facilities post the standard warnings at the entrance.

Working the room

Most onsen have several baths: an indoor main pool, an outdoor rotenburo, sometimes a cold plunge, a sauna, a carbonated or mineral bath. The protocol is to move between them over thirty to sixty minutes rather than sitting in one until you have cooked.

The cold plunge after a hot soak is optional and converts most people the first time they try it. The contrast is the point, and the sensation afterwards is out of proportion to the effort involved.

The rotenburo is the one to hold until last if the weather is cold. Sitting outdoors in hot water with snow coming down is the single best thing about winter in Japan, and it is worth planning an evening around.

The room is quiet

Conversation happens, quietly, between people who came together. What does not happen is loud talking, laughing across the room, splashing, swimming, or anything that carries. The acoustics in a tiled room are unforgiving and the expectation is close to library volume.

Children are present and are held to a looser standard, as everywhere. Groups of visitors talking at normal outdoor volume are the most common complaint that onsen staff have about foreign guests, and it is an easy one to avoid.

Do not put your head under. Do not wring the towel out into the bath. Do not shave at the bath edge.

Onsen, sento, and what the water is

An onsen is a bath fed by natural geothermal spring water, legally defined by mineral content and temperature at source. A sento is a public bathhouse using ordinary heated water — cheaper, urban, often architecturally wonderful, and a genuine piece of neighbourhood life. A super sento is the large suburban leisure version with several baths, a restaurant and massage chairs.

The water itself varies more than visitors expect. Sulphur springs smell of eggs and are milky; iron-rich springs run brown and stain towels; some are strongly alkaline and leave your skin slippery. The composition is posted on a board at the entrance, usually with a list of the ailments it is traditionally held to help.

Whether to rinse off afterwards depends on the water. For strongly mineral springs the local advice is generally not to, so the minerals stay on the skin, unless the water is acidic enough to irritate. If in doubt, do what the people around you do.

After the bath

Dry off in the washing area before you walk back into the changing room, using the small towel, so you are not dripping across the floor other people are standing on. The large towel and your clothes are waiting there.

The post-onsen state — warm, slightly light-headed, skin oddly soft — is best enjoyed horizontally for a quarter of an hour. Drink something cold. Most ryokan leave water or barley tea in the changing room for exactly this, and many sento sell bottled milk from a fridge, which is a tradition with no justification beyond being correct.

An evening onsen followed by eight hours of sleep in a cool room is, empirically, the best sleep you will have all year.
