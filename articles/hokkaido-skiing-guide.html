Skiing in Hokkaido: Why the Snow Is Actually Different

Siberian air crossing a warm sea produces snow so dry it does not pack. That single meteorological accident is why skiers fly past the Alps to get to an island north of Japan.

The meteorology, briefly

Cold, dry air sweeps off the Siberian landmass in winter and crosses the Sea of Japan, which is comparatively warm. It picks up moisture on the way, hits the mountains of Hokkaido, and drops it as snow that is exceptionally light and dry — low in water content, and consequently unable to pack down.

This happens with great regularity from December through February. Hokkaido resorts routinely record snowfall totals that put them among the snowiest inhabited places on earth, and the quality of it is what the reputation is built on rather than the quantity alone.

The practical effect is that fresh snow stays skiable for longer, the runs stay soft, and the powder days that are rare elsewhere are close to routine here. It is also why the terrain does not need to be dramatic to be worth travelling for: the snow is doing the work.

Niseko, and what it has become

Niseko is the internationally famous one and is effectively four connected resort areas on one mountain, Niseko Annupuri, with Mount Yotei — a near-perfect volcanic cone — filling the view opposite.

It has been transformed by two decades of foreign investment, initially Australian and later broader. English is spoken everywhere, the restaurants are international, the accommodation runs to genuinely luxurious, and prices are far above the Japanese norm. Some skiers find this a relief and others find it defeats the purpose.

What is not in dispute is the terrain and the snow, along with a well-organised night skiing operation and controlled gate access to the backcountry that is more permissive than most Japanese resorts.

The alternatives, which are cheaper and quieter

Rusutsu is close to Niseko, has three mountains, excellent tree skiing and a fraction of the crowds. It is the most common recommendation from people who have skied both.

Furano, in the centre of the island, is a proper Japanese ski town with a long season, reliable snow and a working town attached rather than a resort village. Kiroro, near Sapporo, gets some of the heaviest snowfall on the island.

Sapporo Teine and Bankei are within reach of the city itself, which makes a trip that mixes skiing with city food and nightlife entirely practical — an option Niseko does not offer.

Tomamu, Kamui and a long tail of smaller local hills round it out. Several of the smaller places have the odd combination of superb snow and almost no one on it.

Tree skiing, and the rules about it

The birch and silver fir forests, spaced widely enough to ski between, are the other half of the appeal. Powder that does not pack, in gladed trees, is the specific thing people come for.

Japanese resorts have historically been restrictive about off-piste, and the rules vary considerably between them. Some have designated tree runs and marked gates; others prohibit it outright and will confiscate a pass. Niseko's gate system is well established and clearly signed.

Take the local rules seriously rather than assuming a Western resort's norms. The avalanche risk is real, tree wells are a genuine hazard in snow this deep, and the resorts that allow access do so on the basis that people respect the closures.

What a Japanese ski day actually looks like

The lifts are older and slower than at a comparable European or North American resort, and nobody minds, because the queues are short and the snow keeps falling while you ride.

Lunch is in a cafeteria on the mountain and is proper food at low prices: curry rice, ramen, katsu. There is no equivalent of the Alpine mountain restaurant with a wine list, and the money saved is substantial.

The mountains close earlier than visitors expect, often around four, though several resorts run floodlit night skiing which is a genuine feature rather than a novelty and is at its best when it is snowing.

Rental equipment is widely available, well maintained and cheap, and increasingly includes powder-specific skis, which matters here more than it would elsewhere.

Getting there and getting around

New Chitose Airport serves Sapporo and has direct international flights as well as frequent connections from Tokyo. Transfers from the airport to Niseko take around three hours by bus.

The Shinkansen reaches Hakodate and is being extended towards Sapporo, but for now flying is the sensible approach for a ski trip.

A car gives flexibility to chase conditions between resorts, but winter driving here is a serious proposition and requires proper tyres and experience of snow. Resort buses cover the main routes and most visitors do not need to drive.

Season, cost and the onsen at the end

The season runs roughly December to early April, with January and February the reliable heart of it. Late December and the New Year period are expensive and busy with domestic visitors; February half-term brings the international crowd.

Costs vary enormously by resort. Niseko is priced internationally; Furano and the smaller hills are priced for Japan, and lift passes at the latter can be a fraction of a European equivalent.

And the thing that makes a Japanese ski trip different from any other: the onsen. Almost every resort has hot spring bathing, frequently outdoors, and finishing a day in deep snow by sitting in volcanic water while it snows on your head is the argument that converts people permanently.
