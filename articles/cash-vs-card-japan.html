Japan Still Runs on Cash — Here’s How Much to Bring and Where to Get It

Cards are increasingly accepted in Japan, but ‘increasingly’ is not the same as ‘reliably.’ Plan for cash and treat the card acceptance as a bonus.

Why Japan still uses cash

Japan's cash culture is not technological backwardness. It reflects a combination of historical distrust of consumer credit, very low crime rates that make carrying cash safe, a retail infrastructure built around cash transactions, and a cultural preference for exact, visible payment. The ¥100, ¥500 and ¥1,000 denominations handle most daily transactions efficiently.

The safety point is not a throwaway. Losing a wallet in Tokyo has a genuinely high probability of it being handed in to a police box with the money still in it, and that fact underwrites the whole system. In a country where cash is not at meaningful risk, the case for cards is weaker than it is elsewhere.

There is also a merchant reason. Card processing fees are a real cost for a six-seat restaurant with thin margins, and plenty of small businesses have concluded that cash is simply cheaper to accept.

Is it changing

Yes, slowly. Card acceptance has expanded significantly in recent years — the convenience store chains, department stores, chain restaurants and supermarkets almost universally take Visa and Mastercard now, and contactless is normal in cities.

But neighbourhood restaurants, small temple admission counters, some bus routes, onsen entrance fees, market stalls and many rural businesses remain cash-only. You will encounter cash-only situations, and whether to carry cash is not really a decision.

Two acceptance details worth knowing. American Express and Diners are accepted far less widely than Visa and Mastercard, so do not travel with only an Amex. And QR payment apps — PayPay and its competitors, which is what you will see locals using — generally require a Japanese bank account or phone number and are not a practical option for a visitor.

How much to bring

A reasonable daily cash budget for someone eating well and visiting paid attractions is ¥5,000–8,000 per day. For a one-week trip to Tokyo and Kyoto, ¥50,000–60,000 in cash is a workable target, supplemented by card use where available.

Carry ¥10,000–20,000 on you at any given time — enough for a day with a buffer. More than ¥30,000 in a wallet is unnecessary given how dense the ATM network is; you are never far from a convenience store.

Bring a small amount of yen from home for the first few hours if it is convenient, but do not exchange large sums before you travel. Home-country exchange rates are almost always worse than withdrawing from a Japanese ATM, and the airport exchange counters in Japan are worse still.

The convenience store ATM

The most reliable cash source for foreign visitors is the ATM inside a 7-Eleven. These accept almost all international cards, have English and several other language menus, charge a flat fee of a couple of hundred yen, and run around the clock. Japan Post ATMs in post offices and AEON bank ATMs are the other dependable options.

Traditional Japanese bank ATMs — Mizuho, MUFG, SMBC — frequently do not accept foreign cards at all, or accept them only during banking hours, which defeats the purpose. Do not waste time on them.

Two practical points. Withdraw larger amounts less often, because the fee is flat and your home bank probably adds its own. And tell your bank you are travelling before you go; a Japanese ATM withdrawal is a classic fraud trigger and a blocked card at 9pm in a country where you are relying on cash is a genuinely bad evening.

The money itself

Notes come in ¥1,000, ¥5,000 and ¥10,000. A new series was issued in 2024 — different portraits, different security features — and both old and new circulate together as legal tender. Occasionally an older vending machine or ticket machine will reject the new notes; if that happens, it is the machine, not the money, and a convenience store will break it for you.

Coins run ¥1, ¥5, ¥10, ¥50, ¥100 and ¥500. The ¥500 is a substantial coin worth about three or four dollars, which takes some adjustment for visitors used to treating coins as small change. The ¥5 and ¥50 are the ones with holes in the middle.

You will accumulate coins at a startling rate, because cash payment plus consumption tax produces awkward totals constantly. The fix is to load them onto your IC card at any station machine, which accepts coins and turns them into train fare and convenience store purchases.

Where cards work and where they do not

Cards work reliably at hotel check-in, department stores and shopping centres, chain restaurants, supermarkets, convenience stores, pharmacy chains, Shinkansen ticket machines and major-city attractions.

Cards work unreliably or not at all at local izakaya, neighbourhood restaurants, temples and shrines, market stalls, some local buses, small guesthouses, coin lockers of the older type, and anything describable as family-run. Festival food stalls are universally cash. So are most admission boxes at small temples, which frequently amount to a wooden slot and an honesty system.

The reliable test is to look before you order. If there are no payment logos on the door, the window or the till, assume cash — and if you are unsure, kaado, tsukaemasu ka settles it in three words before you have committed to a meal.

The IC card exception

The IC card — Suica, ICOCA and their regional equivalents — sits between cash and card and is worth treating separately. It is a prepaid tap card accepted on trains and buses nationwide, at convenience stores, at vending machines, in many shops and at a lot of coin lockers.

Load it with cash at any station machine and keep a couple of thousand yen on it. The combination of an IC card and a modest cash reserve handles the overwhelming majority of daily transactions without friction, and it removes the fumbling for coins that slows everything down.

Recent iPhones and many Android phones can hold a Suica in the wallet app and top it up from an overseas card, which sidesteps the physical card entirely. If that works on your handset it is the single best small preparation you can make before arriving.

Etiquette, and one thing not to do

Money is passed on the small tray by the till, not hand to hand. Put your notes in the tray; change and receipt come back the same way. It is universal and it is not a formality you can skip without the cashier being briefly stuck.

There is no tipping anywhere in Japan — not in restaurants, taxis, hotels or bars. Leaving money on a table will result in someone chasing you down the street to return it. If you want to express appreciation, say gochisousama deshita on the way out; it lands better than money would.

Finally, count your change and take your receipt. Not because anyone is trying anything — the accuracy here is absolute — but because the receipt is what you need for tax-free processing, and the tax-free counter will not help you without it.
